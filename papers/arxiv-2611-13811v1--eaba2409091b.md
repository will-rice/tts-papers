Abstract Antalia-2 Mini is a single-voice Turkish text-to-speech model
with 7,620,931 parameters. It reads characters and produces 48 kHz audio
at 41 times real time on an Apple M5 laptop CPU (2 threads). Its
training audio came only from VoxCPM2 (the teacher), about 300 times
larger, cloning one synthetic clip; automatic checks kept about 1,790 h.
Fine-tuning the vocoder on the acoustic model’s own mel spectrograms cut
the word error rate (WER) of Whisper’s transcripts from a mean of 3.89%
to 1.75% at adjacent pre-release checkpoints. The teacher corrupts the
start of a clip when the first word begins with ö, the student learned
this, and a spoken prefix we cut from the output hides it. On the
495-sentence Freya-TR-Eval benchmark, where we tuned settings, version
1.0.1 has WER 1.09% and character error rate 0.22% on our public
harness. A paired bootstrap cannot distinguish these from EMA Lightning,
an 8.6M Turkish model, which scores higher on full-band UTMOS, an
English-trained quality proxy. No human listening test exists. Weights,
inference code and the benchmark harness are public under Apache-2.0;
training code and data are not.

## 1  Introduction

Antalia-2 Mini has 7.62M parameters, reads characters and learned only
from the audio of a model about 300 times larger. On one benchmark a
paired bootstrap cannot distinguish its word and character error rates
from that teacher’s or EMA Lightning’s, and no human listening test of
it exists yet.

A text-to-speech (TTS) model small enough for a CPU can run on the
machine that needs the speech, with no GPU server and no text leaving
the device. Among the Turkish systems we ran, two small open-weights
models, Piper (voice dfki) at 16M parameters and MMS-TTS at 36M, have
word error rates (WER) of 3.41% and 6.38%, against 1.16% for
Trendyol-TTS at about 2.38B. We get a WER by having the Whisper speech
recognizer (Radford et al., 2023) transcribe a model’s audio and
comparing the transcript with the input text, here on the public harness
of the Freya-TR-Eval benchmark. Antalia-2 Mini (Mini below) aims for the
low error rates of large systems at 7.62M, in one voice.

Its default weights are 30.5 MB at fp32, with an optional 15.3 MB fp16
download (Table 7). A browser page and a signed Android engine also run
it on the device, but no real phone has been measured.

Mini reads characters, 47 symbols in all, with no phonemizer to turn
spelling into sound symbols, and learns how letters sound from its
training audio. Turkish spelling suits this choice. Öney and Durgunoğlu
(1997) describe it as phonologically transparent, with regular
letter-sound correspondences, and Babayiğit (2022) names Ğ as the
exception. A text normalizer first turns numbers, dates, times, amounts,
initialisms, web addresses and English or brand words into spoken form
(Table 2), and its known defects carry into the speech.

All of Mini’s training audio came from one model, VoxCPM2 (Zhou et al.,
2026), roughly 300 to 313 times its size. We had this teacher read text
of our choosing in one synthetic voice, so every clip came with the text
it was asked to read. Mini, the student, trains on those clips with no
access to the teacher’s internal features. Section 9 traces this idea
back at least to TTS-by-TTS (Hwang et al., 2021). Teacher errors become
training targets, as Pipatanakul et al. (2026) state, and Section 6
documents one that Mini learned.

Among the other systems we ran, EMA Lightning (Aslan, 2026) is nearest
ours in size and recipe. Its card says it was also trained only on
synthetic speech from VoxCPM2, about 1,000 h, so the two models share a
teacher model, with different voices. Our scoring protocol follows that
card. The vocoder is the network that turns a mel spectrogram into
audio, and the acoustic model before it turns text into that mel. Mini
has 10.93% fewer parameters, 7,620,931 against 8,556,172, with the
vocoder in both counts.

We report six results, and Figure 2 traces the recipe behind them. The
terms box defines the words they use.

## 2  The model

An acoustic model reads characters, its generator draws a mel
spectrogram in 8 steps, and a vocoder turns the mel into 48 kHz audio in
one pass. At the default settings the generator, trained with shortcut
self-consistency, does 84.0% of the multiply-accumulates, and the model
also renders hidden text that is cut from the output.

for the start and end of a sequence, the space, 8 punctuation marks, 32
letters (29 Turkish plus q, w and x) and 3 circumflex vowels. It has no
phonemes and no digits, so a normalizer inside the package first writes
everything else in these symbols (Table 2). Our checklist records that
no native speaker has reviewed address readings such as dabılyu and
bölü.

### Audio features.

A mel frame covers 2,048 samples of 48 kHz audio. Frames start 512
samples apart (10.667 ms), and the bands follow the Slaney mel scale
from 0 to 24 kHz. We normalize as
$`y=(\ln\max(\mathrm{mel},10^{-5})+5.9)/2.6`$. The generator stacks
frames in pairs, so a token lasts 21.333 ms and holds 256 values.

### Encoder, alignment, durations.

The text encoder is an embedding followed by 3 ConvNeXt blocks (Liu
et al., 2022), a kind of convolutional residual block, and one
self-attention block with rotary position embeddings, or RoPE (Su
et al., 2024).

Nothing in a training clip says which frames belong to which character.
Monotonic alignment search, MAS (Kim et al., 2020), finds this. A
training-only layer, the prior, maps each encoder output to an expected
mel frame, and a dynamic program picks the most likely left-to-right
assignment, with at least one frame per symbol. A small convolutional
duration predictor learns the resulting lengths, with gradients stopped
at the encoder output, and replaces MAS at synthesis.

### Flow matching.

The generator follows a learned velocity from noise at $`t=0`$ to a mel
at $`t=1`$. Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) trains that velocity by
regression. We mix a target mel $`x_{1}`$ with Gaussian noise $`x_{0}`$,
and the network, given the text condition $`c`$ and a step size of 0 (an
input the next paragraph explains), predicts the velocity of the
straight line between them:

```math
x_{t}=(1-t)\,x_{0}+t\,x_{1},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}=\bigl\|v_{\theta}(x_{t},t,0\mid c)-(x_{1}-x_{0})\bigr\|^{2}. \tag{1}
```

### Shortcut self-consistency.

The learned velocity averages over many such lines, so its paths curve.
Small steps follow them but cost many network passes, and a large step
leaves them. Shortcut self-consistency (Frans et al., 2025) gives the
network the step size $`d`$ and trains one step of size $`d`$ to land
where two steps of size $`d/2`$ land (Figure 3a). The half steps run on
the current weights with no gradients, and one call at the full size
regresses onto the mean of their velocities:

```math
\displaystyle s_{1} \displaystyle=v_{\theta}\bigl(x_{t},\,t,\,\tfrac{d}{2}\mid c\bigr),\qquad s_{2}=v_{\theta}\bigl(x_{t}+\tfrac{d}{2}\,s_{1},\;t+\tfrac{d}{2},\;\tfrac{d}{2}\mid c\bigr),
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{SC}} \displaystyle=\bigl\|v_{\theta}(x_{t},t,d\mid c)-\tfrac{1}{2}(s_{1}+s_{2})\bigr\|^{2}. \tag{2}
```

Figure 3: Shortcut self-consistency training lets one model sample at 1,
2, 4 or 8 steps; the release uses 8, and the benchmark scored 4, 8 and
16 only. (a) Schematic, after Frans et al. (2025). (b) Rows are to scale
on t. At the final settings on our own harness, 4 steps gave WER 1.17
against 0.97 at 8 (3 seeds; Section 7).

Each utterance trained this way draws one of 7 levels uniformly, from
$`d=1`$ down to $`d=1/64`$ (Figure 3b). A level takes its half steps
from the level below, and the last from plain flow matching at $`d=0`$.
One model can then sample at 1, 2, 4 or 8 steps, and the other powers of
2 up to 64 also lie on the trained grid. On the benchmark we scored 4, 8
and 16 steps only.

Training sums these two losses and one term each for the prior and the
duration predictor, with unit weights. A quarter of each batch trains
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{SC}}`$ and the rest $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}`$.
Each is divided by its own token count, so both carry equal total
weight.

### Generator network.

The generator is a diffusion transformer, DiT (Peebles and Xie, 2023),
of 5 blocks with RoPE and gated SwiGLU feed-forward layers (Shazeer,
2020). The encoder output, repeated per frame, passed through one
ConvNeXt block and averaged per token, joins $`x_{t}`$ at the input.
Time and step size enter through adaptive layer normalization (adaLN),
where a small network sets each normalization layer’s scale and shift.
In adaLN-single (Chen et al., 2024) all blocks share that network, and
each block adds a small learned table.

### Sampling.

Classifier-free guidance (Ho and Salimans, 2022) runs the generator
twice per step, with the text condition and with a learned null vector
that replaces the condition on 10% of training utterances. The sampler
extrapolates past the conditional velocity $`v_{c}`$, away from the null
velocity $`v_{\varnothing}`$, with weight $`w`$, and takes $`K`$ Euler
steps from noise scaled by a temperature of 0.6:

```math
\hat{v}=v_{c}+(w-1)(v_{c}-v_{\varnothing}),\qquad x^{i+1}=x^{i}+\tfrac{1}{K}\,\hat{v}\bigl(x^{i},\,\tfrac{i}{K},\,\tfrac{1}{K}\mid c\bigr). \tag{3}
```

The release default, $`K=8`$ and $`w=2.0`$, is 16 generator passes per
chunk of text.

### Vocoder.

The vocoder follows the design of Vocos (Siuzdak, 2024) and has no
upsampling layers. Its 8 ConvNeXt blocks and a linear head predict a
spectrum per mel frame, 1,025 log-magnitudes and 1,025 phases, and an
inverse short-time Fourier transform (iSTFT) rebuilds the waveform from
these overlapping spectra.

Training adds GAN losses, in which discriminator networks (Kong et al.,
2020; Jang et al., 2021) learn to tell real audio from generated and the
vocoder learns to fool them. The loss is
$`45\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{mel}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}+2\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{feat}}`$,
which combines L1 on log-mels at 4 resolutions, a least-squares
adversarial term (Mao et al., 2017) and a match on the discriminators’
internal activations.

### The inference path.

Synthesis normalizes the text and splits it into chunks of at most 300
characters, about 19 s at the release defaults. Every sentence of
Freya-TR-Eval (Pamuk et al., 2026), the benchmark we score on, is one
chunk. Predicted durations are divided by a speed of 0.95, rounded and
clamped to 1 to 60 frames (640 ms). A doubling rule lengthens repeated
sounds first (Section 6).

Every chunk gets a hidden " ee." after it, meant to put the real last
word mid-utterance. We have no measurement isolating a benefit at the
final settings, and its WER effect went both ways in earlier seed-0 runs
with the first vocoder fine-tune. A chunk that starts with ö or ı also
gets a hidden "ee, " before it, for the defect of Section 6. All
networks run on the whole string, and the audio is cut at the predicted
spans with 5 ms fades (Figure 4).

### Output chain.

Version 1.0.1 ends with fixed signal processing (Table 1), the only
default that differs from version 1.0.0. An equalizer (EQ), a gain that
varies with frequency, uses 5 second-order filters (Bristow-Johnson,
2021). A de-esser turns down loud energy above 5.5 kHz, the band of
hissing sounds such as s. A fixed +1.6 dB gain offsets the mean loudness
drop from those two, 1.596 dB, measured with the BS.1770 standard
(ITU-R, 2023) on 24 clips at 48 kHz. A soft limiter guards the peaks,
and 96 test clips over 4 output rates did not reach its threshold.

Version 1.0.0’s EQ, the “1.0.0 tone” of our figures, raised the bass and
cut the treble (Figure 19a). We chose the 1.0.1 EQ by ear after one
outside comment on that tone, and automatic scores moved slightly
against it (Section 7).

### Where the compute goes.

At the defaults a 5.11 s sentence costs 14.49 GMAC ($`10^{9}`$
multiply-accumulates), or 2.84 GMAC per second of output. The generator
dominates because it runs 16 times per chunk and the vocoder once. Four
steps with guidance cost 1.65 GMAC per second, 8 steps without guidance
1.63 and 1 step without guidance 0.59. These counts do not measure time.

## 3  Data from one teacher

Unmodified VoxCPM2, cloning one synthetic clip, generated all our
training audio on one 8-GPU machine. Automatic checks kept 1,325,846 of
1,361,734 clips, about 1,790 h, but Whisper checked only 7,500 of the
444,437 short clips, so no rule tested whether the rest read their text
correctly.

### The teacher.

All training audio was generated by unmodified VoxCPM2 (Zhou et al., 2026) (openbmb/VoxCPM2, revision 32279eff, Apache-2.0), cloning the
voice of one 9.88 s reference clip that is itself a synthetic render. On
all 1,361,734 clips it ran with classifier-free guidance 2.0 and 10
steps in ultimate mode, which gives the model the clip’s transcript and
two encodings of its audio. VoxCPM2’s main checkpoint has about 2.29B
parameters, about 2.38B with its AudioVAE (the part that decodes to
48 kHz audio), roughly 300 to 313 times the student. Its authors report
over 2 million hours of training speech in 30 languages, with no
separate figure for Turkish.

### The voice.

The training set contains no recordings of people: every clip was
synthesized by VoxCPM2 from one synthetic reference clip. The voice is
synthetic, chosen by listening from the renders of an earlier in-house
model. That model and VoxCPM2 were themselves trained on recordings of
real speakers, and the earlier model’s voice-locking started from a
reference recording of a consenting voice actor. We have not audited the
voice’s similarity to any real speaker. The model has no speaker input
and cannot clone voices.

Voice-locking fixes one voice across renders. We clustered renders of
that earlier model and, by listening, fixed the voice as 38 of them,
with a median F0 (fundamental frequency, heard as pitch) of 130.4 Hz and
a 5th to 95th percentile band of 125.1–137.5 Hz. VoxCPM2 then cloned the
24 kHz render closest to the cluster center, and one 48 kHz output
became the reference clip for all training data, the chosen reference
below.

identity. Identity is the cosine similarity of a render’s ECAPA speaker

We generated 917,297 lines, sentences up to about 25 s, and kept 898,046

sentences written by Qwen3 (Yang et al., 2025) and our own number
templates.

audio, after edge-silence trim and before the ×6 weighting of the
short-clip fine-tune.

### The short set.

We built it because the main run’s word errors concentrated in sentences
of 3 to 4 words and little of the long set was under 3 s (Figure 5b).
That length effect is confounded with register, the style of the
sentence (Section 7). We kept 427,800 of 444,437 clips, 207.29 h, with a
median of 1.64 s. Fragments of 2 to 6 words cut from the long-set text
pools make up 159.81 h. On the shipped path only the short-clip
fine-tune used this set, weighted ×6, and the vocoder never saw it.

A bug reached this set. Turkish pairs i with İ and ı with I, so when
Python’s str.upper turned a leading i into ASCII I, the normalizer
lowered it to ı. As a result 4,580 of the 5,589 kept ı-initial lines are
misspelled (for example ıyi, ıki). They stayed in the short set, and we
have no measurement isolating their effect on the model.

### Keeping the benchmark out.

We excluded benchmark sentences from the training text, which dropped 11
long-set lines and 5,562 short-set lines. A later recheck of the final
text with the same guard found no exact, contained or near match
(rapidfuzz string similarity of 85 or more), though it did not re-run
the short set’s rule for near matches to part of a sentence.

### What Whisper checked.

The Whisper speech recognizer (Radford et al., 2023), with the large-v3
checkpoint in faster-whisper (Klein and SYSTRAN, 2023), checked all
917,297 long-set clips. We turned it off for the short set after 15
shards, 7,500 of its 444,437 clips, because it pads each clip to 30 s,
which made QC about 10 times slower than generation. The other 436,937
clips have empty transcripts and a placeholder CER of 0. Speaking rate,
ECAPA identity, F0 band and UTMOS rules still applied to them, relaxed
for very short ones.

In the long set the 10% CER gate reads the whole line, and 82% of
ö-initial lines with a wrong first word passed it (Section 6). Clips
Whisper could not transcribe never reached long-set training, and the
same checkpoint scores the benchmark, which may flatter WER
(Section 10).

## 4  Training the acoustic model

The bf16 autocast weight cache kept gradients from the generator for the
first 6,000 of the main run’s 420,000 steps. The short-clip fine-tune in
the shipped weights is not distinguishable from the main run on the
benchmark, and the shipped lineage took 114.5 GPU-hours.

The shipped acoustic weights are an exponential moving average (decay up
to 0.9995), taken after 420,000 steps of the main run on the long set
and 51,880 steps of the short-clip fine-tune on both sets. Both use
AdamW (betas 0.9 and 0.98, weight decay 0.01), gradient clipping at 1.0
and bf16 autocast (mixed precision).

The main run used a base learning rate of $`3\times 10^{-4}`$ and 3 H200
GPUs, then 2 from step 55,000, with 96k mel frames per GPU from step
2,000. In parallel we trained a sibling run on long-set clips with a QC
UTMOS of at least 3.1.

### A silent bug.

We computed shortcut targets under no_grad inside the same autocast
region as the training pass. Autocast caches the weights it casts, so
the training pass received detached copies and the generator got no
gradients. Setting cache_enabled=False fixed it.

The signature is a flow-matching loss flat near 2.03 in training and
exactly constant in validation on the moving-average weights, with a
shortcut loss of 0 (Figure 8). The encoder, prior and duration predictor
trained normally (prior loss 0.529 to 0.039 by step 6,000), so total
loss still fell.

Figure 8: The bf16 autocast weight cache gave the generator no gradients
for the first 6,000 steps of the main run and 15,000 of the sibling run.
Validation loss on the moving-average weights stayed at exactly 2.0359
in the main run and 2.0342 in the sibling run, and at step 13,000 of the
sibling run the generator’s output weights still had zero norm.

![](arxiv-2611-13811v1--eaba2409091b.figures/inline-3.svg)

### The short-clip fine-tune.

It starts from step 420,000 of the main run with a fresh AdamW and a
learning rate of $`10^{-4}`$, and spends its last 23,880 steps at a
floor of $`5\times 10^{-6}`$. With the short set repeated six times, the
two sets total 2,802.62 h, of which short clips are 44.07%. Validation
runs on the first 256 clips of the validation split, all from the long
set, and its flow-matching loss ended at 0.3969, where it stood at step
2,000. A flat validation loss was also the bug’s signature, so we
compared checkpoints. The generator’s moving-average weights moved by
6.8% in relative L2 norm between the main run’s last step and the
shipped file.

On the benchmark the short-clip fine-tune is not distinguishable from
the main run. Its WER minus the main run’s is $`-`$0.08
\[$`-`$0.32, +0.15\] at 4 sampling steps and $`-`$0.05
\[$`-`$0.25, +0.15\] at 8 (95% intervals from a paired bootstrap over
sentences, 3 seeds, our own harness, no output chain, same vocoder).
Validation holds no short clips and these whole-benchmark intervals
include 0, so we cannot say what the fine-tune did for short input.

The sibling run’s filter has no fair test either. In the second
evaluation round during training, with the same vocoder run, it had WER
6.28% and UTMOS 1.78 against 5.21% and 2.26 for the main run (seed 0,
our own harness), at different learning rates and step counts.

### Compute.

MAS took 61.2% of main-thread profiler samples in an acoustic step.
Sending only its log-likelihoods to the CPU and giving it more threads
raised median throughput on 3 H200s from 15,954 to 20,628 audio-s/s
(+29%).

The shipped lineage took 114.5 GPU-hours, 36.4 for the acoustic model on
H200s and 78.0 for the vocoder (56.0 on H200s, 22.0 on A100s and an
L40S). All training runs took 159.0, of which 130.6 ran on the machine
of Section 3, where setup, generation and QC took another 33.7.
Evaluation is not counted.

## 5  The vocoder kept WER high

On our own harness, at adjacent pre-release acoustic checkpoints with
the same seed, fine-tuning the vocoder on the acoustic model’s own mels
cut WER from a mean of 3.89% to 1.75%, while a vocoder frozen over the
same stretch stayed at 3.63–4.14%.

Our 2.16M vocoder got worse as it trained on real mels. Its
copy-synthesis UTMOS on 40 corpus clips, meaning clips from the training
set, fell from 2.54 at step 20,000 to 2.17 at step 148,000, against 3.29
for the teacher (Figure 9a). Mel loss did not track the fall.

fine-tuning, the 3.93M vocoder alone costs about 0.24 UTMOS. Pre-release
checkpoints. (a) Checkpoints differ by about $`\pm`$0.1 UTMOS. The 2.16M
vocoder’s mel loss read 0.3927, 0.4225, 0.3544 and 0.3809 at steps 20k,

Its output also grew a ripple at 93.75 Hz, the vocoder’s frame rate. The
envelope peak at that rate rose from 4.2 dB at step 20,000 to 5.3 dB at
step 148,000, against 2.79 dB for the teacher (Figure 9b). The larger
3.93M vocoder of the shipped lineage reached UTMOS 2.69 with a peak of
2.82 dB at step 85,000.

### Fine-tuning on generated mels.

This real-mel vocoder still left WER above 3.6%. It had trained only on
real mels, and at inference it receives the generator’s mels, so we
fine-tuned a copy of its step 157,000 on those, the first vocoder
fine-tune.

The evaluation loop during training gave 7 pairs. Each scores the
real-mel vocoder on one acoustic checkpoint and the first vocoder
fine-tune on an adjacent one, at seed 0 and 4 sampling steps. Over these
pairs the fine-tune cut WER from a mean of 3.89% to 1.75% (Figure 10a).
The drop per pair runs from 1.66 to 2.63 points, and every paired
sentence-bootstrap 95% interval excludes 0. CER fell from 0.984% to
0.385% and UTMOS rose from 2.45 to 2.93 (Figure 10b).

A pair’s two checkpoints sit 1,000 to 4,000 steps apart. Further
acoustic training does not explain the drop, because the real-mel
vocoder, frozen at vocoder step 218,000, stayed at WER 3.63–4.14% and
UTMOS 2.44–2.47 while the main run moved from acoustic step 263,000 to
330,000, the last 5 of the 7 pairs (Figure 10c). It was still training
for the first two pairs.

Figure 10: At adjacent acoustic checkpoints, seed 0 and 4 sampling

For this fine-tune the acoustic model samples one fixed mel per clip, at
4 steps and classifier-free guidance 2.0 on the clip’s own MAS
durations, so frame counts match the teacher’s audio, which stays the
target. Each training crop takes the generated mel with probability 0.8
and the real mel otherwise, which is the “80%” of Figure 10c. The
shipped vocoder’s stages use AdamW (betas 0.8 and 0.99, weight decay
0.01, clipping at 10.0) on 1.003 s crops, 32 per GPU, with no moving
average.

These are pre-release checkpoints at temperature 1.0 with no output
chain, on our own harness. We never scored a real-mel vocoder at the
final acoustic checkpoint. WER here is Whisper’s, and no human listening
test of this model exists. With more training the main run and the first
vocoder fine-tune reached 1.38% on 3 seeds (acoustic step 420,000,
fine-tune step 301,490, 4 sampling steps), a later result from outside
the 7 pairs.

### Precedent.

Tacotron 2 and HiFi-GAN (Shen et al., 2018; Kong et al., 2020) also
report gains from vocoders trained on predicted features. Those are
human ratings on teacher-forced features, where the acoustic model sees
the real previous frames, so the effect sizes do not compare with ours
(Section 9).

### What remains.

After fine-tuning, the vocoder alone costs about 0.24 UTMOS (Figure 9c).
Copy synthesis through the first vocoder fine-tune at step 301,490, the
parent of the shipped vocoder, scores 3.069 against 3.306 for the
teacher on 200 corpus clips, and aligned generated mels at temperature
0.6 score 3.045. On the teacher’s 495 benchmark renders it scores 3.173
against 3.419, with WER 1.251% against 0.919% (1 seed, our own harness,
unseen text).

The last 76,860 of the shipped vocoder’s updates, the final vocoder
fine-tune on mels from the short-clip fine-tune, gave no demonstrated
gain. On 3 seeds through the release API on our own harness, before the
fixes of Section 6, WER moved from 1.115% to 1.089% (131 to 128 word
errors) and UTMOS from 3.268 to 3.256. The final vocoder fine-tune saw
mels at 4 steps, temperature 0.8 and MAS durations, while the release
samples at 8 steps, temperature 0.6 and predicted durations. The vocoder
never saw short-set audio.

## 6  The teacher’s ö defect and two reading fixes

By Whisper, the teacher gets first words right on only 26% of ö-initial
kept long-set lines, and the student learned this. A hidden spoken
prefix hides the defect without removing it, with 12 of 12 first words
right on 4 benchmark sentences. The paired interval for a duration rule
on doubled sounds includes 0.

### The defect in the teacher.

Only 26% of the 12,554 kept long-set lines whose first word begins with
the vowel ö, capital or not, have that word right in their QC Whisper
transcripts (95% CI 25.4–26.9; Figure 11a). Lines with other capital
first letters reach 94%, and capital Ö alone, the like-for-like figure,
scores 23.66%. No listening test of teacher clips exists.

Figure 11: By Whisper, only 26% of ö-initial kept long-set lines have a
right first word, against 94% for other capital first letters. (a) ı
errors have other causes: the word ı from Roman numerals, Irak and its
inflections, heard as drak, and spelling. (b) Later positions are pooled
and use aligned transcripts.

The same ö words score about 98% later in the clip (Figure 11b), which
ties the defect to the clip start. The clip-start rate depends on the
word, with 76.8% for özellikle against 0.8% for öyle. Wrong transcripts
often open with a phantom syllable, as in latte for önce.

We did not establish the cause inside the teacher. With 4 renders per
configuration on ö prompts, Whisper read 4 of 4 first words right in
reference-only mode (an encoding of the reference clip’s audio, no
transcript), and in ultimate mode 0 or 1 of 4 with most references and 4
of 4 with one. Samples this small are only suggestive.

### How we know it is audio.

All of this rests on Whisper, which could be misreading good audio. Two
checks point to the audio instead. At equal character count, long-set ö
lines that Whisper reads with a wrong first word run a median 0.315 s
longer than those with a right one, which suggests extra sound, and
right ö lines match lines starting with ü, the highest-scoring letter,
within noise (Figure 12a). The short-set rows use no recognizer, and
there all ö lines run 0.169 s longer than ü lines while ı lines do not
stand out.

Figure 12: The ö defect adds audio measurable without speech recognition
(ASR), 82% of ö lines with a wrong first word passed the 10% CER gate,
and most other systems’ first words transcribe correctly on the same
sentences. (b) Rejected long-set lines are counted, not binned. (c)
Teacher with the chosen reference. Small n: 4 sentences.

Whisper also does not invent the syllable. On the 4 ö-initial benchmark
sentences, a small control, it reads 11 or 12 of 12 first words (3 takes
each) from most other systems on the public harness, including EMA
Lightning, whose card reports the same teacher model with another voice.
On our own harness it reads 0 of 4 from the teacher with the chosen
reference, in 1 take (Figure 12c).

### How the student inherited it.

Our 10% whole-line CER gate missed most of the defect. Of the 11,270
generated long-set lines with a wrong ö first word, the gate rejected
1,879, other rules rejected 120, and 9,271 (82%) passed (Figure 12b).
The lines that passed stayed in the training data, and the student
learned the defect. Without the prefix it gets 5 of 12 first words right
on the 4 benchmark sentences (our own harness, 3 seeds, before the final
vocoder fine-tune). Informally, one of us heard student renders of three
ö-initial sentences as broken.

![](arxiv-2611-13811v1--eaba2409091b.figures/inline-5.svg)

### The hidden prefix.

The defect sits at the clip start, so we make the model say something
else first and cut that audio away. A chunk that starts with ö or ı gets
a hidden "ee, " in front of it, cut at its predicted span (Section 2).
With the prefix, Whisper reads 12 of 12 first words on the 4 sentences
on both harnesses. Figure 13 shows one of them. With the final vocoder
fine-tune, per-clip no-prefix data exists only for seed 0, where Whisper
again writes Löri for Öğle.

![](arxiv-2611-13811v1--eaba2409091b.figures/figure-3.webp)

Figure 13: On benchmark sentence 0397, Whisper wrote Löri in the
no-prefix run and Öğle in the prefix run, at seeds 0 and 1. Read the
words above the cells; the spectrograms only show where the prefix sits
and where we cut it. The transcripts come from two own-harness runs
before 1.0.0, and the no-prefix run predates the final vocoder
fine-tune, so the figure does not isolate the prefix. Sentence:
Freya-TR-Eval (Pamuk et al., 2026; Freya, 2026).

In own-harness runs before 1.0.0, with the final vocoder fine-tune, the
prefix lowers 3-seed WER from 1.089% to 1.013% and CER from about 0.25%
to 0.204%. On seed 0 the gain comes from 2 of the 4 sentences, where we
had also seen the symptom. The missing per-clip data leaves this step
with no 3-seed paired interval.

Applied to every chunk in a probe of 84 new sentences, the prefix broke
other words, such as Jöle, heard as göle or Göle on 2 of 3 seeds. So we
restrict it to ö and ı. The ı part rests on that probe alone, because
the benchmark has no ı-initial sentence. In the probe, first words
starting with capital I went from 6 to 8 of 9 right, and Ilgaz was heard
as Bulgaz, Dılgaz or Rılgaz without the prefix. The teacher’s ı errors
have other causes (Figure 11a).

### Doubled sounds.

The doubling rule multiplies by 1.3 the predicted duration of the second
of two identical symbols, or of the word gap between them (the predicted
span of the space symbol). Such pairs occur in 167 of the benchmark’s
495 sentences (112 pairs within words, 111 across word gaps).

The rule targets doubled sounds heard as one, as in o olumsuz read as
olumsuz, a pattern we found in benchmark errors. Before the rule, 21
sentences failed on all 3 seeds of our own harness. EMA Lightning also
fails 13 of them on all 3 of its seeds and gets 6 right on all 3, so
Whisper can read those 6 from another model’s audio. Of those 6, 3 show
the pattern (Figure 14a).

![](arxiv-2611-13811v1--eaba2409091b.figures/figure-4.webp)

Figure 14: The doubling rule moves 3-seed WER from 1.013% to 0.970%,
with a paired interval that includes 0; it fixes o olumsuz on 3 of 3
seeds on both harnesses, and the doubled rr in berraktı stays wrong.
Own-harness runs use 1.0.0 settings and public takes 1.0.1. (b) The

near 6% because of digits and foreign names the scorer does not match.
Sentence: Freya-TR-Eval (Pamuk et al., 2026; Freya, 2026).

A result on Turkish consonants motivates the rule. Lahiri and Hankamer
(1988) find that doubled Turkish \[t\] and \[k\] between vowels, from 3
male speakers, are held almost three times as long as single ones, about
120 ms longer. Vowels, other consonants and word-gap pairs, which the
rule also lengthens, fall outside their data. On the benchmark we tried
stretch factors of 1.3, 1.5 and, on seed 0 only, 2.0, and we shipped 1.3
(Figure 14b).

Over 3 seeds on our own harness, the rule moves WER from 1.013% to
0.970% and CER from 0.204% to 0.193%. Both paired intervals include 0
(WER $`-`$0.043 \[$`-`$0.135, +0.035\], CER $`-`$0.011 \[$`-`$0.035,
+0.010\] points). Word errors fall from 53 to 46 in sentences with such
a pair and go from 66 to 68 in the rest. The rule came from these
sentences, so these numbers are optimistic by selection.

Off the benchmark the direction agrees, and the change is not separable
from seed noise. On 500 other sentences, 464 of them not in the training
text by exact match (near-duplicates unchecked), 3-seed WER moves from
6.12% to 6.02% and CER from 1.53% to 1.48% (Figure 14c). Nearly all the
WER gain is one seed. For these runs and the stretch factors 1.5 and
2.0, we kept only the lines the scorer printed.

### What did not help.

On our own harness, stretching every word gap by a factor of 1.25 gave
3-seed WER 1.055% against 1.013% without it.

Filtered training gave no demonstrated gain on the same harness. We
fine-tuned the shipped acoustic model twice on filtered data and scored
both runs with the shipped vocoder, not fine-tuned again. One run
dropped lines with a bad start, gave good ö and ı starts more weight,
and scored WER 1.174% and CER 0.256% on seed 0 without the prefix,
against 1.047% and 0.241%. The other also dropped every long-set line
with any character error and scored 1.106% and 0.214% over 3 seeds with
the prefix, against 1.013% and 0.204%. We made no fix at the source, and
regenerating the affected clips remains open (Section 11).

## 7  Evaluation

On the 495 sentences of Freya-TR-Eval, on which we tuned settings, Mini
1.0.1 has WER 1.09% and CER 0.220% on the public harness. A paired
bootstrap cannot distinguish these from EMA Lightning 1.0.1 or, on our
own harness, from the teacher (1 seed), and no human listening test of
Mini exists. EMA Lightning is ahead on full-band UTMOS and, on our own
harness, Mini in the 8 kHz band the benchmark’s card asks for, while 16
sentences fail repeatedly for Mini and not for EMA Lightning.

### The benchmark.

Freya-TR-Eval (Pamuk et al., 2026) holds 495 Turkish sentences of 3 to
13 words in two registers: 406 everyday sentences written by an LLM and
89 short-native sentences from the public corpora Common Voice and
CoVoST2. No reference contains a digit, so these scores do not test
number readings. Three takes add up to 11,751 scored words and 80,922
characters. We report the benchmark card’s secondary metric,
band-matched WER and CER. We did not compute its primary metric,
semantic WER, in which an LLM judge counts only errors that change the
meaning.

### Two harnesses.

The public harness (PatientDesk AI, 2026b) carries the headline numbers
and all 15 systems, and a reader can re-run its open-weights rows
(Appendix B). Development runs, the teacher and 8 kHz UTMOS are on our
own harness, whose code is private. Both pass raw text to each system
and score alike: 8 kHz band matching, the faster-whisper large-v3
recognizer (Klein and SYSTRAN, 2023) at beam 5, text normalization for
scoring with identical output on all 3,772 strings we compared,
corpus-level WER and CER per take from jiwer (Vaessen, 2018), and
UTMOS22-strong (Saeki et al., 2022) from SpeechMOS v1.2.0 at 16 kHz.

### Results.

Mini 1.0.1’s WER of 1.09% on the public harness (Table 5) is second of
the 15 systems we ran, behind EMA Lightning by 0.05 points, a gap the
paired intervals below cannot distinguish from zero. Its CER of 0.220%
is the lowest of the 11 open-weights systems (0.225% for EMA Lightning,
0.229% for Trendyol-TTS) and the second lowest of all 15, behind our own
closed Alania-2 voice Öncel (0.217%). These gaps are 2 to 7 character
errors in 80,922, and none is distinguishable from zero. EMA Lightning
is lower on our own harness (0.198% against 0.205%). Mini ranks 11th of
15 at 3.18 on full-band UTMOS, below most large systems (Trendyol-TTS
3.82, Alania-2 F1 of 7 Oct 3.67). Ranks count Mini 1.0.1 only and
Alania-2 at its top voice in each column. We tuned Mini’s settings on
these sentences, so final-settings numbers are optimistic by selection,
and the scoring Whisper checkpoint also filtered the training data
(Section 10).

### Paired comparison.

differences over at least 10,000 resamples of the 495 sentences, with
each sentence’s two scores kept together. We call Mini “level with”

the two apart; they need not be equal. With 495 sentences and 3 takes

differences cannot be resolved. The term never extends to UTMOS.

### Where the errors sit.

A sentence that fails in every take points to a cause in the model, the
recognizer or the scorer. On the public harness, Mini 1.0.1’s errors

at least one word error, and 70.3% of Mini’s word errors sit in
sentences that fail in all 3 takes, against 54.9% for EMA Lightning. Of
Mini’s 24 such sentences, 17 also fail on all 3 own-harness seeds.

## 8  Speed and where it runs

On one NVIDIA L4 (all 495 sentences, different VMs) Mini 1.0.1 runs at
35× real time, 1.65 times slower than plain EMA Lightning 1.0.1 by RTF
and 9.5 times slower than compiled. On two threads of an Apple M5 laptop
CPU (first 100 sentences, other jobs running) it runs at 41×. No real
phone has been measured.

### GPU.

On one NVIDIA L4, Mini 1.0.1 has an RTF of 0.029 (35× real time). EMA
Lightning 1.0.1 has 0.017 (58×) in plain PyTorch and 0.0030 (331×)
compiled, with the same WER and CER (Figure 20b). By wall time the gap
to plain EMA Lightning is 1.87 times, because Mini speaks 13% longer on
the same text.

## 9  Related work

Training one model on another model’s audio has precedents back to at
least 2021. We found no paper that uses shortcut self-consistency in a
TTS acoustic model, and the one Turkish human MOS study of open systems
we found orders them differently from our UTMOS, on different renders.

### Synthetic training data.

The earliest precedent we found is TTS-by-TTS (Hwang et al., 2021),
which added 179 h of speech from a Tacotron 2-based teacher to 5 h of
recordings. It reports a MOS of 3.74, 40% above the conventional method.
Zhou et al. (2025) train a 20.9M Matcha-TTS student on 100 h of purely
synthetic speech.

Three recent works come closest to ours. By its card, EMA Lightning
shares our teacher model (Section 1). Wayu-Paxa-TTS-Edge (Pipatanakul
et al., 2026) is a fixed-voice Thai student trained only on synthetic
speech from a 15 s reference. Its authors state that teacher errors
become training targets, which matches the start-of-clip ö defect our
student learned (Section 6). Paradee (Mahendrakar, 2026) is an 8.07M
single-voice English student of Kokoro-82M (hexgrad, 2025). It also uses
the teacher’s internal features, so it learns no alignment, while we
train on audio alone.

Trikutam (2026) reports that a small Hindi model trained from scratch on
about 17.6 h fails, and warm-starts the 190M student in the figure from
its teacher. Geng et al. (2026) find that synthetic data improves
phonetic accuracy while suppressing prosodic variability, the range of
rhythm and pitch. We did not check our model for this.

### Small models.

Figure 21a, a sample of systems, lists nine complete models smaller than
Mini. They are two saanoTTS models (Thapa, 2026), two Pocket TTS models
(Orsini, 2026), LE2E (Vecino et al., 2023), Nix-TTS (Chevi et al.,
2023), Mini-MB-iSTFT-VITS (Kawamura et al., 2023) and, by their own
README and card, TinyTTS (tronghieuit, 2026) and Kokoro-7M-Distill
(oddadmix, 2026).¹¹ 1 SupertonicTTS is 44M in its report and 66M on its
released card (Kim et al., 2025; Supertone, 2025); we plot 44M. Paradee
is 8.07M in its paper and 7.88M in its later README; we plot the paper.
XTTS-v2 is 467M by file size, 470M on cards and 750M in Zhou et al.

### Few-step sampling.

We use shortcut self-consistency (Frans et al., 2025). Its original
paper evaluated it on images, and Flow2GAN tested it for mel-conditioned
vocoding (Yao et al., 2026). We found no paper that uses it in a TTS
acoustic model, which is absence of evidence only. EMA Lightning’s card
describes another route, a four-step student distilled with DMD2 (Yin
et al., 2024) from a same-size flow teacher that needs 16 midpoint
steps. Nguyen et al. (2025) report image artifacts when classifier-free
guidance meets shortcut models. Our generator combines the two, and
nothing in our evaluation isolates this effect.

### Vocoder fine-tuning.

Tacotron 2 (Shen et al., 2018) trains its WaveNet vocoder on
ground-truth-aligned predictions and reports a MOS of 4.526 when
synthesizing from predicted features, against 4.362 for a vocoder
trained on ground truth. HiFi-GAN (Kong et al., 2020) fine-tunes on
predicted mels and reports MOS 3.91 to 4.18 for its V1, 3.88 to 4.12 for
V2 and 3.89 to 4.02 for V3, with mel error rising as perceptual quality
rose. Those vocoders train on teacher-forced features. Our mels are
sampled, with durations from alignment to the target clip and no teacher
forcing, and we score WER, so the effect sizes do not compare. JETS
avoids the step by training both stages jointly (Lim et al., 2022), and
Guo et al. (2022) report that a discriminator on the acoustic model is
more effective than fine-tuning.

### Turkish TTS.

The FreyaTTS report (Pamuk et al., 2026) has the only Turkish human MOS
of open systems we found, from 24 raters and 2,566 ratings. Among its
means are XTTS-v2 3.82, FreyaTTS 3.68, F5-TTS 3.63, MMS-TTS 3.58, Piper
3.47 and GlowTTS 2.53. Mini and EMA Lightning are not in it. Its order
of systems disagrees with our UTMOS order on different renders, so we
report no correlation. FreyaTTS and, by its card, EMA Lightning also
read characters.

### Speech quality metrics.

The UTMOS strong learner we use comes from the English main track of the
VoiceMOS 2022 challenge (Saeki et al., 2022), and we found no validation
against Turkish listeners. Asonitis et al. (2026) find that automatic
predictors agree with listeners on audible defects but fall near chance
on clean TTS. Takagi et al. (2026), on Japanese speech, find them
insensitive to prosodic errors, with most scoring lower-F0 speakers
higher, and Mini and EMA Lightning have different voices. Paradee’s
author reports a 0.5 UTMOS gap between two decoders that sounded alike
by ear (Mahendrakar, 2026), and saanoTTS’s author reports that aggregate
predictors missed a failure on sibilants (s-like sounds) evident in
listening (Thapa, 2026). Ozkose et al. (2026) find that rankings of
Turkish systems over eighteen objective metrics depend largely on metric
family and utterance length.

## 10  Limitations and responsible use

No human listening test of this model exists, and we tuned its settings
on the benchmark that scores it. The Whisper checkpoint that scores the
benchmark also filtered the training data. The teacher’s start-of-clip
defect remains in the model, hidden by the prefix, and we have not
audited the voice’s likeness to any real speaker.

### Evidence.

All our quality numbers are automatic metrics on one benchmark. WER and
CER are its band-matched secondary metric, and UTMOS is an
English-trained proxy that we compare across different voices.
Own-harness seeds ran on different GPUs and EMA Lightning’s on an H200.
One seed spans about $`\pm`$0.13 WER points, and the teacher has one
seed.

### Selection on the benchmark.

Final-settings numbers are optimistic by selection. The doubling rule
came from the benchmark’s errors, and we screened temperature, steps,
speed, suffix and EQ on its 495 sentences. The public harness uses other
seeds on the same sentences. The prefix rule’s scope came from
training-data statistics, but its symptom also appeared on benchmark
sentences. A worse benchmark score counted against one teacher reference
(Section 3).

Whisper ran at beam 1 in QC and at beam 5 in scoring. Clips it could not
transcribe left the long set, and our vocoder and settings choices used
its WER. WER may therefore flatter the model relative to human
intelligibility, by an amount we have not measured.

### Errors that remain.

The long set kept 9,271 lines whose first ö word is wrong by Whisper,
filtered fine-tunes gave no demonstrated gain, and we have not
regenerated the data at the source (Section 6). On the public harness,
Mini 1.0.1’s errors repeat across takes more than EMA Lightning 1.0.1’s,
and some of the repeats look like model defects (Section 7). Nobody has
listened to these takes.

### Scope.

The model has one voice and one style and speaks Turkish only, with no
style or emotion control beyond speed. English words are respelled, and
whole English sentences are not supported. The model card names short
interjections on their own as the weakest area, flat or rushed
(PatientDesk AI, 2026a), and we have no metric for them alone. The
vocoder never saw short-clip audio, and Whisper checked only 1.7% of the
short set. Long text is split at sentence ends into independent chunks,
so prosody does not carry across them. Readings follow the normalizer,
so Roman numerals, ordinals before names, unusual formats and some
foreign names can be read wrongly (Section 2).

### Speed and builds.

We have measured no real phone, only an Android emulator, and Section 11
lists the other timing runs we have not made. Other jobs were running
during the M5 measurements. Version 1.0.1’s default EQ, which we chose
by ear, lowers UTMOS by 0.065 against 1.0.0 on the public harness and
raises WER and CER within noise.

### Retraining, voice and use.

Third parties cannot retrain the model (Appendix B). Section 3 gives the
voice’s lineage. We ask users to tell listeners when they are hearing a
synthetic voice, and not to use the model to impersonate real people or
for fraud, harassment or deceptive calls.

## 11  What stays open

Four items stay open: ö clips regenerated by the teacher, a human
listening test, real phones, and new timing runs of version 1.0.1.

### Regenerate at the source.

Our 4 renders per teacher configuration (Section 6) cannot pick a
setting that reads first words right, so regeneration starts with more
renders, checked by ear as well as by Whisper. Then the acoustic model
retrains on the new clips and the vocoder fine-tunes on its new mels, a
step neither filtered run completed. The defect is gone if the student,
with the prefix off, gets ö first words right as often as first words
with other capital letters, on a set larger than the benchmark’s 4
ö-initial sentences.

### Listening test.

A blind test with Turkish listeners on Mini 1.0.0 and 1.0.1, EMA
Lightning 1.0.1 and the teacher would settle three questions that
automatic metrics leave open. Listener transcripts would show whether
WER flatters Mini. Ratings would show whether the UTMOS gaps to EMA
Lightning, which run in opposite directions at full band and at 8 kHz,
match what people hear across two voices, and how the 1.0.1 EQ rates
against 1.0.0’s.

### Phones.

Our only Android speeds come from an emulator with 1.0.0’s output chain
(Section 8). The engine’s app has a speed test, so running the signed
packages on real phones would give the missing numbers, including for
the 32-bit package, which has not been run at all.

### Re-timing.

Timing the browser page and the emulator at 1.0.1 would replace the
numbers we measured with 1.0.0’s output chain. Our same-machine
comparison with EMA Lightning also used Mini 1.0.0, so 1.0.1’s ratios on
that machine’s L40S and 8-vCPU AMD EPYC are unknown (Section 8). Timing
Mini 1.0.1 and EMA Lightning 1.0.1 on one machine would settle them, and
we have no such run planned.

### What a reader can re-run.

The benchmark sentences are Freya-TR-Eval (Pamuk et al., 2026; Freya,
2026), and our dataset holds the harness, one adapter per open system,
results.json, and per-clip transcripts, scores and timing (PatientDesk
AI, 2026b). Every one of the 20 rows of results.json, closed systems
included, can be recomputed with zero difference from the per-clip
files, without rendering anything. Table 5 leaves out EMA Lightning’s
compiled build, Alania-2 F1 (7 Oct) as a bare model, and a 100-sentence
Alania-1 speed row. Mini 1.0.0 ran on the same harness and seeds but is
not in that file, which now holds 1.0.1. To render again, install
package version 1.0.1, run run_antalia_mini.py on a CUDA GPU, then
score.py and aggregate.py, and compare with results.json. For EMA
Lightning, install 1.0.1.

Table 9 traces the shipped weights. On the model repository, commit
8a79528 is 1.0.1 and commit e0d3182b is 1.0.0, and Antalia(revision=...)
selects one. Re-timing 1.0.0 also needs its own wheel from PyPI, because
the 1.0.1 wheel caches the EQ filter. Settings are options, so
Antalia(prefix="", gem_stretch=1.0) turns off the hidden prefix and the
doubling rule.

Seeds fix the audio only on one software stack (Section 2). Re-scoring
the 7 Oct Alania-2 F1 audio on an A100, the GPU that scored the 12 Oct
Alania-2 rows, changed 3 of 1,485 transcripts and WER by 0.0085 points.
RTF depends on the machine, and ours were L4s on GCP g2-standard-8 for
Mini and g2-standard-16 for EMA Lightning.

### What a reader cannot re-run.

Training code, data generation scripts, training data and the chosen
reference clip are not public (Table 8), and the shipped runs used flags
that exist only in our private repository, so third parties cannot
retrain the model. The data recipe exists only as prose, on the model
card and in Section 3. The analyses in Sections 3 to 6 rest on that
private material. The own-harness rows, including every 8 kHz UTMOS
value, use code that is not public, and so do the parity tests of
Section 8 and the normalizer checks below. The dataset has the per-clip
transcripts behind the public-harness intervals of Section 7 but no
bootstrap script, so a reader has to write one.

A normalizer replay of 31,584 recorded text cases on the 1.0.1 code gave
0 mismatches. The browser page’s JavaScript port matches the Python code
on 1,171 of 1,171 sentences, and the Android engine’s Kotlin port on
31,584 of 31,584 cases on the JVM. Replays catch code changes and cannot
catch a wrong reading.
