# Revise-TTS: A Non-Masking Refinement Framework for Masked Diffusion Models in Text-to-Speech

Wanli Sun\sthanksWork done during an internship at HojoAI    Dan Su   
Anton Ragni

###### Abstract

Text-to-speech (TTS) based on masked diffusion models (MDMs) achieves
competitive zero-shot speech synthesis performance. Unlike
autoregressive (AR) models, MDMs define a corruption process that maps
clean speech-token sequences to masked maps and learn a reverse
denoising process to recover the reference tokens. However, MDMs commit
to intermediate predictions that cannot be revised in subsequent
inference steps. Consequently, prediction errors accumulate and
propagate, degrading synthesized speech quality. We present Revise-TTS,
a single-stage framework that applies hierarchical binary quantization
(HBQ) and generative refinement networks (GRN) to zero-shot speech
generation. First, we replace finite scalar quantization (FSQ) with HBQ
to introduce a scalable binary representation for speech tokens with an
explicit coarse-to-fine structure. Second, GRN is trained to reconstruct
the token sequence from a mixture of ground-truth and random tokens.
During inference, iterative refinement enables self-correction by
randomly discarding a small proportion of previously predicted tokens
with new predictions. Experiments and ablation studies demonstrate that
Revise-TTS achieves competitive zero-shot speech synthesis performance
using HBQ. It can further be extended to any MDM-based TTS task as an
alternative training and inference method.

## 1 Introduction

Zero-shot text-to-speech (TTS) has recently achieved high-quality speech
generation through autoregressive (AR) \[36, 14\] and non-autoregressive
(NAR) modeling \[34, 40, 9, 39\]. AR models generate tokens from left to
right, causing error accumulation and requiring $`N`$ decoding steps for
an $`N`$-token sequence. In contrast, NAR models generate all tokens in
parallel and generally require far fewer inference steps, reducing
latency and avoiding AR error accumulation. Masked diffusion models
(MDMs) \[3, 29, 22\] are an important NAR framework that predicts masked
tokens over multiple denoising steps. However, typical MDM decoding
commits non-overlapping indices across intermediate steps, preventing
high-confidence but incorrect predictions from being revised. This
problem is further complicated in two-stage TTS systems with separate
text-to-semantic and semantic-to-acoustic modules, where the source of a
generation error is difficult to identify. Recent advances in
single-stage TTS \[12, 40, 8\] suggest that revisable generation
algorithms can be developed directly within the NAR TTS framework.

Despite this progress, discrete-token quantization in TTS remains
limited. FSQ \[25\] independently quantizes each latent dimension into
fixed scalar levels, producing a flat representation, while higher
precision requires more dimensions. Larger codebooks preserve finer
acoustic details and reduce quantization error but expand the token
prediction space and complicate training. Conversely, smaller codebooks
are easier to optimize but may discard important linguistic and speaker
information.

Error revision algorithms have been explored in visual synthesis \[21,
15\] and text generation \[11, 33, 38\]. These methods either improve
sampling quality \[33\] or add modules to identify incorrect
positions \[21\] or tokens \[38, 11\]. Our approach follows the
Generative Refinement Networks (GRN) framework \[15\], which treats
generation as iterative global refinement rather than masked-token
prediction. By repeatedly revising partial sequences, later steps can
correct earlier errors. We also introduce Hierarchical Binary
Quantization (HBQ) to address FSQ bottlenecks. HBQ represents continuous
latent features through multi-level binary rounds, yielding a
hierarchical representation with progressively higher precision in TTS.
To address the revision in MDM-based TTS, this paper investigates the
use of overlapping in MDM-based TTS following GRN in visual synthesis.
Concretely, it investigates utilizing GRN in TTS model and HBQ in the
quantization of speech tokens.

In summary, our contributions can be summarized as follows:

- •
  the first error-correction MDM-based TTS model;
- •
  an exploration of HBQ for speech-token quantization;
- •
  an extended framework for MDM-based TTS .

## 2 Methodology

Masked Diffusion Models (MDM) \[30, 29\] are parametric models
$`p_{\bm{\theta}}`$ trained to denoise a fixed noising process $`q`$,
which maps a clean speech-token map $`\bm{Y}_{0}`$ to a progressively
masked map $`\bm{Y}_{t}`$ at noise level $`t\in[0,1]`$. Let
$`\bm{Y}_{0}\in\mathcal{V}^{T\times Q}`$ denote the acoustic token map
of the target speech, where $`T`$ is the sequence length and $`Q`$ is
the number of token codebooks. Following the simplified corruption
process \[29\], also known as “absorbing-state” diffusion, the
probability at each target position $`(i,j)`$ for any noise level $`t`$
is given by:

```math
q(\bm{Y}_{t}\mid\bm{Y}_{0},t)=\prod_{i=1}^{T}\prod_{j=1}^{Q}\Bigl[t\cdot\delta_{\mathrm{M}}(y_{t}^{(i,j)})+(1-t)\cdot\delta_{y_{0}^{i,j}}(y_{t}^{(i,j)})\Bigr], \tag{1}
```

where $`\mathrm{M}`$ denotes the mask token and
$`\delta_{a}(y)=\mathbbm{1}[y=a]`$ denotes a point mass at $`a`$. During
denoising, the model predicts the most likely clean token at each masked
position, conditioned on the input text $`\bm{x}`$, the optional speech
prompt $`\bm{Y}^{P}`$, and the corrupted token map $`\bm{Y}_{t}`$, as
follows:

```math
\widehat{y}_{0}^{(i,j)}=\arg\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(y_{0}^{(i,j)}=v\mid\bm{Y}_{t},t;\bm{x},\bm{Y}^{P}\right), \tag{2}
```

where $`\mathcal{V}`$ denotes the vocabulary of acoustic tokens and
$`v\in\mathcal{V}`$ is a candidate token. At each denoising step $`t`$,
only the highest-confidence predictions are committed to update
$`\bm{Y}_{t}`$, which is then reused as the current token map in the
following iteration. Confidence is defined as the maximum predicted
token probability. The number of committed predictions is determined by
a masking schedule function, e.g., a linear schedule \[30\] or a cosine
schedule \[40\]. The remaining positions stay masked, while previously
committed tokens remain unchanged. Consequently, an incorrectly
committed token cannot be revised and may influence subsequent
predictions.

### 2.1 Hierarchical binary quantization

Recent audio tokenizers \[1, 37, 4\] follow an autoencoder architecture,
where an acoustic encoder extracts acoustic features for reconstruction,
while a semantic encoder extracts semantic features that capture
linguistic informations. These two features are concatenated to form
fused representations $`\bm{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C}`$, where $`T`$
is the token-sequence length and $`C`$ is the number of latent channels.
During training of audio tokenizers, the fused representations are
quantized into discrete tokens, which are used for both acoustic
reconstruction and auxiliary semantic supervision. During inference,
only the acoustic decoder is used to reconstruct speech from the
quantized representations.

Hierarchical binary quantization (HBQ) \[15\] is applied to quantize the
fused representation $`\bm{H}`$. First, HBQ normalizes the fused
representation element-wise as $`\bar{\bm{H}}=\tanh(\bm{H})`$. As
illustrated in Figure 1, HBQ performs $`M`$ successive binary refinement
rounds for each scalar $`z\in(-1,1)`$ in $`\bar{\bm{H}}`$. At round
$`m`$, the decision centre $`c_{m}`$ and binary label
$`b_{m}\in\{0,1\}`$ are computed as follows:

```math
\displaystyle c_{m} \displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{m-1}\frac{2b_{r}-1}{2^{r}},\qquad c_{1}=0, \tag{3}
```

```math
\displaystyle b_{m} \displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&z\leq c_{m},\\
1,&z>c_{m},\end{cases}\qquad m=1,\ldots,M, \tag{4}
```

After $`M`$ refinement rounds, each normalized value $`{z}`$ is
represented by the binary labels $`\{b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{M}\}`$. As
illustrated in Figure 1, $`z=-0.113`$ is encoded as the bit sequence
$`011`$, which corresponds to the discrete index $`3`$ and the quantized
value $`\hat{z}=-0.125`$. Each refinement round halves the current
interval. Thus, after $`M`$ rounds, each code represents an interval of
width $`2^{1-M}`$ in the normalized latent space, yielding a maximum
quantization error of $`2^{-M}`$.

Figure 1: Illustration of HBQ. Each round halves the selected
quantization interval. For $`z=-0.113`$, three rounds produce the bit
sequence $`011`$, corresponding to the discrete index $`3`$.

Encoding fused representations using a codebook of size $`2^{CM}`$
(e.g., $`C=16`$ and $`M=4`$) is challenging for TTS models because of
the large codebook size. Following prior work \[15\], two compact
representations are considered:
$`\bm{Y}_{\text{ind}}\in\left\{0,\ldots,2^{M}-1\right\}^{[T,C]}`$ and
$`\bm{Y}_{\text{bit}}\in\{0,1\}^{[T,C,M]}`$. Both preserve the same
information capacity of $`CM`$ bits per frame, but the use of
$`\bm{Y}_{\text{ind}}`$ in the cascading TTS models would require
$`C2^{M}`$-way classifications, whereas $`\bm{Y}_{\text{bit}}`$ performs
$`CM`$ binary classifications.

HBQ and FSQ are both lookup-free quantization algorithms that avoid an
explicit VQ objective term during training, such as the commitment loss
used in vector quantization (VQ) or residual vector quantization (RVQ).
HBQ quantizes each feature dimension using an explicit coarse-to-fine
ordering, whereas FSQ quantizes each feature dimension using a fixed set
of scalar levels. In addition, the outputs of HBQ can be used directly
for bitwise prediction or packed into an integer index. The following
experiments compare FSQ with HBQ configurations using different
combinations of $`C`$ and $`M`$ to demonstrate the effectiveness of HBQ.

### 2.2 Generative refinement network for TTS

Although HBQ provides a compact quantization algorithm for MDM-based
TTS, improved quantization does not address the irreversible prediction
errors during iterative decoding. Therefore, an overlapping decoding for
MDM following GRN \[16\] is adopted, enabling the model to preserve
correct tokens while replacing incorrect tokens in subsequent
iterations.

Using the two HBQ representations defined above lead to two modes:
$`\mathrm{GRN}_{\mathrm{ind}}`$ and
$`\mathrm{GRN}_{\mathrm{bit}}`$,which are in the index and flattened bit
token spaces, respectively. Both variants share the training and
sampling procedures described below.

#### Training

At each training iteration, we independently sample a retention ratio
$`l\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)`$. A small $`l`$ produces a heavy corruption,
whereas a large $`l`$ produces a light corruption. Uniform sampling
therefore exposes a single model to the continous corruption levels
encountered during iterative refinement. A binary selection map
$`\bm{S}`$ is then sampled according to $`l`$ and used to construct the
corrupted input token map $`\bm{F}`$ by mixing the ground-truth token
map $`\bm{Y_{0}}`$ with uniformly sampled random tokens:

```math
\bm{F}=\bm{S}\odot\bm{Y}_{0}+(\bm{1}-\bm{S})\odot\bm{Y}_{\mathrm{rand}}, \tag{5}
```

where $`\bm{Y}_{\mathrm{rand}}`$ is a randomly sampled token map with
the same shape as $`\bm{Y}_{0}`$. Given conditioning inputs such as text
$`{\bm{x}}`$ and prompt speech $`{\bm{Y}^{P}}`$, the model is trained to
reconstruct all target acoustic tokens using the cross-entropy loss:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRN}}=-\frac{1}{TQ}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\log p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(y_{0}^{(i,j)}\mid\bm{F},\bm{x},\bm{Y}^{P}\right). \tag{6}
```

The objective in Eq. 6 trains GRN to reconstruct the entire target token
map from partially corrupted inputs, thereby replacing corrupted tokens
while preserving correct ones. During inference, the retention ratio
follows a cosine schedule function and no separate policy network is
learned for token selection.

#### Sampling

Although GRN is trained with the one-step objective in eq. 6 to
reconstruct $`\bm{Y}_{0}`$ from a randomly corrupted token map
$`\bm{F}`$, inference applies the same model iteratively. $`\bm{F}_{k}`$
denotes the input token map at refinement step $`k`$. Starting from a
uniformly sampled target token map
$`\bm{F}_{0}=\bm{Y}_{\mathrm{rand}}`$, GRN predicts
$`\widehat{\bm{Y}}_{k+1}`$ from $`\bm{F}_{k}`$ at refinement step $`k`$.
A binary selection map $`\bm{S}_{k+1}`$ is sampled using retention ratio
$`l_{k+1}`$ to construct the next input:

```math
\displaystyle\bm{F}_{k+1} \displaystyle=\bm{S}_{k+1}\odot\widehat{\bm{Y}}_{k+1}+(\bm{1}-\bm{S}_{k+1})\odot\bm{Y}_{\mathrm{rand}}, \tag{7}
```

```math
\displaystyle l_{k+1} \displaystyle=\rho\left[1-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\tau_{k+1}\right)\right],
```

where $`\rho=0.999`$ and $`\tau_{k+1}=\min\{(k+1)/(K-1),1\}`$ denotes
the normalized refinement progress over $`K`$ steps. Predictions not
selected by $`\bm{S}_{k+1}`$ are replaced by $`\bm{Y}_{\mathrm{rand}}`$
and re-predicted at the next step, allowing earlier errors to be
corrected. After $`K`$ refinement steps, the final prediction
$`\widehat{\bm{Y}}_{K}`$ is returned.

## 3 Related Works

### 3.1 Masked diffusion models for TTS

Masked diffusion models (MDMs) \[3\] extend diffusion modeling to
discrete data spaces and have been applied to image \[6\] and text
generation \[35, 26\]. Unlike continuous diffusion models that recover
data by progressively denoising Gaussian noise, MDMs define the
denoising process by revealing masked tokens. Although the denoising
process remains sequential, inference latency depends on the number of
denoising steps rather than on the output sequence length in AR sequence
modeling.

MaskGIT \[6\] is a foundational MDM-based work for image generation,
which utilizes a bidirectional Transformer to predict masked tokens and
determines a subset of high-confidence predictions to keep at each
iteration. MaskGCT \[34\] extends this paradigm to the zero-shot
text-to-speech domain through text-to-semantic and semantic-to-acoustic
generation, implemented as two MDM-based modules in a two-stage cascaded
system. Earlier two-stage TTS systems \[5\] applied MDMs only to the
semantic-to-acoustic module, while retaining an AR text-to-semantic
module that can still lead to unreliable and high latency generation.
However the accumulation of errors caused by eq. 2 have been shown to
degrade the quality of audio generation. To alleviate this issue,
subsequent work \[12\] simplified the original two-stage pipeline into a
single-stage architecture using a bidirectional Transformer \[32\].
OmniVoice \[40\] further formulated zero-shot TTS as a single-stage MDM
that maps text directly to acoustic tokens. It was initialized from a
pre-trained AR large language model (LLM) \[31\] and adopted
full-codebook random masking to improve model effectiveness, training
efficiency, and speech intelligibility.

### 3.2 Accumulationg errors analysis for masked diffusion models

Although MDM-based TTS models can theoretically complete inference in a
single step, multi-step iterative decoding is typically used in practice
to balance speech quality and latency. Thus, MDM-based TTS models still
suffer from error propagation during inference, similar to exposure bias
in AR modeling. Specifically, tokens cannot be modified after they are
unmasked, even if errors have been introduced. To alleviate error
accumulation, several error-correction methods have been explored for
MDMs \[11, 33, 38\] and related applications, such as visual
synthesis \[21, 15\]. ReMDM \[33\] revises tokens generated during
prediction through additional correction steps, providing a
training-free self-correction mechanism. Other methods \[19, 10, 21,
11\] are trained to predict either the positions of incorrect tokens or
the correct tokens themselves using additional heads or modules. In
contrast to GRN \[15\] and Progressive Self-Correction \[11\], no such
error correction investigation has been conducted in TTS and this is
what this paper is aiming to accomplish.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental setup

#### Dataset

For the ablation studies, we train on LibriTTS-R \[20\] (585 hours of
English at 24 kHz) and the premium subset of WenetSpeech4TTS \[23\] (945
hours of Mandarin at 16 kHz). The primary model is trained on the
English and Mandarin subsets of Emilia \[17\], containing 46.8k and
49.9k hours of speech, respectively.

We evaluate Revise-TTS on the LibriSpeech-PC \[24\] test-clean set \[9\]
and the bilingual benchmark introduced by Seed-TTS \[2\]. For objective
evaluation, these testing sets contain 2,215 English samples and 2,020
Mandarin samples in total.

#### Implementation

Revise-TTS follows OmniVoice \[40\] as the backbone and uses an XCodec
2.0 \[37\] speech tokenizer with either FSQ \[25\] or HBQ \[16\]
quantization.

#### Training and inference

For the ablation study and primary model, we train on 4 and 16 NVIDIA
A100 GPUs with batch sizes of 1.8k and 6.5k seconds (20,480 tokens per
GPU) for 100k and 250k steps (one epoch), respectively. We use the AdamW
optimizer with $`\beta_{1}=0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}=0.999`$,
$`\epsilon=10^{-8}`$, and a learning rate of $`10^{-4}`$. Inference uses
32 iterative refinement steps.

#### Metrics

Objective metrics include speaker similarity, intelligibility, and
naturalness. Speaker similarity is the cosine similarity between
WavLM-based embeddings \[7\] of ground-truth and synthesized speech. We
measure intelligibility using WER for English and CER for Mandarin (both
denoted WER below), with a HuBERT-based ASR model \[18\] for
LibriSpeech-PC test-clean, Paraformer-zh \[13\] for Mandarin Seed-TTS,
and Whisper large-v3 \[27\] for English Seed-TTS. Naturalness is
measured using UTMOS \[28\].

Subjective evaluation uses CMOS ($`[-3,3]`$) for relative speech-quality
preference and SMOS ($`[0,5]`$) for perceived speaker similarity to the
prompt audio. We randomly select 10, 10, and 20 audio samples from
LibriSpeech-PC, Seed-TTS EN, and Seed-TTS ZH, respectively, to form the
English and Mandarin test sets. Each sample is evaluated by at least 15
native speakers via Amazon Mechanical Turk.

## 5 Conclusion

This paper proposed Revise-TTS, a single-stage zero-shot TTS framework
that applies GRN non-masking training and overlapping inference to
MDM-based TTS. During training, the model reconstructs the token maps
from mixtures of ground-truth and random tokens. During inference, it
iteratively revises a small proportion of previous predictions.
Revise-TTS also adopts HBQ to provide an explicit coarse-to-fine token
structure and support explicit bit-wise prediction. Experiments show
that HBQ outperforms FSQ under matched codebook. Revise-TTS achieves
competitive intelligibility and naturalness, although speaker similarity
remains improvement.
