###### Abstract

Phonetic error detection (PED) aims to identify phoneme-level
substitutions, deletions, and insertions and is essential for
educational and clinical speech assessment and intervention. Despite
recent advances in speech models for phoneme recognition, their ability
to reliably detect fine-grained phonetic errors remains unclear because
of a lack of high-quality evaluation data. To address this gap, we
introduce PEDBench, the first PED benchmark spanning a continuum from
natural to controlled speech. It comprises real-world recordings of
spoken errors made by children (N=334), scripted error productions by
human actors (N=1,188), and synthetic error productions generated using
phone-conditioned text-to-speech (N=1,083). All phonetic annotations
were transcribed and verified by two phonetically trained annotators.
Our evaluation of 13 state-of-the-art speech models shows that they fail
to reliably detect phonetic errors, even under controlled conditions
with acted and synthetic speech, where acoustic variability is reduced.
The models also exhibit low precision, frequently misclassifying
correctly pronounced phonemes as errors. Our speech-language models,
fine-tuned on synthetic data, outperform commercial and open-source
baselines on word-level PED, although a sim-to-real domain gap remains.
We plan to publicly release our code and recipes, along with our acted
and synthetic datasets, to support the development of more accurate PED
systems.

## 1 Introduction

Automatic phoneme-level pronunciation assessment aims to detect phonetic
errors in speech, with applications ranging from literacy education to
clinical speech-language pathology. The need for scalable assessment and
support is substantial. Recent national assessment results show that 40%
of U.S. fourth-grade students perform below basic level in reading U.S.
Department of Education, Institute of Education Sciences (2025). In
parallel, a national survey found that 1 in 13 U.S. children ages 3–17
experienced a voice, speech, or language disorder during the preceding
12 months, yet more than 40% of these children received no treatment
during that period Black et al. (2015). Prior work attributes these
gaps, in part, to limited access to accurate early assessment and
affordable, effective intervention Morgan et al. (2016). Speech models
that reliably detect phonetic errors could enable tools that expand
access to early screening and supplement educational and clinical
interventions.

Figure 1: Overview of the inference and evaluation pipeline for phonetic
error detection.

A substantial body of work has investigated PED and related tasks,
including mispronunciation detection and diagnosis (MDD) Lounis et al.
(2024), disfluency diagnosis Lian et al. (2024), and automatic speech
assessment El Kheir et al. (2023). Nevertheless, whether
state-of-the-art (SOTA) speech models can detect phonetic errors
reliably enough for real-world deployment remains unclear. We identify
three key limitations that hinder progress. First, inconsistent
ground-truth annotations: our analysis reveals inconsistencies in human
phoneme transcriptions in existing PED datasets, undermining the
reliability and validity of evaluations. Second, limited evaluation of
SOTA models: prior work has not yet evaluated SOTA general-purpose
phoneme recognition models and speech-language models for PED, while
many specialized PED models remain closed-source, limiting their
downstream use. Third, inadequate evaluation metrics: commonly reported
metrics, including phoneme and phoneme-feature error rates for PR and
precision, recall, and F1 scores for MDD, may not adequately
characterize a model’s ability to detect phonetic errors.

To address these limitations, we introduce PEDBench, the first benchmark
for systematically evaluating state-of-the-art (SOTA) speech models on
PED. Our contributions are threefold:

1.  1.  Novel PED datasets with verified ground-truth transcriptions: We
        curate three novel datasets: 1) natural child speech with errors; 2)
        scripted error productions by adult actors; and 3) synthetic adult
        speech with errors. Together, these datasets span a continuum from
        naturalistic, real-world speech to controlled, simulated speech,
        enabling evaluation across varying levels of acoustic variability
        and task difficulty. All phoneme-level transcriptions are
        independently produced or verified by two phonetically trained
        annotators.
2.  2.  Evaluation of SOTA speech models: We evaluate 8 specialized phoneme
        recognition (PR) models, 1 specialized commercial PED model, 2
        commercial speech-language models (SLMs), and 2 open-source SLMs on
        public adult and child speech datasets and our novel PED datasets.
        We report both PED and phoneme recognition metrics, including our
        proposed diagnosis-weighted precision, recall, and F1 scores, which
        require both correct error detection and correct diagnosis rather
        than detection alone. Our results show that SOTA speech models still
        fail to detect even simple phonetic errors from word-level speech,
        under controlled conditions without noise or other sources of
        acoustic variability.
3.  3.  Supervised fine-tuning of SLMs with synthetic speech for PED: We
        show that LoRA fine-tuning of Qwen2.5-Omni-7B on TIMIT provides a
        strong baseline for PED. We further demonstrate that training with
        synthetic speech with errors generated from phoneme-controlled TTS
        further improves Qwen2.5-Omni-7B’s performance, enabling it to
        outperform all evaluated baseline models. These results highlight
        the potential of synthetic speech to scale training data for robust
        PED.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Phonetic Error Detection

Prior work has investigated phonetic error detection (PED) in contexts
such as mispronunciation detection and diagnosis (MDD) Lounis et al.
(2024), disfluency diagnosis Lian et al. (2024), and automatic speech
assessment El Kheir et al. (2023). Most prior studies formulate PED as a
phoneme recognition problem Leung et al. (2019): a phoneme recognizer is
trained using connectionist temporal classification (CTC) loss, and the
predicted phoneme sequence is aligned with the canonical sequence to
identify errors. More recently, Zhou et al. (2025) proposed a multi-task
objective that incorporates phoneme similarity, while Li et al. (2026)
introduced the Kids-Weighted Finite-State Transducer (K-WFST). However,
these recent specialized PED models Zhou et al. (2025); Li et al. (2026)
remain closed-source. More broadly, PED research remains fragmented and
lacks a standardized benchmark for measuring progress.

### 2.2 State-of-the-art Phoneme Speech Models

Recent advances in speech processing have led to substantial
improvements in phoneme recognition Bharadwaj et al. (2026b). However,
these state-of-the-art specialized phoneme recognition (PR) models have
not yet been systematically evaluated for PED. W2V2P-XLSR53 adapts
self-supervised wav2vec 2.0 models through pretraining and fine-tuning
on diverse data mixtures Xu et al. (2021). ZIPA models Zhu et al. (2025)
use encoder–CTC architectures trained from scratch on multilingual data
with consistency-regularized CTC (CR-CTC) loss. More recently, Lavechin
et al. (2026) introduced BabAR, a phoneme recognition model both
pre-trained and fine-tuned on multi-lingual child speech.

Speech-language models (SLMs) are promising candidates for downstream
PED applications such as reading instruction. Recent work Bharadwaj et
al. (2026b) has shown that both commercial and open-source SLMs achieve
competitive PFERs on public phoneme recognition benchmarks, although
they still lag behind specialized PR models. Liu et al. (2026) explored
training SLMs with a Whisper audio encoder and a language-model decoder
for word-level mispronunciation detection. However, the PED capabilities
of state-of-the-art SLMs remain underexplored.

## 3 PEDBench

As shown in Figure 1, PEDBench aims to establish the first standardized
benchmark for evaluating state-of-the-art (SOTA) speech models on
phonetic error detection (PED). The benchmark addresses a central
question: Can SOTA speech models detect phonetic errors in controlled
and real-world settings? This section describes the details of the
benchmark design.

### 3.1 Benchmarked Datasets

#### 3.1.1 Public Datasets

We included two of the most commonly used public benchmarks in the PED
and MDD literature: L2-ARCTIC-Perceived (N=3599) (Zhao et al., 2018) and
CMU-Kids (N=5180) (Mostow, 1996). More details can be found in
Appendix A.1.

#### 3.1.2 Our Novel PED Datasets

Prior work has shown that phoneme production in real-world speech can be
ambiguous. As illustrated in Figure 4, speech sounds may fall near the
phonetic boundary between two phoneme categories, leading to
inconsistent transcriptions across human annotators and compromising
evaluation validity. To reduce this annotation inconsistency, we adopt
two approaches: (1) generating speech with phonetic errors using
phoneme-controlled text-to-speech (TTS) models, and (2) recruiting
native speakers to intentionally produce phonetic errors by following a
script. For our real-world child speech data, we retain only samples for
which two independent annotators provide identical phoneme
transcriptions. Together, these efforts yield 3 novel PED datasets that
enable the evaluation of SOTA speech models’ phonetic error detection
performance in both controlled and real-world settings.

##### Synthetic Speech Dataset.

We generated synthetic speech data by embedding one phonetic
substitution or deletion error into target words and synthesizing them
in words, sentences, and passages using Amazon Polly text-to-speech
(TTS), with pronunciation controlled through SSML \<phoneme\> tags. As
shown in Table 4, we first identified two substitution errors for each
phoneme and included the deletion error for each consonant. For
consonants (N=22), we selected errors in consultation with a
speech-language pathologist (SLP) collaborator. For vowels (N=14), we
selected errors based on best available evidences we could find from
prior speech-language pathology and literacy research Roepke and
Brosseau-Lapré (2021); Cebrian et al. (2021); Chung et al. (2014). We
used 60 target words drawn from GFTA-3, a widely used assessment of
speech-sound production (Goldman and Fristoe, 1969).

After generation, phonetically trained annotators reviewed the synthetic
recordings in two independent rounds and flagged those that did not
match their intended phoneme transcriptions. After excluding all flagged
recordings, 1,141 recordings remained: 396 isolated words, 375
sentences, and 370 passages. From these recordings, we retained 361
complete matched sets, each containing an isolated-word recording and
corresponding sentence and passage recordings with the same target word
and phonetic error. The final synthetic evaluation dataset therefore
comprised 1,083 utterances. This dataset enables us to evaluate models
on objectively defined phonetic errors across different speech lengths
while holding the target word and error constant. Separately, we also
denote the 396 isolated synthetic words data as synthetic word (SW)
training data used for supervised fine-tuning experiments.

##### Acted Speech Dataset.

To bridge the gap between naturalistic and synthetic speech, we
recruited three adult native English speakers with training in phonetics
and asked them to intentionally produce scripted phonetic errors. The
script matched the 396 SW training data in both target words and
intended phonetic errors. After each speaker completed and confirmed
their recordings, one of the other speakers reviewed and flagged any
deviations from the script. Speakers re-recorded the flagged items until
no recordings were flagged. The resulting acted-speech evaluation
dataset comprised 1,188 utterances and also allows us to evaluate models
on objectively defined phonetic errors, while serving as an intermediate
evaluation condition between authentic child speech and fully synthetic
speech.

##### Child Speech Dataset.

We also collected child speech containing naturally occurring phonetic
errors. We elicited word- and sentence-level speech from 199 typically
developing Spanish–English bilingual children aged 3–7 years using a
picture-naming task with 33 target words and three target sentences.
Following data collection, two phonetically trained annotators
independently transcribed 3,574 utterances at the phoneme level. We
retained only recordings that both annotators identified as containing
phonetic errors and for which they agreed exactly on the realized
phoneme sequence. The resulting child-speech evaluation dataset
comprised 334 utterances with high-confidence error labels from 147
children: 323 word recordings and 11 sentence recordings. In addition,
we also identified all the error types in this child-speech evaluation
dataset, and generated matching synthetic speech data (N=154). After
combining it with the SC training dataset described in Section 3.1.2, we
have the synthetic combined (SC) training dataset (N=550). More details
can be found in Appendix F.3

### 3.2 Evaluation Pipeline

Figure 1 illustrates the phonetic error detection (PED) pipeline used in
PEDBench, which follows the standard approach in the mispronunciation
detection and diagnosis (MDD) literature. For each speech recording, we
obtain the canonical phoneme sequence $`C`$ by applying a
grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) conversion to its textual transcript. Human
annotators provide the realized phoneme sequence $`U`$, while the speech
model processes the recording to produce the predicted phoneme sequence
$`\hat{U}`$. Our pipeline is implemented on top of the PRiSM toolkit
Bharadwaj et al. (2026b), reusing its inference recipes and
phonetic-feature distance, and extending it with the CMU-39 projection
(Appendix E), MDD alignment (Appendix D), and PED metrics described
below.

A challenge in prior work is that datasets and speech models use
different phoneme inventories. Multilingual models, in particular, may
emit IPA symbols outside the English phoneme inventory. PEDBench
therefore standardizes all three sequences by projecting them onto the
39-phoneme inventory of the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary Weide (1998),
represented in IPA, yielding $`C^{39}`$, $`U^{39}`$, and
$`\hat{U}^{39}`$. Further details of phoneme standardization are
provided in Appendix E. Because these sequences generally differ in
length, PEDBench first aligns them on a common grid following the
hierarchical MDD evaluation framework (Li et al., 2017). Implementation
details are provided in Appendix D.

### 3.3 Evaluation Metrics

##### Phoneme-recognition metrics.

PEDBench reports the standard phoneme error rate (PER) and phoneme
feature error rates (PFER). Detailed definitions of these metrics are
provided in the appendix C.

##### PED metrics.

![](arxiv-2610-20830v1--a51b5020a276.figures/figure-2.webp)

Figure 2: Phonetic Error Detection Alignment and Metric Calculation
Rules Gelin et al. (2023).

As illustrated in Figure 2, each position in the alignment grid receives
two correctness labels: canonical versus uttered (whether the phone was
mispronounced) and canonical versus predicted (whether the model
rejected the canonical phone). Together, these labels classify each
position as a true acceptance (TA), false rejection (FR), false
acceptance (FA), or true rejection (TR). Each TR is further classified
as a correct diagnosis (CD) if the predicted phone matches the uttered
phone, or a diagnostic error (DE) otherwise. From these counts, we
compute the false rejection rate (FRR), false acceptance rate (FAR),
overall diagnostic error rate (DER), and _diagnosis-weighted_ (DW)
precision, recall, and F1. Standard MDD precision and recall use
$`\mathrm{TR}`$ in the numerator, crediting a model for detecting an
error even when its diagnosis is incorrect. Our DW variants instead use
the stricter numerator $`\mathrm{CD}`$, awarding credit only when the
model both detects the error and correctly identifies the realized
phone, which is more aligned with real-world use needs.

## 4 Benchmarked Models

PEDBench evaluated 8 specialized phoneme recognition models, 1
specialized PED model, and 4 speech-language models (SLMs).

### 4.1 Specialized Phoneme Recognition Models

We evaluate 8 specialized phoneme-recognition (PR) models from 6 model
families. From the wav2vec 2.0 family, we include its three
top-performing systems: MultiIPA, W2V2P-LV60, and W2V2P-XLSR53 (Xu et
al., 2021). We also include ZIPA-CTC and ZIPA-CTC-NS, two
Zipformer-based recognizers (Zhu et al., 2025). We additionally include
three recently released models: HuPER (Guo et al., 2026),
PhoneticXeus (Bharadwaj et al., 2026a), and BabAR (Lavechin et al.,
2026).

### 4.2 Specialized PED Model

We also evaluated the Azure’s Pronunciation Assessment model Microsoft
(2025), a specialized model designed to assess speech pronunciation on
both the word and phoneme level that can be configured to perform phone
recognition.

### 4.3 Speech-Language Models

Speech-language models (SLMs) are well suited to PED because their
in-context learning capabilities support adaptation to applications with
varying criteria for correct pronunciation. Their ability to generate
text also supports downstream tasks such as reading instruction.

In PEDBench, we evaluate 2 proprietary SLMs, Gemini-3.8-Flash and
GPT-Audio-1.5, and 2 open-weight SLMs, Qwen2.5-Omni-7B (Xu et al., 2025)
and Audio Flamingo 3 (AF3) (Ghosh et al., 2026). We selected these
models for their strong performance on recent audio-language benchmarks.
To adapt Qwen2.5-Omni-7B and AF3 to phoneme recognition, we performed
supervised fine-tuning using the training split of the TIMIT
dataset Garofolo et al. (1993). Further training details are provided in
the appendix F. We prompt each model to transcribe the input audio as a
phoneme sequence using the 39-phoneme inventory of the CMU Pronouncing
Dictionary Weide (1998). The system and user prompts are shown in
Figure 5.

## 5 Fine-Tuning Experiments with Synthetic Data

A core challenge in PED is collecting large-scale real-world training
data covering diverse phonetic errors, particularly rare
ones Macklin-Cordes and Round (2020). Synthetic speech offers a
promising way to scale training data while covering both common and rare
error types. In this work, we ask the following question: Can synthetic
speech data be used to fine-tune speech models for better PED?

To answer this question, we fine-tuned three models—W2V2P-XLSR53,
Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, and Audio Flamingo 3 (AF3)—and evaluated them on our
new child-speech and acted-speech datasets. In downstream tasks such as
reading assessment, common speech errors for each target word are often
known. This information can guide the generation of synthetic training
data to improve detection of these errors. As described in
Sections 3.1.2 and 3.1.2, we constructed synthetic word (SW) training
data covering the error types in the acted-speech evaluation dataset,
and synthetic combined (SC) training data additionally covering those in
the child-speech evaluation dataset. We fine-tuned each model separately
on SW and SC, adding TIMIT to the training mixtures for Qwen2.5-Omni-7B
and AF3. Further implementation details are provided in Section F.

As shown in Table 1, we evaluated all benchmarked models on L2-ARCTIC
and CMU-Kids. Commercial models were excluded from CMU-Kids evaluation
due to licensing requirements. This evaluation shows that even the
best-performing models still fail to reliably detect phonetic errors in
real-world, sentence-level speech, achieving only 34.32 F1 with
TIMIT-fine-tuned Qwen2.5-Omni-7B on L2-ARCTIC and 54.55 F1 with
ZIPA-CTC-NS on CMU-Kids. In addition, PFER may not adequately reflect
PED performance. Although ZIPA-CTC-NS achieved an impressive PFER of
7.86 on CMU-Kids, its F1 of 54.55 remains unsatisfactory.

We also investigated whether dataset limitations may explain the poor
performance. Qualitative inspection of both datasets suggested that some
speech sounds in accented speech in L2-ARCTIC and child speech in
CMU-Kids can be ambiguous and difficult to transcribe consistently,
particularly vowels and voiceless consonants. To examine annotation
consistency, we asked our phonetically trained annotators to reannotate
a random sample of 100 utterances from each dataset. Evaluated against
the original annotations, these human reannotations yielded F1 scores of
42.25 on L2-ARCTIC and 66.22 on CMU-Kids. This disagreement suggests
that annotation uncertainty may also contribute to the low measured
performance. These findings motivate curating PED datasets with more
clearly defined and consistently verified phoneme transcriptions.

### 6.2 Can SOTA speech models detect phonetic errors in our novel PED datasets?

As shown in Figure 2, the best-performing PR model (BabAR: 59.51 F1),
specialized PED model (Azure: 49.96 F1), and SLM (Gemini: 58.26 F1)
still fail to reliably detect phonetic errors in our word-level
child-speech dataset, despite its transcriptions being agreed upon by
two independent annotators. For our acted dataset, which is more
controlled and designed to reduce ambiguity in phoneme realization, the
best-performing PR model (ZIPA-CTC: 74.79 F1), specialized PED model
(Azure: 75.98 F1), and SLM (GPT-Audio: 76.17 F1) also fail to perform
PED reliably. Their low precision suggests frequent false rejections.

As shown in Figure 3, even on our synthetic evaluation data, where
speech is generated from specified phoneme sequences to produce
objectively defined phonetic errors, the best-performing models at the
word (Azure: 74.32 F1), sentence (Azure: 39.27 F1), and passage
(ZIPA-CTC: 14.22 F1) levels fail to perform PED reliably. Performance
degrades substantially as audio length increases, while recall remains
stable across levels, suggesting that the degradation is primarily
driven by lower precision and more false rejections.

Results reported in Tables 1, 2, and 3 are all unscripted results to
fairly compare between models. Specialized PED and SLMs can also be
evaluated in the scripted setting where canonical phoneme sequence can
also be used as the model input. As shown in Figure 6, the results are
mixed, and canonical scripting did not consistently help improve models;
more detailed discussion can be found in Appendix G.1.

![](arxiv-2610-20830v1--a51b5020a276.figures/inline-2.svg)

### 6.3 Can synthetic data help fine-tune speech models for better PED?

As shown in Table 2, our LoRA fine-tuned SLMs significantly outperformed
other model candidates. In our acted dataset, the fine-tuned Qwen
achieved 90.06 F1 and the fine-tuned AF3 achieved 88.83 F1 , which is
better than the best performing SLM model (GPT-Audio: 76.17 F1),
specialized PED (Azure: 75.98 F1) and PR model (ZIPA-CTC: 74.79 F1). In
our child dataset, the fine-tuned AF3 achieved 63.82 F1 and the
fine-tuned Qwen achieved 63.47 F1, outperforming the best performing SLM
model (Gemini: 58.26), specialized PED (Azure: 49.96 F1) and PR model
(BabAR: 59.51 F1). Although the fine-tuned Qwen and AF3 models performed
strongly on acted speech, their performance on child speech remains
unsatisfactory. This may reflect the distribution shift between the
adult speech used for training (TIMIT + SC) and the child speech used
for evaluation. These results demonstrate the promise of using synthetic
data to scale quantity and diversity of training data. However, the
sim-to-real and adult-to-child gap may still hinder model
generalization.

### 6.4 Error Analysis

![](arxiv-2610-20830v1--a51b5020a276.figures/figure-3.webp)

Figure 3: Error profiles for four representative models on Authentic
Child Speech and Acted Speech.

To identify failure modes obscured by aggregate metrics, we analyze four
representative models: Qwen2.5-Omni-7B fine-tuned on TIMIT and synthetic
data, W2V2P-XLSR53 for specialized phoneme recognition, BabAR for
child-speech models, and Gemini for commercial SLMs. We categorize each
aligned realized phone as correctly predicted, substituted, or deleted,
and separately report insertions per 1,000 realized phones (Figure 3).
On acted speech, Qwen fine-tuned on TIMIT and SW data achieves the
lowest substitution, deletion, and insertion rates among these four
models. BabAR, pretrained and fine-tuned exclusively on child speech,
performs substantially worse on acted adult speech but remains
competitive on our child-speech dataset. Phone-level confusion matrices
and insertion inventories for all four models are provided in
Appendix H. The models struggle to distinguish acoustically similar
sounds, including /T/–/f/, /S/–/tS/, /O/–/A/, and /eI/–/I/. These
confusions motivate targeted synthetic training data to help models
learn the phonetic boundaries between these sounds. The difficulty of
distinguishing /T/ from /f/ using audio alone also motivates future work
on audio-visual speech recognition. We hypothesize that tongue and mouth
movements may provide noise-invariant cues for robust PED.

## 7 Conclusion

In this work, we introduced PEDBench, the first standardized benchmark
for evaluating SOTA speech models on phonetic error detection. We found
that all SOTA speech models still fail to reliably detect phonetic
errors across word- and sentence-level speech in both real-world and
highly controlled settings. We explored synthetic data for fine-tuning
SLMs and demonstrated their potential to scale PED training data,
especially for rare errors that are difficult to collect in the real
world. This work lays the groundwork for more accurate evaluation and
effective training of PED models toward reliable deployment in
real-world applications.

## 8 Limitations

While we believe PEDBench can support the future evaluation and
development of PED systems, it has the following limitations:

##### Evaluation Goals.

PEDBench aims to expose the limitations of SOTA speech models through
diagnostic datasets with objectively defined phonetic errors. However,
its three new datasets do not yet provide a comprehensive evaluation of
PED. Future work should collect real-world speech covering a broader
range of phonetic errors from speakers with diverse linguistic
backgrounds and speech and language abilities. These expanded datasets
would support more comprehensive model validation before real-world
deployment.

##### Fine-Tuning Claims.

Our results demonstrate the potential of synthetic data generated
through phoneme-controlled TTS to scale training datasets. However, they
do not yet establish that our fine-tuned models can robustly detect
phonetic errors. The current pipeline remains constrained by the ability
of TTS models to reliably produce the intended phonetic errors and
requires human review to exclude inaccurately generated samples. Future
work should develop TTS models that generate these errors more
accurately and provide finer control over speech production, acoustic
variability, and speaker identity.

##### Phoneme Transcription.

Despite our best efforts to ensure annotation quality through
independent transcription or verification by two phonetically trained
annotators, some ambiguity and subjectivity may remain. Our acted and
synthetic speech datasets help reduce this ambiguity by introducing
phonetic errors under controlled conditions, but they may not eliminate
it entirely.

##### Modality.

The models evaluated in this work use only audio input; we have not yet
explored audio-visual input. Incorporating visual information may help
models distinguish acoustically similar phonemes, such as /T/ and /f/,
by capturing differences in visible articulatory movements.

##### Language.

PEDBench focuses on phonetic error detection in English and uses the
CMU-39 phoneme inventory to standardize evaluation. All three new
PEDBench datasets are in English, limiting the benchmark’s applicability
to multilingual settings.

##### Benchmarked Models.

PEDBench evaluates prominent state-of-the-art models across three
categories: specialized phoneme recognizers, specialized PED models, and
speech-language models. However, the evaluation is not exhaustive, and
some available models are not represented in this work.

## 9 Ethical Considerations

For the data, we downloaded publicly available datasets and complied
with their respective licenses. In particular, because the CMU Kids
license prohibits transmitting the data to third parties, we did not
evaluate commercial models on CMU Kids through their APIs. For our new
PED datasets, our university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved
the data collection protocol and data security plan. The protocol
explicitly included consent language permitting the use of the collected
data to evaluate third-party commercial automatic speech recognition
(ASR) models. However, the consent did not permit public release of the
child speech data. Accordingly, only our acted and synthetic datasets
can be released publicly. We plan to retain the child speech dataset as
a private held-out evaluation set for a future PED leaderboard.

For the models, we benchmarked existing speech models and fine-tuned
open-source speech-language models. Upon acceptance of the paper, we
will release our fine-tuned model checkpoints in accordance with the
licenses of the underlying models. Our code builds on the PRiSM toolkit
Bharadwaj et al. (2026b), which in turn follows the
Lightning-Hydra-Template (MIT license). We will release our code under a
license compatible with both and retain the original copyright notices.

### A.2 Spectrum of Ambiguity in Phoneme Production

As shown in Figure 4, natural phoneme production can be ambiguous,
making it difficult for annotators to agree on phoneme transcriptions.

## Appendix C Evaluation Metrics

##### Phoneme Recognition Metrics.

Recent PED work commonly reports phoneme-recognition metrics. Let $`d`$
denote the unit-cost Levenshtein distance and $`|U_{i}^{39}|`$ the
number of reference phonemes in utterance $`i`$. We report the
corpus-level phoneme error rate
$`\mathrm{PER}=\sum_{i}d\bigl(U_{i}^{39},\hat{U}_{i}^{39}\bigr)\,/\,\sum_{i}|U_{i}^{39}|`$
between the uttered and predicted sequences, and, following Bharadwaj et
al. (2026b), the phonetic feature error rate
$`\mathrm{PFER}=\sum_{i}d_{\phi}\bigl(U_{i}^{39},\hat{U}_{i}^{39}\bigr)\,/\,\sum_{i}|U_{i}^{39}|`$,
where $`d_{\phi}`$ is an edit distance whose substitution cost is the
articulatory-feature distance between phonemes. PFER therefore penalizes
confusions between articulatory similar phonemes less than dissimilar
ones.

##### PED Metrics.

PER and PFER aggregate performance over all phone positions, whereas
phonetic errors are sparse; consequently, a model may perform well on
PER/PFER metrics while missing the relatively few errors that matter. We
therefore adopt the hierarchical evaluation framework used in the
mispronunciation detection and diagnosis (MDD) literature (Li et al.,
2017; Daoud and Messaoud, 2025).

```math
\displaystyle\mathrm{FRR}=\frac{\mathrm{FR}}{\mathrm{TA}+\mathrm{FR}},\qquad\mathrm{FAR}=\frac{\mathrm{FA}}{\mathrm{TR}+\mathrm{FA}},
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathrm{DER}=\frac{\mathrm{FR}+\mathrm{FA}+\mathrm{DE}}{\mathrm{TA}+\mathrm{FR}+\mathrm{TR}+\mathrm{FA}},
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathrm{Precision}_{\mathrm{DW}}=\frac{\mathrm{CD}}{\mathrm{TR}+\mathrm{FR}},
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathrm{Recall}_{\mathrm{DW}}=\frac{\mathrm{CD}}{\mathrm{TR}+\mathrm{FA}},
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathrm{F1}_{\mathrm{DW}}=\frac{2\,\mathrm{CD}}{2\,\mathrm{TR}+\mathrm{FR}+\mathrm{FA}}.
```

FRR measures how often correctly pronounced phones are incorrectly
flagged, a failure mode that can undermine user trust in deployment. FAR
measures how often actual phonetic errors are incorrectly accepted as
canonical. DER captures all incorrect detection and diagnosis outcomes:
false rejections, false acceptances, and correctly detected errors
assigned the wrong realized phone.

## Appendix D MDD Alignment and Metrics

We followed the standard MDD literature to align the canonical, uttered
and predicted phoneme sequences Gelin et al. (2023).

For each utterance we align the canonical sequence $`C`$, the perceived
sequence $`U`$, and the model prediction $`P`$. All three are first
mapped to CMU-39 (see Appendix E).

First, we align $`C`$ to $`U`$ by minimum edit distance without looking
at $`P`$. This fixes the mispronunciation events, so every model is
scored against the same events. If two alignments have the same cost, we
select the one with fewer rows and break remaining ties by preferring a
match or substitution, then a deletion, then an insertion.

Second, we place $`P`$ onto these fixed events. The alignment first
minimizes disagreements with $`U`$. Among equally good alignments, it
prefers more exact matches with $`U`$, then fewer extra rows, then more
matches with $`C`$. A predicted phone that fits no event becomes its own
insertion row.

Third, we label each aligned row $`(c,u,p)`$. When the speaker said the
phone correctly ($`c=u`$), the row is a true acceptance (TA) if the
model also outputs $`c`$, and a false rejection (FR) if it outputs
anything else. When the speaker made an error ($`c\neq u`$), the row is
a false acceptance (FA) if the model outputs the canonical phone $`c`$,
a correct diagnosis (CD) if it outputs what the speaker said ($`u`$, or
nothing if the speaker dropped the phone), and a diagnosis error (DE)
otherwise. True rejections are TR $`=`$ CD $`+`$ DE. A phone the model
inserts where nothing was said, row $`(\varepsilon,\varepsilon,p)`$,
counts as FR. We sum these counts over all rows of all utterances.

From these counts we compute the false rejection rate, false acceptance
rate, overall diagnostic error rate, and diagnosis-weighted precision,
recall, and F1 defined in Section 3.3. Standard MDD precision and recall
put TR in the numerator; the diagnosis-weighted versions use CD, so a
model gets credit only when it also names the phone the speaker
produced. PER is the edit distance between $`P`$ and $`U`$ divided by
the length of $`U`$.

Different models output different alphabets, including multilingual IPA,
English IPA, or ARPAbet. To fairly compare them, we map every output to
the same 39-phone inventory of 24 consonants and 15 vowels (see
Table 5). The projection rules were reviewed by reviewers with
linguistic and phonetic trainings. This inventory follows CMU-39 but
writes each phone in IPA, and merges AH/AX into 2 and ER/AXR into @.

The mapping has two steps. Normalization decomposes Unicode, writes
affricates with a tie bar, maps variant symbols to standard ones, and
removes length, stress, and aspiration marks. Projection then removes
spaces and punctuation, and re-reads the string by longest match,
mapping each symbol to one of the 39 phones. Because spaces are removed,
adjacent symbols can merge into one phone (e.g., a i becomes aI). Most
diacritics are ignored and the base phone is kept. Symbols with no
mapping are removed and logged, not counted as errors themselves.

Six symbols depend on their context. Flaps (, ) become t or d, the nasal
flap R̃ becomes t, d, or n, the glottal stop becomes t, and becomes b or
v. For these, we align the sequence to the projected canonical sequence
and use the canonical phone at that position if it is one of the allowed
choices. Otherwise, we use a default (t, n, v; the glottal stop is
dropped).

### F.5 Prompts

Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, Audio Flamingo 3, Gemini, and GPT-Audio receive the
same system prompt and user prompt, and fine-tuning uses the same prompt
as evaluation (see Figure 5). When scripted, a short instruction and the

Qwen2.5-Omni-7B and Audio Flamingo 3 decode greedily with up to 512 new
tokens, GPT-Audio uses temperature 0 with up to 512 output tokens, and
Gemini uses a low thinking level with up to 4,096 output tokens. All
results in the main paper are unscripted unless marked otherwise.

### F.6 Output Parsing

For Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, Audio Flamingo 3, and Gemini, we strip whitespace
and any Markdown code fence around the response and read it as JSON. We
take the value under transcription, or the only value if the object has
a single key. Extra text after the JSON is ignored, but text before it
invalidates the response. A response with no JSON is kept only if it is
a plain string of phones, and a response cut off partway through the
JSON is recovered when possible. Audio Flamingo 3 sometimes answers in
ARPAbet, which we convert to IPA. Gemini and GPT-Audio are called with
structured output, which forces a JSON answer. If a response still
cannot be parsed, we score it as an empty prediction, so every reference
phone counts as deleted. Phones outside the CMU-39 set are not format
errors. They go through the projection in Appendix E, and anything that
cannot be mapped is dropped (see Table 9 for examples).

## Appendix G Additional Experiments

### G.1 Scripted vs Unscripted Results

We also conducted experiments to assess whether providing a canonical
script improves or degrades speech model performance. Intuitively, the
script may constrain predictions and reduce false rejections, which
appear to be a common issue for these models. However, it may also bias
predictions toward the canonical pronunciation, making models more
lenient and less sensitive to phonetic errors. The scripted and
unscripted prompts are shown in Figure 5.

As shown in Figure 6, the results were mixed. On the authentic
evaluation data, providing the script reduced Azure’s performance
(-11.82) but improved performance for Gemini (+9.38), GPT-Audio (+6.19),
and Qwen (+5.14). The scripted Qwen model fine-tuned on TIMIT and
synthetic combined (SC) data scored 68.61, outperforming scripted
Gemini-3.8-Flash (67.64).

On the acted evaluation data, providing the script reduced performance
for Azure (-14.37), GPT-Audio-1.5 (-24.06), and our fine-tuned Qwen
model (-10.42). These results suggest that consistently integrating
information from the canonical script and the audio remains a challenge
requiring further investigation. Nevertheless, the unscripted Qwen model
fine-tuned on TIMIT and synthetic word (SW) training data scored 90.06,
outperforming scripted Gemini-3.8-Flash (82.19).

### G.2 Analysis Focused on Target Words

As shown in Table 10, we also evaluated PED performance within the
target word span, rather than across the entire sentence or passage, to
assess whether detection of the embedded error changes across word,
sentence, and passage conditions. These metrics include insertions at
both boundaries of the target word.

We found that specialized PR models tend to perform better as audio
length increases. Specialized PED models, GPT-Audio-1.5, and our Qwen
models maintain relatively stable performance across audio lengths.
However, both Gemini-3.8-Flash and AF3 show performance degradation with
longer context, particularly Gemini-3.8-Flash. These results suggest
that differences in model architecture and pretraining data mixtures may
influence how well SLMs perform PED on longer audio.

## Appendix H Phone-Level Error Analysis

For phone-level error analysis, we pick four representative models and
analyze their runs on Authentic Child Speech and Acted Speech. We choose
our best fine-tuned model (Qwen2.5-Omni-7B + TIMIT+SC on Authentic and +
TIMIT+SW on Acted) to represent open-weight speech-language models,
W2V2P-XLSR53 to represent open-weight phone recognition models, BabAR to
represent child-speech models, and Gemini-3.8-Flash to represent
proprietary models. Figures 7–10 show consonant and vowel confusion
matrices. Rows are perceived phones and columns are predicted phones,
and each row is normalized by the number of times the phone was
perceived ($`n`$). The \<DEL\> column shows how often the phone was
missed, and the \<INS\> column shows how often the model inserted that
phone where nothing was said, as a share of the same $`n`$; it is not
part of the row’s 100%.

### H.1 Authentic Child Speech

Qwen+TIMIT+SC gets 77.9% of the 1,502 perceived phones right (17.1%
substituted, 5.0% deleted, 41 insertions per 1k phones). Gemini follows
with 74.8% (20.5% substituted), then BabAR with 71.4% (11.5% deleted)
and W2V2P-XLSR53 with 58.9% (33.4% substituted). All four models often
miss : 40–60% of its 40 occurrences are deleted. They also confuse 2
with nearby vowels. Gemini writes @ for 44 of 186, while W2V2P-XLSR53
and BabAR write A (42 and 32). Three of the four models hear t as t͡S (13
to 28 of 177), whereas BabAR does this only 5 times. Gemini and
W2V2P-XLSR53 show more insertions (58 and 94 per 1k phones), while Qwen
most often inserts l.

### H.2 Acted Speech

Qwen+TIMIT+SW gets 96.7% of the 5,088 phones right, Gemini 89.9%, and
W2V2P-XLSR53 87.0%. Most of their remaining errors are vowel
substitutions. All three often write I for 2 (14 to 30 of 495) and E for
I (16 to 27 of 219), and all three hear T as f (12 to 20 of 54).
W2V2P-XLSR53 also misses l and (56 of 297 and 52 of 279). BabAR gets
only 28.8% right because it deletes 64.3% of phones. BabAR is built to
transcribe young children and returns no output for 501 of the 1,188
adult Acted utterances (42%). On 80 of these recordings, raising the
pitch by seven semitones brought its output back for all but 8%, so we
read its Acted results as a domain mismatch rather than poor phone
recognition.
