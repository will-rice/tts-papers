###### Abstract

Chunk-interleaved full-duplex speech language models listen and speak in
fixed time chunks, and each chunk must be processed before the next one
arrives. Their workload is asymmetric across chunks: listen chunks leave
compute idle while awaiting audio, and speak and tool-call chunks
require serial generation, which delays responses and tool calls. Using
the idle compute for the next chunk requires decoding it before its
audio arrives, under an assumed input. Taking silence as the assumed
input and comparing the output with the output under real audio on four
models, we observe two properties. First, the next audio chunk does not
change the output in 95.3% of chunks. Second, 88% of changes begin at
the first output token. Building on these properties, we propose
Speculative Listening, a training-free, lossless framework for
input-time self-speculative decoding. During idle time, the model drafts
the next chunk under silence and, for changes at the first token, a
second branch from the runner-up first token. It keeps all drafts in a
cross-chunk hypothesis pool and verifies them when the audio arrives. On
our eight-task benchmark TickTock-Bench, Speculative Listening speeds up
speak chunks by 2.6–2.8$`\times`$ and tool-call chunks by up to
23.4$`\times`$. On average, it reduces the decoding delay before a
response starts by 73%, to 74–241 ms, close to the 200 ms gap between
human turns, and the maximum lag behind real time by 94.4%.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Decoding in a chunk-interleaved full-duplex speech language
model. (a) Autoregressive decoding leaves compute idle while it waits

Speculative Listening drafts the next chunk under silence during the
idle window and verifies the whole draft in one forward pass when the

Full-duplex speech language models listen and speak at the same
time (Défossez et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2026; Thinking Machines Lab,
2026). A widely adopted design is the chunk-interleaved
architecture (Zhang et al., 2024b; Cui et al., 2026; Thinking Machines
Lab, 2026; Orantqing et al., 2026; Ant Group, 2026; Zhang et al., 2026),
which divides a streaming conversation into fixed time chunks
(Figure 1). In each chunk, the model processes the newly arrived audio
and decodes its output, which may keep listening, speak, or call a
tool (Orantqing et al., 2026; Ant Group, 2026). The audio of the next
chunk arrives in real time, and the chunk duration acts as a hard
deadline (Thinking Machines Lab, 2026): a chunk that takes longer to
decode delays the following chunks, and the conversation lags behind
real time. The model then processes the user’s speech late, and its
responses become stale in the ongoing interaction.

Profiling four chunk-interleaved models chunk by chunk (Figure 3a), we
find their workload asymmetric across chunks. Listen chunks only process
the newly arrived audio and leave compute idle for 70–89% of the chunk
duration while awaiting the next audio chunk (Figure 1a). We call this
unused time the _idle window_. Speak and tool-call chunks require serial
generation, and its length grows with the output: a long output, such as
a tool call, takes tens of serial forward passes (Figure 4a), each bound
by the memory bandwidth of loading model weights and the KV
cache (Shazeer, 2019). Tool-call chunks miss the deadline, and a model
with a written channel decoded alongside speech (Zhang et al., 2026)
misses it on 95% of speak chunks. Decoding also delays the start of each
response: under AR, it adds 417–1089 ms on average. Human speakers
respond about 200 ms after the other speaker ends (Levinson and
Torreira, 2015).

Prior work addresses neighbouring parts of this problem. Lossless
speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023)
accelerates serial generation with draft models (Li et al., 2024; Cai et
al., 2024) or context lookup (Saxena, 2023; Luo et al., 2025) once the
input is available. Recent speech systems compute during listening
windows by training the model to emit reasoning tokens (Wu et al., 2026)
or by pre-generating candidate responses in turn-based cascaded
pipelines (Li and Grover, 2025). In end-to-end chunk-interleaved models,
the idle window has not yet been used to decode the output of the next
chunk.

Using the idle window requires decoding a chunk before its audio
arrives, that is, under an assumed input. We take silence as the
simplest assumption, which we call the _silence hypothesis_, and at
every chunk across four models decode the output twice: once with the
real audio and once with the audio replaced by silence (Section 3.2).
Comparing the two outputs, we observe two properties (Figure 3b,c):

1.  1.  The next audio chunk usually does not change the output. The output
        under silence matches the real-audio output token for token in 95.3%
        of chunks pooled over the four models.
2.  2.  When the output changes, it diverges at the first token. Pooled over
        the four models, 88% of divergences occur at the first output token,
        where most of the models decide to stay silent or start responding.

Building on these properties, we propose _Speculative Listening_, a
training-free and lossless framework for _input-time self-speculative
decoding_ (Figure 1b and Figure 2). During the idle window, the target
model drafts the upcoming chunk under the silence hypothesis and keeps
the drafts of all earlier chunks in a *cross-chunk hypothesis pool* (Fu
et al., 2024). A second draft branch, started from the runner-up first
token, covers divergences at the first token. When the real audio
arrives, one forward pass processes it and verifies the whole draft. If
the output departs from the draft, later forward passes continue from a
matching draft in the pool, such as the runner-up branch. Post-audio
computation then grows only with changed tokens (Section 3.3).

We also introduce _TickTock-Bench_, a Spec-Bench-inspired (Xia et al., 2024) unified benchmark and replay harness for full-duplex decoding
under chunk deadlines. It covers eight reasoning, conversation, and
agentic tasks (Section 4). Experiments on four models show that
Speculative Listening speeds up speak chunks by 2.6–2.8$`\times`$ on
average and tool-call chunks by up to 23.4$`\times`$ (Figure 6b),
reduces the maximum lag behind real time by 94.4% on average (Table 3),
and shortens the decoding delay before a response starts by 73% on
average, to 74–241 ms (Section 5.4).

Our contributions are three-fold:

- •
  We identify two properties of chunk outputs in chunk-interleaved
  full-duplex models: the next audio chunk usually does not change the
  output, and changes concentrate at the first output token.
- •
  We propose Speculative Listening, a training-free and lossless
  decoding framework whose post-audio computation grows only with
  changed tokens, and which stays faster than autoregressive decoding
  under stochastic sampling and multi-session serving.
- •
  We introduce TickTock-Bench, a benchmark and replay harness for
  decoding in chunk-interleaved full-duplex models, which enables a
  comparison of decoding methods under the same chunk deadlines.

## 2 Related Work

#### Full-duplex speech language models.

They listen and speak at the same time. Moshi (Défossez et al., 2024)
modeled the user’s speech and its own as parallel streams.
Chunk-interleaved models arranged input and output in one causal
sequence: Zhang et al. (2024b) split the dialogue into time slices,
OmniFlatten (Zhang et al., 2025) flattened the streams into a single
sequence, and MiniCPM-o 4.5 (Cui et al., 2026) and the interaction model
of Thinking Machines Lab (2026) processed streaming dialogue in
fixed-duration chunks. Gander (Orantqing et al., 2026) and
Realtime-Venus (Ant Group, 2026) added tool calls and external
delegation to the stream, and LWS (Zhang et al., 2026) added a written
channel that can be decoded in the same chunk as speech.

#### Speculative decoding.

Speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023) drafted
several tokens cheaply and verified them in one forward pass of the
target model, which preserves its output distribution. Drafts came from
a smaller model, from heads trained on the target’s hidden states as in
Medusa (Cai et al., 2024) and EAGLE (Li et al., 2024), or from a subset
of the target model’s own layers, as in Draft & Verify (Zhang et al.,
2024a) and Kangaroo (Liu et al., 2024). Training-free methods drafted
without extra parameters: prompt lookup decoding (PLD) (Saxena, 2023)
matched $`n`$-grams in the context, Token Recycling (Luo et al., 2025)
reused candidate tokens from earlier decoding steps, and lookahead
decoding (Fu et al., 2024) collected $`n`$-grams from Jacobi iterations
in a pool. Spec-Bench (Xia et al., 2024) compared these methods under a
unified protocol for text generation. In spoken dialogue, speculative
generation was used to start responses earlier: RelayS2S (Mai and Liang, 2026) let a fast duplex model draft the opening of a response that a
cascaded pipeline can continue, and SpecSpeak (Jiang et al., 2026)
started speech synthesis from draft tokens while the target model
verified them. These methods draft once the input is available;
Speculative Listening is a training-free self-speculative method that
drafts the output of a chunk before its audio arrives.

#### Computing ahead of the input.

Human speakers plan their response while still listening, which keeps
the gap between turns near 200 ms (Levinson and Torreira, 2015). Speech
systems have used the listening time in several ways. Chronological
Thinking (Wu et al., 2026) trained a full-duplex model to emit reasoning
tokens while listening. PredGen (Li and Grover, 2025) pre-generated
candidate responses from partial user input in a cascaded pipeline.
Saguaro (Kumar et al., 2026) prepared speculations for likely
verification outcomes while the current verification was still running.
These methods compute ahead of their input with extra training, a
cascaded pipeline, or a separate draft model; Speculative Listening
drafts the next chunk with the unchanged model and verifies it against
the real audio.

## 3 Method

In this section, we formalize decoding in a chunk-interleaved model,
report two observations on chunk outputs, and present Speculative
Listening.

### 3.1 Preliminary: Chunk-Interleaved Decoding

A chunk-interleaved model divides a conversation into time chunks
$`t=1,2,\dots`$ of fixed duration. Let $`\mathbf{a}_{t}`$ denote the
input tokens of chunk $`t`$, namely the encoded audio of the chunk
together with any concurrent text input, and let $`\mathbf{y}_{t}`$
denote its output tokens. The model processes all chunks as one causal
sequence, and the context of chunk $`t`$ is
$`\mathbf{x}_{<t}=(\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{t-1},\mathbf{y}_{t-1})`$.
Within a chunk, we drop the chunk index and write
$`\mathbf{y}_{t}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{|\mathbf{y}_{t}|})`$ with
$`y_{i}\in\mathcal{V}`$. In most models, the first token $`y_{1}`$
decides whether the model keeps listening or starts an output, through
an explicit listen/speak token in some models (Cui et al., 2026;
Orantqing et al., 2026) and an end-of-chunk token in others; the
remaining tokens carry the content, such as text for a speech decoder or
a structured tool call. Under greedy decoding, a model with parameters
$`\theta`$ generates

```math
y_{i}=\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{V}}\,p_{\theta}\left(y\mid\mathbf{x}_{<t},\mathbf{a}_{t},\mathbf{y}_{<i}\right). \tag{1}
```

A forward pass of the model processes a block of input tokens and yields
the distribution of the next token at each position. Autoregressive
decoding prefills $`\mathbf{a}_{t}`$ and then decodes the output token
by token. Each forward pass takes the token decoded by the previous one
as input, and the forward passes run one after another. Their number
after $`\mathbf{a}_{t}`$ arrives grows linearly with
$`|\mathbf{y}_{t}|`$. A forward pass over a few tokens takes about as
long as a forward pass over one token (Shazeer, 2019; Kim et al., 2023).
We call the time from the arrival of $`\mathbf{a}_{t}`$ to the end of
the chunk’s computation its _post-audio latency_. A chunk whose
post-audio latency exceeds the chunk duration misses its _deadline_ and
delays the following chunks.

#### Chunk profile.

We profiled autoregressive decoding by chunk type on the four models of
Section 5.1 (Figure 3a). Listen chunks process the audio and output only
control tokens, and compute waits for the next audio during 70–89% of
the chunk duration. We call this time the _idle window_. Speak and
tool-call chunks sit at the other end. A tool call takes tens of serial
forward passes and completes 1.8–2.6 s after its audio arrives, and
every evaluated tool call missed the deadline. LWS, whose speak chunks
can also decode a written channel, missed the deadline on 95% of them.

### 3.2 Observations

Using the idle window requires decoding chunk $`t`$ before
$`\mathbf{a}_{t}`$ arrives, that is, under an assumed input. We take
silence as the simplest assumption: the _silence hypothesis_ replaces
$`\mathbf{a}_{t}`$ with $`\mathbf{a}_{\text{sil}}`$, the input tokens of
a chunk of silence with no text input. To measure how often it yields
the real output, we ran an oracle probe without a time limit. It decodes
every chunk twice, once with $`\mathbf{a}_{t}`$ and once with
$`\mathbf{a}_{\text{sil}}`$, and checks whether the two outputs are
identical (Figure 3b,c).

#### Observation 1: the next audio chunk usually does not change the output.

Pooled over the four models, the two outputs match in 95.3% of chunks.
The match rate is lowest on Realtime-Venus while the user is speaking,
when incoming speech updates the context (Figure 3b and Section B.2).

#### Observation 2: changes concentrate at the first token.

Pooled over the four models, 87.7% of mismatched chunks diverge at the
first output token, which in most models is the decision to keep
listening or to start an output (Figure 3c).

### 3.3 Speculative Listening

Speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023) drafts
the next few tokens and feeds the whole draft to the target model as
input in one forward pass. In this forward pass, the distribution for
the $`i`$-th token depends only on the first $`i-1`$ draft tokens. When
these tokens are correct, it equals the distribution of autoregressive
decoding at step $`i`$. Verification accepts the draft tokens up to the
first mismatch and replaces the mismatched token with the choice of the
target model. A single forward pass can then commit several tokens, and
it takes about as long as decoding one token (Section 3.1). Speculative
Listening uses the target model itself as the draft model and drafts
chunk $`t`$ under the silence hypothesis during the idle window, before
$`\mathbf{a}_{t}`$ arrives. It verifies the draft in the forward pass
prefilling $`\mathbf{a}_{t}`$ (Figure 2). The forward passes of drafting
run before the audio arrives. When the draft matches the whole output,
the number of post-audio forward passes no longer grows with
$`|\mathbf{y}_{t}|`$.

#### Drafting under the silence hypothesis.

After chunk $`t-1`$ finishes, the model decodes greedily on its own KV
cache,

```math
\tilde{y}_{i}=\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{V}}\,p_{\theta}\left(y\mid\mathbf{x}_{<t},\mathbf{a}_{\text{sil}},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{<i}\right),\quad i=1,\dots,\gamma, \tag{2}
```

until it reaches the end of the chunk or the idle window ends, which
yields the primary draft
$`\tilde{\mathbf{y}}=(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots,\tilde{y}_{\gamma})`$. When
$`\mathbf{a}_{t}`$ arrives, the KV cache is rolled back to the end of
$`\mathbf{x}_{<t}`$, and one forward pass over
$`(\mathbf{a}_{t},\tilde{\mathbf{y}})`$ prefills the audio and computes
the target distributions
$`p_{i}=p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}_{<t},\mathbf{a}_{t},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{<i})`$
for $`i=1,\dots,\gamma+1`$. Following greedy verification (Xia et al.,
2024), the number of accepted tokens is

```math
n=\min\left(\left\{\,i-1\mid 1\leq i\leq\gamma,\ \tilde{y}_{i}\neq\arg\max p_{i}\,\right\}\cup\{\gamma\}\right). \tag{3}
```

The forward pass commits $`\tilde{y}_{1},\dots,\tilde{y}_{n}`$ and the
correction $`\arg\max p_{n+1}`$, and decoding continues from there. The
committed output is identical to Equation 1. Every forward pass commits
at least one token, and the number of post-audio forward passes never
exceeds the number under autoregressive decoding. Under stochastic
decoding, the draft is verified by speculative sampling (Leviathan et
al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023), which preserves the target distribution
(Section 5.4).

#### Hypothesis pool and runner-up branch.

The idle window of one chunk holds a short speak chunk but may end
before a tool call of several dozen tokens is drafted. A user request
spans several chunks, and drafts made during earlier chunks may already
contain part of the eventual output. Speculative Listening keeps all
drafts of a session in a _hypothesis pool_ $`\mathbf{C}`$, indexed by
their $`N`$-grams as in lookahead decoding (Fu et al., 2024).
Observation 2 shows that most mismatches start at the first token. When
the primary draft finishes before the idle window ends, the model
decodes a second draft, the _runner-up branch_, from the runner-up first
token
$`\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{\tilde{y}_{1}\}}p_{\theta}(y\mid\mathbf{x}_{<t},\mathbf{a}_{\text{sil}})`$,
and adds it to $`\mathbf{C}`$. During drafting and verification, after
each forward pass the runtime looks up the longest $`N`$-gram in
$`\mathbf{C}`$ and in the session’s earlier outputs matching the suffix
of the output so far, and appends its continuation to the following
forward pass. The number of post-audio forward passes then grows only
with output tokens missing from every draft and with the appended pool
segments (Section 5.4).

In this section, we introduce TickTock-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating
decoding methods on chunk-interleaved full-duplex models. In these
models, the chunk duration acts as a hard deadline: a chunk that takes
longer to decode delays the following chunks, and the conversation lags
behind real time. The model then processes the user’s speech late, and
its responses become stale in the ongoing interaction. The value of a
decoding method for these models lies in how it changes this behaviour:
how long a response or a tool call waits after the user’s audio, how
often chunks miss their deadlines, and how far the conversation falls
behind. Measuring these effects requires checking each chunk against its
deadline, by chunk type, in the model’s own runtime. Chunk-interleaved
models are also growing in number, each with its own runtime, and a
shared benchmark lets decoding methods be compared across them.
Following Spec-Bench (Xia et al., 2024), which compared speculative
decoding methods on text generation under a unified protocol,
TickTock-Bench evaluates all methods on the same sessions and hardware
under this chunk-level measurement. We release it for evaluating future
decoding methods on these models in the same way.

TickTock-Bench covers three task categories (Table 1): speech reasoning
with Web Questions (Berant et al., 2013), Llama Questions (Nachmani et
al., 2024), and TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017) from OpenAudioBench (Li et
al., 2025), AlpacaEval from VoiceBench (Chen et al., 2024), and Big
Bench Audio (BBA) (Artificial Analysis, 2024; Srivastava et al., 2022;
Suzgun et al., 2023); conversational dynamics with Full-Duplex-Bench
(FDB) v1 (Lin et al., 2025) and v1.5 (Lin et al., 2026b); and agentic
tool use with FDB v3 (Lin et al., 2026a). Speech reasoning tests spoken
question answering, conversational dynamics tests listening and
responding around pauses, turn ends, and overlapping speech, and agentic
tool use tests tool calls.

The replay harness feeds each session to the model chunk by chunk
through its own runtime, one session at a time on one NVIDIA A100 GPU,
and labels each chunk by its output as a listen, speak, or tool-call
chunk. We use the speedup over autoregressive decoding in mean
post-audio latency per chunk type as the primary metric, measured in
wall-clock time without speech synthesis. Computation that a method
performs before the audio arrives runs in the idle window, and any part
beyond the idle window is added to the post-audio latency. To measure
how far decoding falls behind real time, we also report the deadline
miss rate and the maximum lag behind the audio per session, together
with \#MAT (Xia et al., 2024). Details are provided in Appendix A.

Avg. is the mean speedup over the seven speech-reasoning and

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

#### Evaluation.

On FDB v3, we reported tool-call chunks for Streaming SLM and Gander,
and speak chunks for Realtime-Venus and LWS. Realtime-Venus issued no
tool calls on these sessions under greedy decoding, and LWS has no
tool-call channel. The ablation and the analysis use FDB v3 and Llama
Questions. All evaluated methods are lossless: in exact arithmetic,
their outputs are identical to those of autoregressive decoding
(Section 5.4).

#### Models.

We evaluated four chunk-interleaved full-duplex models with a 1 s chunk
duration, covering more than one model family: (1) _Streaming SLM_, a
chunk-interleaved full-duplex model that we trained from the turn-based
Qwen2.5-Omni-7B Thinker, with audio input, text input, and tool calls;
(2) *Gander* (Orantqing et al., 2026) and (3) *Realtime-Venus* (Ant
Group, 2026), two public agentic full-duplex models built on
MiniCPM-o (Cui et al., 2026), run with their released runtimes; and (4)
*LWS* (Zhang et al., 2026), a tri-channel full-duplex model built on
MiniCPM-o whose speak chunks can decode a written text channel alongside
the text it speaks. In all four models, the backbone (Thinker) decodes
text tokens, and a separate speech decoder (Talker) synthesizes the
audio from these tokens or their hidden states. We measured the Thinker
only, since it is the only component in which the methods differ.

#### Baselines.

We compared Speculative Listening with autoregressive decoding (AR) and
three training-free, model-free speculative decoding methods that run
after the audio arrives. Prompt Lookup Decoding (PLD) (Saxena, 2023)
drafts tokens by matching $`n`$-grams in the context. Token
Recycling (Luo et al., 2025) drafts from candidate tokens recycled from
earlier decoding steps, and lookahead decoding (Fu et al., 2024) drafts
from $`n`$-grams collected by Jacobi iterations; we evaluated both in
chain verification mode to match the models’ runtimes.

#### Implementation.

All models ran in bfloat16 with greedy decoding and batch size 1, under
the protocol of Section 4.

### 5.2 Main Results

#### Speedup across models and tasks.

We examined whether decoding in the idle window shortens post-audio
latency across models and tasks. As shown in Table 2, we observe two
main trends. First, Speculative Listening is faster than autoregressive
decoding and the three training-free baselines on all models and tasks.
It reduces mean speak-chunk latency by 2.6–2.8$`\times`$ on average
across the four models and tool-call latency on FDB v3 by
23.41$`\times`$ on Streaming SLM (Figure 6b) and 3.45$`\times`$ on
Gander; the three baselines reach 0.96–1.23$`\times`$. The number of
tokens committed per forward pass shows the same gap: Speculative
Listening reaches a \#MAT of 2.15–2.63, and the baselines reach
1.01–1.23. Second, on Streaming SLM, the speedup grows with output
length: speak chunks contain a few tokens and are sped up by
2.6–3.0$`\times`$, and tool calls contain several dozen tokens and are
sped up by 23.41$`\times`$. Both trends follow from the source of the
drafts and the way they are verified. Speculative Listening drafts with
the target model before the audio arrives, and as Observation 1 shows,
most drafts match the real output. The baselines draft from $`n`$-grams
in the context after the audio arrives, and these $`n`$-grams rarely
match the new response in a speak chunk. An accepted draft is verified
within the audio forward pass and saves one forward pass per output
token under autoregressive decoding, and longer outputs save more. By
drafting in the idle window and verifying in the audio forward pass,
Speculative Listening decodes most of a chunk’s output before its audio
arrives.

#### Deadline misses.

We further examined whether Speculative Listening keeps the models in
real time. A deadline miss delays the following chunks, and the delay
accumulates over consecutive chunks. To quantify this effect, we
measured the maximum lag behind the audio per session (Appendix A). As
shown in Table 3, under AR the maximum lag reaches 2.6 s on Streaming
SLM and 4.0 s on LWS, and 35% of the sessions of Streaming SLM lag
behind the audio by more than 1 s. With Speculative Listening, the lag
stays at zero on Streaming SLM and Realtime-Venus and averages 42 ms on
Gander and 55 ms on LWS, and at most 1% of the sessions lag behind the
audio by more than 1 s. These results show that Speculative Listening
reduces the maximum lag behind real time by 94.4% on average.

### 5.3 Ablation Study

To examine the contribution of each component, we added
silence-hypothesis drafting, the hypothesis pool, and the runner-up
branch to AR one at a time on Streaming SLM and Gander, using FDB v3 for
tool-call chunks and Llama Questions for speak chunks.

#### Effects of silence-hypothesis drafting.

We first added silence-hypothesis drafting alone. As shown in Table 4,
it speeds up speak chunks by 2.6–2.9$`\times`$ and tool-call chunks by
1.2–1.4$`\times`$. These results show that drafting within one idle
window covers short outputs. A tool call of several dozen tokens exceeds
the idle window, and its draft stops before the call is complete.

#### Effects of the hypothesis pool.

We then added the hypothesis pool. As shown in Table 4, it raises the
tool-call speedup from 1.42$`\times`$ to 22.04$`\times`$ on Streaming
SLM and from 1.21$`\times`$ to 2.04$`\times`$ on Gander. These results
show that drafts from earlier chunks cover most of a tool call: a user
request spans several chunks, and the drafts made in earlier chunks
already contain most of the eventual call.

#### Effects of the runner-up branch.

Finally, we added the runner-up branch. As shown in Table 4, it raises
the tool-call speedup from 2.04$`\times`$ to 3.45$`\times`$ on Gander
and from 22.04$`\times`$ to 23.41$`\times`$ on Streaming SLM. These
results show that the branch helps where the silence draft departs at
the first token (Observation 2): it provides a draft starting from the
alternative first token. On Streaming SLM, the pool already leaves
little for the branch to remove.

### 5.4 In-Depth Analysis

#### Analysis on user-perceived latency.

To examine whether the speedup shortens the delays that users hear, we
measured two latencies (Figure 5 and Appendix C). The response onset
latency is the time from the arrival of the audio of the first speak
chunk of a response to the end of its Thinker output. The tool-call
latency is the time from the arrival of the audio of a tool-call chunk
to the end of the call. As shown in Table 5, Speculative Listening
reduces the response onset latency by 73% on average across the four
models and the tool-call latency by 72–96%. These results show that the
post-audio speedup shortens the delays before a response and before a
tool call. Human speakers start a response about 200 ms after the other
speaker ends (Levinson and Torreira, 2015). Under AR, decoding alone
adds 417–1089 ms before a response starts. With Speculative Listening,
it adds 74–241 ms, close to this gap, and tool calls become ready within
the chunk duration on average.

#### Analysis on output length and sampling.

To examine whether the speedup holds beyond the main setting, we varied
the output length and the sampling temperature $`T\in\{0.3,0.7,1.0\}`$
(Figure 4a,b). With Speculative Listening, a chunk needs 1.0–1.4
post-audio forward passes for outputs of 2 to 128 tokens. AR grows from
2.0 passes for one token to 73.6 passes for 65–128 tokens, the three
baselines follow the same trend, and silence-hypothesis drafting alone
reaches 52.9 passes. Speculative Listening speeds up speak and tool-call
chunks by 2.1$`\times`$ and 13.3$`\times`$ at $`T=0.3`$ and by
1.4$`\times`$ and 5.4$`\times`$ at $`T=1.0`$. Sampled tokens depart from
the greedy draft more often as the temperature rises and shorten the
accepted prefix. These results show that Speculative Listening needs few
post-audio passes at every output length and remains effective under
sampling.

#### Analysis on response quality.

To examine whether Speculative Listening preserves the outputs of AR, we
compared the two. Under float32, Speculative Listening reproduces AR
token for token on nearly all sessions. Under bfloat16, used in the main
tables, rounding can change outputs, and the AlpacaEval judge scores of
AR and Speculative Listening differ by less than 0.1 point on all four
models (Table 6 in Section B.1). These results show that Speculative
Listening preserves the response quality of AR.

## 6 Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we studied decoding latency in chunk-interleaved
full-duplex speech language models. We identified an asymmetric workload
across chunks: listen chunks leave compute idle while awaiting audio,
and speak and tool-call chunks require serial generation growing with
output length. Comparing outputs under real audio and the silence
hypothesis, we found two properties: the next audio chunk usually does
not change the output, and changes concentrate at the first token.
Building on these properties, we proposed Speculative Listening, a
training-free and lossless framework. It drafts each chunk in the idle
window and verifies the draft in the audio forward pass. On
TickTock-Bench, it reduces the maximum lag behind real time by 94.4% and
the decoding delay before a response starts by 73% on average. Future
work includes drafting under input hypotheses beyond digital silence,
such as streaming acoustic features.

## Limitations

This work has three limitations. First, Speculative Listening targets
low-concurrency, latency-sensitive deployment. It computes during the
idle window, and the idle window shrinks as more sessions share a GPU or
are batched. In our multi-session experiment, concurrent sessions share
one GPU, and the speedup decreases as the number of sessions grows
(Section B.4). Second, we compared the Thinker only, since the Talkers
of the four models synthesize speech in different ways and would
confound the comparison. Third, our formulation targets
chunk-interleaved architectures with an idle window between chunks;
parallel-stream architectures such as Moshi (Défossez et al., 2024),
which decode at every 80 ms audio frame, require a different
formulation.

## Appendix A Benchmark Details

We release the session manifests, the replay harness, and the evaluation
scripts. Latencies measured on other hardware or under other CPU load
differ from ours. The lag accumulates over chunks as
$`b_{t}=\max(0,\,b_{t-1}+\ell_{t}-\text{chunk duration})`$ with
$`b_{0}=0`$, where $`\ell_{t}`$ is the post-audio latency of chunk
$`t`$. The response onset and tool-call latencies in Section 5.4 include
this lag. Drafting for chunk $`k`$ starts when the computation of chunk
$`k-1`$ ends, and its budget is the chunk duration minus the measured
latency of chunk $`k-1`$, which includes the audio encoder and runtime
overheads. Drafting beyond the budget is added to the post-audio latency
of chunk $`k`$. Under AR and the baselines, the GPU stays idle during
the idle window.

### B.4 Multi-Session Serving

To examine Speculative Listening under GPU contention, we ran
$`N\in\{1,2,4\}`$ concurrent sessions on one A100 GPU and measured
wall-clock latency. As shown in Figure 4c, at $`N=1`$ Speculative
Listening speeds up speak chunks by 2.9$`\times`$ and tool-call chunks
by 22.7$`\times`$. At $`N=4`$, it speeds up speak chunks by
2.0$`\times`$ and tool-call chunks by 10.5$`\times`$, and it reduces the
deadline miss rate of tool-call chunks from 100% to 2.9%. These results
show that Speculative Listening remains effective under GPU contention,
and its gain on long tool calls decreases as $`N`$ grows. Concurrent
sessions slow down drafting, which leaves less time to draft long tool
calls.

## Appendix C Case Study

To illustrate how Speculative Listening shortens the delays that users
perceive (Wang et al., 2026b; Ao et al., 2026), we walk through two
example sessions in Figure 6. The latencies in the figure are typical
values measured on TickTock-Bench (Table 5).

Figure 6: Case study on two illustrative sessions. SL: Speculative
Listening. Span labels give typical measured latencies (Table 5), and
green spans mark the time saved by Speculative Listening. Check marks
denote drafted tokens accepted by verification. (a) Response onset. The
first speak chunk is drafted under silence in chunk 2 and verified when
its audio arrives. (b) Tool call. R: tool result. Drafts made while the
user speaks contain the tool call and enter the hypothesis pool. Under
AR, the call misses the deadline, and the dashed arrow links the audio
of chunk 5 to the time AR starts processing it.

#### Response onset.

As shown in Figure 6a, the user asks a question in chunks 1 and 2, and
the model starts its answer in chunk 3. Under AR, the model decodes the
first speak chunk after its audio arrives, and the output is ready after
0.9 s. With Speculative Listening, the model drafts chunk 3 under
silence during the idle window of chunk 2, and the audio forward pass of
chunk 3 verifies the draft. The output is ready after 0.2 s. In chunks 4
to 6, both methods finish each speak chunk within the chunk duration,
and each chunk starts when its audio arrives. The answer then streams at
the same pace under both methods and starts 0.7 s earlier with
Speculative Listening.

#### Tool call.

As shown in Figure 6b, the user requests a flight over chunks 1 to 3.
During each idle window, Speculative Listening drafts the next chunk
under silence. From chunk 2 on, these drafts contain the tool call with
the details heard so far, and the hypothesis pool keeps them. While the
user is still speaking, verification rejects them at the first token. In
chunk 4, the audio forward pass verifies the drafted call, and the call
is ready after 0.1 s. Under AR, the model decodes the call token by
token for 2.6 s and misses the deadline by 1.6 s. Chunk 5 then starts
1.6 s after its audio arrives. In this example, the tool returns its
result 0.5 s after the call is dispatched, and the result enters the
first chunk that starts after it returns (Wang et al., 2026a; Shen et
al., 2026). It enters chunk 5 with Speculative Listening and chunk 7
under AR. The answer to the user then starts 2.2 s earlier with
Speculative Listening.
