# Route Anything: A Study of Model and Policy Routing for General-Purpose AI Systems

Pengfei Zhou  Yixing Ma  Hexin Wang^(‡)  Weidong Tang  Zhenglin Wan
 Wangbo Zhao  Yang You^(‡) National University of Singapore, HPC-AI
Lab InfRec, Cardinal AI Lab University of California, Berkeley Hong Kong

###### Abstract

Artificial general intelligence (AGI) requires a method that can
understand, generate, reason, and act across diverse domains and
modalities. Rather than relying on a single model to master every
capability, a promising path toward AGI now is to orchestrate a
heterogeneous ecosystem of specialized models, tools, and policies
(candidates) into a unified AI system. At the heart of this vision lies
a fundamental question: how can an AI system dynamically select and
coordinate the most capable candidate for each task while balancing
performance and computational cost? In this work, we present Route
Anything, a unified framework for studying model and policy routing
across general-purpose AI systems. We develop a unified theoretical
foundation that characterizes optimal routing under quality–cost
trade-offs and establishes how the information available to a router
determines its attainable performance. To systematically investigate
routing across diverse capabilities, we first construct an omni-modal
trajectory matrix spanning 25 benchmarks across language understanding,
image, video, and speech generation, coding and workplace agents, and
robotic manipulation, comprising 374,401 scored runs from 452 candidate
configurations. Building on this framework, we train and test different
routers to investigate the potential of omni-modal model routing, the
capabilities of learned routers, and the conditions under which routing
improves efficiency. Our results reveal substantial opportunities for
capability orchestration: per-input oracle routing outperforms the best
fixed candidate by 5.4 points, and learned routers outperform it by 0.3
points across 16 benchmarks, by 2.1 points on video generation, and by
1.1 points on robot manipulation while using 24% less policy time. More
importantly, routing raises efficiency by $`8.82\times`$ on average
compared with the reference model, including $`25.5\times`$ on coding
tasks and $`7.1\times`$ on text question answering.

## 1 Introduction

Agentic AI systems now can draw on multiple kinds of specialized models,
tools, and policies: a language model answers a question, an image or
video generator renders a prompt, a speech model reads text aloud, and a
learned policy controls a robot. Rather than relying on one model to
master every capability, a general-purpose system can orchestrate this
heterogeneous ecosystem and choose, for each input, which module handles
it. We call each such model, tool, or policy a _candidate_. The best
candidate changes from input to input, so a router that chooses well is
expected to match one fixed strongest candidate at a fraction of its
price, or beat it by sending every input to the most suitable candidate
that handles it best.

Routing research has mostly studied language models: cascades that
escalate to a stronger model \[1\], routers learned from preferences
\[2\], and quality–cost formulations that connect the two \[3\]. Whether
routing helps across modalities remains an open question. Thus we
present a unified framework _Route Anything_ for studying model and
policy routing across modalities. To support such anlyasitical
framework, we first build an omni-modal trajectory matrix in which every
candidate in a pool runs on the same inputs, for 25 benchmarks across
text and image understanding, image, video, and speech generation, code
and workplace agents, and robot manipulation, with three to seven
candidates per pool and 374,401 scored runs of 452 candidate
configurations (Figure 1). We train and test routers using different
strategies and ask three questions: _how much can routing gain, how well
do learned routers work, and when is a cheaper route good enough?_

Our unified routing theory frames all three: a router’s value is the
best expected outcome, net of priced cost, given what it observes, so
what it can reach is a ladder from the best fixed candidate to the
per-input oracle, and a reference policy with a quality tolerance fixes
the price of quality. The matrix measures each rung, and two code and
text testbeds with recorded prices \[4, 5\] add the cost side. We also
provide empirical insights through extensive experiments:

![](arxiv-2611-05275v1--c8326736c4ef.figures/inline-1.svg)

#### Contributions.

(1) A unified theory of routing. We connect resource prices and quality
constraints to optimal model and policy selection, and characterize how
the information available to a router determines its attainable
performance (§2). (2) An omni-modal trajectory matrix. We run 452
candidate configurations on the same inputs of 25 benchmarks across text
and image understanding, image, video, and speech generation, code and
workplace agents, and robot manipulation, for 374,401 scored runs (§3
and Appendix B). (3) The potential of omni-modal routing. We quantify
the potential gain of routing (5.4 points over the best fixed
candidate), train and test learned routers that beat the best fixed
candidate by 0.3 points overall and 2.1 points on video generation, show
that task-type labels capture 8% of the potential gain on benchmarks
with such labels, and examine how training targets and baseline choices
affect measured gains (§3 and §4). (4) When routing improves efficiency.
We show that routing raises efficiency over the reference by
$`8.82\times`$ on average and up to $`25.5\times`$ on code with matched
quality, and evaluate on diverse conditions (§5).

## 2 A Router’s Value

Sections 3 and 4 use the information ladder and the training targets
(Propositions 3 and 4), mostly at $`\lambda=0`$ since most of the matrix
records no costs; Section 5 adds prices, a reference, and a certified
floor (Lemma 1, Theorem 2, and Proposition 5).

#### Candidates, states, and outcomes.

An input $`x`$ (a question, an image with a question, a task with files,
a generation prompt, or camera frames with a robot state) comes with a
finite menu $`\mathcal{U}(x)`$ of _candidates_: language models, image,
video, or speech generators, robot policies, or execution plans such as
“try a cheap model, verify, and escalate on rejection”. Running
candidate $`u`$ delivers a value $`V_{u}\in[0,1]`$, the benchmark’s
metric, and consumes $`D_{u}>0`$ units of one resource, such as dollars
or seconds, with $`\mathbb{E}[D_{u}]<\infty`$. A router observes a
_state_ $`s=\sigma(x)`$, which may be the benchmark, a capability type,
or the input itself, and chooses a candidate $`g(s)`$, possibly at
random. We write $`v(s,u)=\mathbb{E}[V_{u}\mid s]`$ and
$`c(s,u)=\mathbb{E}[D_{u}\mid s]`$. Routing does not change which inputs
arrive, so the law $`\pi`$ of states is fixed; sequential choices within
a task enter as complete plans.

#### Price, reference, and floor.

At a resource price $`\lambda\geq 0`$, router $`g`$ earns
$`J_{\lambda}(g)=\mathbb{E}[V_{g}]-\lambda\mathbb{E}[D_{g}]`$; at
$`\lambda=0`$ it is ranked by quality alone. When no price is given, we
compare $`g`$ with a named _reference_ policy, such as always calling
the strongest model, whose value and cost on the same inputs are
$`Y_{B}`$ and $`C_{B}`$, and define the quality change $`Q`$, the saving
$`\mathcal{S}`$, the efficiency $`\eta`$, and the quality floor with
tolerance $`\epsilon\geq 0`$:

```math
Q=\mathbb{E}[V_{g}-Y_{B}],\quad\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{E}[C_{B}-D_{g}],\quad\eta=\frac{\mathbb{E}[V_{g}]}{\mathbb{E}[D_{g}]},\quad\eta_{B}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{B}]}{\mathbb{E}[C_{B}]},\quad Q\geq-\epsilon. \tag{1}
```

###### Lemma 1 (Reference-relative gain).

$`\eta-\eta_{B}=(Q+\eta_{B}\mathcal{S})/\mathbb{E}[D_{g}]`$. Efficiency
improves if and only if $`Q+\eta_{B}\mathcal{S}>0`$, and the quality
floor is the separate condition $`Q\geq-\epsilon`$.

Both conditions are needed: a primary that is correct on 15% of tasks at
a tenth of the cost of an always-correct reference has
$`\eta=1.5\,\eta_{B}`$ but $`Q=-0.85`$.

###### Theorem 2 (Optimal routing at a price and under a floor).

Let the state take finitely many values, each with $`\pi(s)>0`$, let the
menu depend on $`x`$ only through $`s`$, and let costs be positive. (i)
A router maximizes $`J_{\lambda}`$ if and only if, in every state, it
plays only candidates maximizing $`v(s,u)-\lambda c(s,u)`$. The highest
efficiency $`\eta^{\star}=\max_{g}\eta(g)`$ is the unique root of
$`\Phi(\lambda)=\sum_{s}\pi(s)\max_{u}[v(s,u)-\lambda c(s,u)]`$, and the
efficiency-optimal routers are exactly the maximizers of
$`J_{\eta^{\star}}`$. (ii) If the reference is itself a router and
$`\epsilon\geq 0`$, an efficiency-optimal router under the floor
$`Q\geq-\epsilon`$ exists, and for some $`\kappa\geq 0`$ every such
router maximizes $`J_{\lambda}`$ at
$`\lambda_{\epsilon}=\eta^{\star}_{\epsilon}/(1+\kappa)`$, where
$`\eta^{\star}_{\epsilon}`$ is the constrained optimum.

The proof combines Dinkelbach’s method \[6\] with linear-programming
duality (Appendix A.2). Part (ii) says that maximizing efficiency under
a floor yields no new kind of router: it is routing at one price, set by
the reference and the tolerance rather than by hand or by a budget
\[3\]. Without recorded costs, the rule is to play the candidate with
the highest expected value given the state.

#### The information ladder.

What a router can reach depends on what it observes. Write
$`W_{u}=V_{u}-\lambda D_{u}`$ and let
$`V^{\star}(\sigma)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\max_{u}\mathbb{E}[W_{u}\mid\sigma(x)]\bigr]`$
be the best return of a router that observes $`\sigma(x)`$, attained, as
in part (i) of Theorem 2, by playing in each state a candidate that
maximizes $`\mathbb{E}[W_{u}\mid\sigma(x)]`$.

###### Proposition 3 (Information ladder).

Let $`\sigma_{1}`$ be coarser than $`\sigma_{2}`$, that is,
$`\sigma_{1}(x)`$ is a function of $`\sigma_{2}(x)`$, and let the menu
depend on $`x`$ only through $`\sigma_{1}(x)`$. Then

```math
V^{\star}(\sigma_{1})\ \leq\ V^{\star}(\sigma_{2})\ \leq\ V^{\star}(x)\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\bigl[\max_{u}W_{u}\bigr]. \tag{2}
```

The first inequality is an equality if and only if, for almost every
value $`s_{1}`$ of $`\sigma_{1}`$, a single candidate is optimal at
almost every finer state $`s_{2}`$ with $`\sigma_{1}=s_{1}`$. The last
gap equals
$`\mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{E}[\max_{u}W_{u}\mid x]-\max_{u}\mathbb{E}[W_{u}\mid x]\}`$,
and it vanishes if and only if, given almost every $`x`$, one candidate
attains $`\max_{u}W_{u}`$ almost surely.

With $`\sigma_{1}`$ the benchmark, $`V^{\star}(\sigma_{1})`$ is the best
fixed candidate per benchmark. The outer rungs are measurable: the best
fixed candidate on the left and the per-input oracle on the right. Inner
rungs are bounded below by any router that observes that state and
estimated optimistically by the best lookup chosen on the test inputs. A
coarse state helps only where it changes the best candidate, and the
last gap is outcome noise that no observable state predicts, as when a
candidate’s success on an input is a coin flip.

#### What to learn.

A learned router needs a training target. Two are common: each
candidate’s outcome, and the identity of the winning candidate.

###### Proposition 4 (Outcomes, not winners).

(a) For each candidate, the score that minimizes the expected binary
cross-entropy, or squared error, against $`V_{u}`$ given $`x`$ is
$`m_{u}(x)=\mathbb{E}[V_{u}\mid x]`$, and routing to
$`\argmax_{u}m_{u}(x)`$ attains $`V^{\star}(x)`$ at $`\lambda=0`$. (b)
Let the _winner_ be the candidate with the highest realized value, ties
broken by a fixed rule, and let $`p_{u}(x)`$ be the probability that
$`u`$ wins, which a cross-entropy classifier on winner labels estimates.
Routing to $`\argmax_{u}p_{u}(x)`$ can lose almost half of the value
range: if candidate $`A`$ always delivers $`a<1`$ and candidate $`B`$
delivers $`1`$ with probability $`b\in(\tfrac{1}{2},a)`$ and $`0`$
otherwise, then $`p_{B}=b>p_{A}`$, while $`\mathbb{E}[V_{B}]=b<a`$.

Tie-breaks make it worse: with binary outcomes and ties sent to a faster
candidate, a candidate that succeeds with probability 0.9 loses the
label to a faster independent candidate that succeeds with probability
0.5 (it wins with probability 0.45). Per-candidate regression uses every
outcome, including ties and failures, whereas a winner label keeps one
choice per input.

#### Certifying the floor.

Theorem 2 is applied to fitted values and costs, so a fitted floor need
not hold on test inputs.

###### Proposition 5 (Certified quality floor).

Let values lie in $`[0,1]`$, let $`\epsilon\geq 0`$ and
$`\alpha\in(0,1)`$, and let the routers $`g_{1},\dots,g_{K}`$ and their
order be fixed by the fitting data. Let $`p_{k}`$ be valid $`p`$-values
for the null hypotheses $`Q_{g_{k}}<-\epsilon`$, computed on $`n`$
inputs drawn i.i.d. from the deployment law, independently of the
fitting data, with matched outcomes; for instance
$`p_{k}=\exp\{-n(\hat{Q}_{k}+\epsilon)_{+}^{2}/2\}`$, where
$`\hat{Q}_{k}`$ is the sample mean of $`V_{g_{k}}-Y_{B}`$. Let
$`\hat{g}=g_{\hat{k}}`$ with
$`\hat{k}=\max\{k:p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\leq\alpha\}`$, or the reference if
$`p_{1}>\alpha`$. Then $`\mathbb{P}(Q_{\hat{g}}<-\epsilon)\leq\alpha`$.

Figure 2: Certifying a floor. Routers on the price path are tested in
order on $`\mathcal{B}`$.

A natural order is the _price path_ (Figure 2): the maximizers of the
fitted $`J_{\lambda}`$ as $`\lambda`$ rises from 0 to the fitted
$`\eta^{\star}`$, along which fitted value and cost fall while fitted
efficiency rises (Appendix A.5). The test stops at the first router it
cannot certify, so no multiplicity correction is needed, as in
fixed-sequence testing \[7, 8\]; returning the reference when nothing is
certified mirrors high-confidence policy improvement \[9\]. The
certified router comes with a certified price: the reference, the
tolerance, and the level fix $`\lambda`$.

#### Setup.

Every candidate in a pool runs on the same inputs, and each run is
scored by the benchmark’s own metric, rescaled to $`[0,1]`$ and reported
on a 0–100 scale. The 25 benchmarks fall into seven groups (Table 1):
text QA \[10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16\], image-text QA \[17, 18, 19\],
image generation \[20, 21, 22, 23, 24\], video generation \[25\], speech
generation \[26\], code and workplace agents \[27, 28, 29, 30, 31\], and
robot manipulation \[32\]; Appendix B lists them with their metrics and
candidates. The language-model candidates are Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6.1
Sol, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, joined by GPT-6 Astra on long-context,
knowledge, and agent tasks. Inputs are split 70/10/20 into training,
development, and test sets by source group, so identical inputs and
items from one source never cross splits; RoboTwin’s 2,000 test scenes
come from 10 tasks the routers never see in training. The _fixed
candidate_ plays the best candidate per benchmark on training inputs,
where missing outcomes never disqualify a candidate (Section 4.4 shows
why this matters), and the _oracle_ plays the best observed candidate on
each input. These are the outer rungs of Proposition 3. We measure them
on the 19,814 test inputs on which every candidate is scored, which
leaves out SciCode, whose later steps contain each model’s own earlier
code, and estimate the rungs in between with the best lookup per
benchmark or per type chosen in hindsight.

#### Finding 1: The potential gain is large, and it lies within task types.

On the 19,814 test inputs, the best fixed candidate per benchmark scores
82.60 and the per-input oracle 87.98 (Table 1). The gap is 0.7 points
for speech generation, where every generator is nearly perfect, and
18–19 points for video generation and robot manipulation, where no
candidate dominates. Little of it is available to a router that sees
only the benchmark or the task type. The best candidate per benchmark
chosen in hindsight raises 82.60 only to 82.62. On the 18 benchmarks of
the rule-based capability tree, which assigns each of 5,034 test inputs
to one of 107 capability leaves (Section 4.1), the best candidate per
leaf chosen in hindsight scores 77.89, against 77.03 for the fixed
candidate and 87.44 for the oracle: 8% of the gap lies between types. On
RoboTwin, the best policy per task and scene condition, again chosen on
the test scenes, recovers 23% of the 18.4-point gap. Code tasks show the
same split. The best released CodeRouterBench routers capture 55% of the
value of perfect routing information when the task type is unknown, but
only 6–8% within a task type (Appendix C.5).

Inside a type, outcomes vary input by input. On GenEval2, each of the
five other generators beats the fixed one by more than two points on
76–100 training prompts while trailing it on more, and the per-prompt
difference has a standard deviation of 31–38 points against mean
differences of 4–14. Part of this variation is noise that no router can
predict (the last gap of Proposition 3). On AA-LCR, where every question
is answered three times, the oracle’s 2.4-point margin over the fixed
candidate vanishes once each candidate is scored by its mean over the
three runs.

Section 4 studies learned routers on every benchmark except seven
understanding ones (MMLU-Pro, MMLU-Redux, SuperGPQA, GPQA, and the three
image-text QA benchmarks), which hold three quarters of the inputs and
half of the pooled gap in Table 1, with gaps of 1.4–6.0 points; every
benchmark with a gap above 8 points is studied.

## 4 How Well Do Learned Routers Work?

Our routers fine-tune Qwen3.5-4B on the task text and the output
requirement, and for robots also on three camera frames and the robot
state (Appendix B.3). An _outcome router_ scores each pair of input and
candidate profile with a sigmoid head, trained by cross-entropy against
the candidate’s normalized outcome (Proposition 4(a)), and plays the
highest score. A _type router_ classifies the input into a capability
type and plays that type’s best candidate, read from a table fitted on
training inputs, or the fixed candidate when it cannot place the input.
A _winner-label router_ is trained to name the best candidate. Each
study is scored on the test inputs where all its compared policies have
outcomes (Table 2); differences carry paired 95% bootstrap intervals
over source groups, or over scenes for RoboTwin.

#### Finding 2: learned routers beat the best fixed candidate by 0.3 points, and by up to 2.1 points per output group.

One router over 16 benchmarks in five output groups scores 79.98,
against 79.68 for the fixed candidate and 84.80 for the oracle. It
matches the fixed candidate to within 0.01 points on 10 benchmarks and
departs from it where candidates complement each other input by input:
Terminal-Bench ($`+8.3`$ on 36 inputs), VBench-2.0 ($`+2.1`$), OneIG
($`+1.1`$), and GDP.pdf ($`+1.0`$); its largest loss is AA-LCR
($`-2.4`$). The leaf classifier is 86–89% accurate, yet 83–86% of inputs
still go to the fixed candidate, because within a benchmark one
candidate wins almost every type (Proposition 3). A rule-based tree with
107 leaves, which needs no classifier, scores 0.09 points above the
fixed candidate, almost all of it on OneIG. In image generation, across
31 router designs and 124 configurations on GenEval2 and
Qwen-Image-Bench, none beats the fixed generator with an interval above
zero, and 44 are significantly worse. An untrained Qwen3.5-4B that picks
the candidate itself scores 1.15 points below the fixed candidate.

### 4.2 Routers That Read the Scene

Figure 4: Routing by scene condition. Robot success on 2,000 test
scenes.

#### Setup.

On RoboTwin, the outcome router reads the instruction, three
$`240\times 320`$ camera frames, and the robot state, and predicts the
success of each of seven vision-language-action policies with one
sigmoid head per policy, trained by cross-entropy on all 49,000 training
outcomes. No task identity enters the input, and the 2,000 test scenes
come from 10 tasks the router never sees, half in clean scenes and half
in domain-randomized ones. ME-Dex is the best policy on training scenes
and also on the test scenes (70.45%); PatchWAM is second (63.65%). A
policy’s time on a scene is its inference seconds, summed over its
queries.

#### Finding 3: on robots, the router reads the scene and saves time.

The router sends 929 of the 1,000 clean scenes to PatchWAM and 842 of
the 1,000 randomized scenes to ME-Dex (141 more to GigaBrain). The best
policy flips between the two conditions, and the router tracks it: on
clean scenes it scores 81.6%, against 81.7% for PatchWAM and 78.4% for
ME-Dex; on randomized scenes 61.5%, against 62.5% for ME-Dex and 45.6%
for PatchWAM (Figure 4). Overall success is 71.55% against 70.45%
($`+1.10`$ $`[-0.40,+2.55]`$), at 11.05 instead of 14.51 seconds of
policy time per scene, 24% less: $`1.33\times`$ the success per
policy-second of the best single policy. This is the coarse rung of
Proposition 3 learned from pixels, since the condition-level choice
alone would score 72.1%. The remaining 17% to the oracle require
predicting which policy succeeds on a given scene.

### 4.3 What a Router Should Learn

Figure 5: Learn outcomes, not winners. Winner labels mislabel most
successes.

#### Finding 4: learn outcomes, not winners.

With the same inputs, backbone, and training scenes, the winner-label
classifier reaches 42.8% robot success against 71.6% for per-policy
outcome regression (Table 4). The labels explain the gap, as Proposition
4(b) predicts: several policies succeed on most training scenes, a
latency tie-break decides 94% of the labels, and 24,556 of the 31,408
successful training outcomes become wrong-class targets, so the
classifier learns to pick fast policies (1.9 seconds per scene) that
often fail. Outside robotics, no panel compares the two targets
directly, and no target beats the fixed candidate. Generative
winner-label routers on 16 benchmarks learn from 11,273 labels of which
only a third are decided by a unique best outcome, the rest by cost,
tokens, or a fixed order; the best of 12 recipes scores 78.87 against
79.44 for the fixed candidate. On 11 text, agent, and image benchmarks,
the outcome router stays within noise of the matched fixed candidate
($`-0.40`$), and a type router over 68 types, although 75% accurate,
maps 93% of its predictions to the same candidate as the true type and
scores 1.32 points below it.

### 4.4 Matched Comparisons

Figure 6: Compare candidates on the same inputs. Missing scores flip the
sign.

#### Finding 5: compare candidates on the same inputs.

A fixed baseline that admits a model only if it has a score on every
training input drops strong models over a few missing scores. In the
11-benchmark study, Opus 5.5 missed a few scores on seven benchmarks,
for example 11 of 1,122 training inputs of HLE, so the baseline fell
back to the next model there: on HLE it scored 52.90 instead of 58.24,
and on AA-Omniscience 70.00 instead of 77.50. Pooled, the baseline
dropped from 68.30 to 65.52, and the outcome router’s 0.40-point deficit
became a 2.38-point gain (Table 4). Choosing and evaluating the fixed
candidate under the routers’ fallback, which uses GPT-6 Astra’s outcome
where Opus 5.5 has none, removes the artifact. The same rule governs
portfolios. Adding a model changes which tasks reach the fallback, so
averages frozen at the current portfolio misrank candidates, whereas
comparing both portfolios on the same tasks picks the best added model
in 96.4–97.1% of CodeRouterBench comparisons, against 86.3–94.3% for
frozen averages (Appendix C.3).

## 5 When Is a Cheaper Route Good Enough?

### 5.1 The Price of a Route

#### Setup.

A route trades quality for cost where candidates differ more in price
than in value. The matrix records prices for image and speech generation
and time for robot policies. For code and text, two testbeds add token
prices: CodeRouterBench \[4\], with eight models on 9,999 coding tasks
(7,080 probing tasks for fitting and 2,919 test tasks) and the reference
claude-opus-4-6 (Opus 4.6), and RouterBench \[5\], with eleven models on
36,497 mostly non-code prompts split 70/30 and the reference
gpt-4-1106-preview. On CodeRouterBench, states are the 21
dimension$`\times`$source cells, and each state offers 22 candidates:
direct dispatch to one of eight models, or one of seven primaries
followed by Opus 4.6 when the primary’s released score, an oracle
verifier, is below $`\theta\in\{1,0.5\}`$. Such a reference-fallback
cascade with verifier decision $`H\in\{0,1\}`$ and
primary-plus-verification cost $`A`$ delivers

```math
V=HY_{P}+(1-H)Y_{B},\qquad D=A+(1-H)C_{B}. \tag{3}
```

Values, costs, and state frequencies are fitted on probing tasks, the
floor $`Q\geq-\epsilon`$ is imposed there, and policies are evaluated on
test tasks; always-Opus scores 0.4383 at 11.655 m\$ per task.
RouterBench uses source-family states and the same construction. For
image generation, states are the five benchmarks, candidates are the
generators (three on GenEval2, six elsewhere), and the reference plays
the best generator per benchmark (the fixed candidate).

#### When the reference is worth calling.

A candidate that calls the reference is worth its price only while the
reference earns enough on the inputs it receives.

###### Corollary 6 (Admission price).

In the setting of Theorem 2, let $`\lambda^{B}`$ be the largest price at
which some candidate that calls the reference maximizes
$`v(s,\cdot)-\lambda c(s,\cdot)`$ in some state. If
$`\lambda^{B}<\eta^{\star}`$, the efficiency-optimal router never calls
the reference, and neither does a floor-optimal router whose price
$`\lambda_{\epsilon}`$ exceeds $`\lambda^{B}`$. A cascade (3) whose
primary is also available by direct dispatch maximizes only at prices up
to
$`\mathbb{E}[(Y_{B}-Y_{P})\mathbf{1}_{H=0}\mid s]/\mathbb{E}[C_{B}\mathbf{1}_{H=0}\mid s]`$,
the reference’s added value per dollar on the inputs it receives.

#### Finding 6: a cheaper route is often good enough.

Across the four testbeds of Table 5, routing raises efficiency over the
reference by $`8.82\times`$ on average (the mean of $`25.5`$, $`7.1`$,
$`1.35`$, and $`1.33\times`$, with image generation at
$`\epsilon=0.5`$). On CodeRouterBench, the admission price of Opus 4.6
is $`\lambda^{B}=100`$ score per dollar ($`2.9\,\eta_{B}`$), below the
zero-loss floor price of 144 and far below $`\eta^{\star}=1022`$, so by
Corollary 6 the zero-loss router never calls Opus. It dispatches every
task to a cheaper model and reaches $`25.5\times`$ Opus’s efficiency at
a 0.44-point loss (Table 5). Always calling Qwen3-Max, the cheapest
model, already reaches $`20.1\times`$ at a 4.2-point loss; routing by
state recovers that loss while adding 27% efficiency. The states need
not be oracle labels: prompt-inferred cells reach $`25.3\times`$, and
learned quality–cost routers $`22.7`$–$`24.2\times`$ at their zero-loss
points (Appendix C.2). Without states, every zero-loss policy calls
Opus, which then runs on 28.7% of tasks. On RouterBench, the best router
that avoids the reference scores 9.7 points below it, so the zero-loss
router cascades cheap primaries to the reference and reaches
$`7.1\times`$. A fitted floor is not a guarantee. Refitted on half of
the probing tasks, the zero-loss floor holds on test in only 12.5% of
200 splits; certified on the other half by Proposition 5 with
empirical-Bernstein $`p`$-values on the mixture-refined path, it holds
in all 200, at a median $`19.4\times`$. Image generation follows the
same logic. GPT Image 2.5 costs \$0.21 per image, 3.5–5.4$`\times`$ the
other generators, and is the best candidate on two of the five
benchmarks. The route that stays within half a point on training prompts
moves Qwen-Image-Bench to Grok Imagine 2.0 and OneIG to Qwen-Image 3,
cutting test cost by 26% at a 0.47-point loss, and a two-point tolerance
halves cost. In speech generation the best generator is also among the
cheapest, so there is nothing to trade, and on robots the time saving of
Section 4.2 comes with no loss of success. Router overhead is small next
to these prices: a 4B router takes 0.13s per decision on one H20 GPU,
whereas generating an image takes 27–158s and a video 211–314s.

### 5.2 Spending More Within a Task

#### Setup.

A candidate can spend more inside a task: a plan that retries after a
failure, or that verifies an output and escalates on rejection. A router
can also buy a finer state: a forecast of a candidate’s success, or a
record of past outcomes. By Theorem 2, a richer candidate is worth
playing only if its value net of its full cost beats the candidates it
replaces, and that value must be computed on the inputs that reach each
step (Proposition 8). By Proposition 3, a finer state can only help an
optimal router, but it is worth buying only if the help exceeds its
price (Proposition 9). We test both on CodeRouterBench, with fresh model
calls for verifier verdicts and forecasts, and semi-synthetic streams
for memory (Appendix C).

#### Finding 7: richer candidates and finer states are worth their cost only if they beat what they replace.

_Retries._ A failure leaves harder tasks: in all 42 ordered model pairs,
the second model succeeds less often after the first fails (median ratio
0.49 at $`\theta=1`$). _Verifiers._ For Qwen3-Max at $`\theta=0.5`$, a
free compiler gate gives $`4.09\times`$ dollar efficiency at a 2.4-point
loss, and an LLM judge $`2.63\times`$ if free but $`0.30\times`$ at its
list price; in seconds, always-Opus beats every verified cascade,
because Opus answers faster than the primaries. _Finer states._ A
forecast can save at most the attempt it replaces: a DeepSeek-v4-pro
forecast costs 2.4 attempts at list price, a prompt-only TF-IDF router
0.06% of one.

## 6 Related Work

#### Routing language models.

Cascades and routers trade quality against cost \[1, 2, 3\], and learned
routers estimate each model’s quality from the query \[34, 35, 36, 37,
38\]. The acceptance step of a cascade is a reject option \[39\],
studied as confidence-based or budgeted deferral \[40, 41, 42, 43, 44\].
Dekoninck et al. \[3\] characterize optimal routing and cascading
through a Lagrangian price tuned to a budget; here, the reference and
the quality tolerance fix that price, and a finite-sample test certifies
the resulting floor. Routing benchmarks record per-model outcomes and
costs on shared prompts \[5, 4\].

#### Routing beyond text.

SCOPE-Router arranges vision-language models for execution-oriented
tasks \[19\], and routing ideas also inspire vision-token pruning
strategies \[45\], dynamic submodels for video segmentation \[46\], and
acceleration policies inside diffusion transformers \[47\].
Coordinators, harness-level routers, and token-level routers extend
routing beyond model identity \[48, 49, 50, 51, 52\]. We study routing
across output types on one matrix: language models, image, video, and
speech generators, and robot policies, all scored on shared inputs.

#### Portfolios and tools.

Algorithm portfolios choose among complementary solvers per instance
\[53, 54, 55\]. Their virtual best solver corresponds to our per-input
oracle, and its advantage over the single best solver gives the
potential gain in Proposition 3. Residual coverage measures what an
additional solver contributes to the portfolio \[56, 57\]. Accounting
for call costs connects this selection problem to fractional
programming, which optimizes ratio objectives such as quality per unit
cost \[6, 58\]. The value of information addresses the related question
of whether acquiring a routing signal is worth its cost \[59, 60\].
Similar cost considerations arise in test-time compute, where gains from
extra calls depend on the task and allocation \[61, 62\], while
imperfect verifiers can limit the gains from resampling \[63\].
Feedback-driven self-repair uses earlier attempts to guide later ones
\[64, 65\], making resampling at equal cost a natural baseline \[66\].

## 7 Conclusion

Across text, images, video, speech, code, and robots, the best candidate
changes from input to input, and the per-input oracle sits 5.4 points
above the best fixed candidate per benchmark. Most of that potential
gain lies within task types, and a learned type router already scores
0.3 points above the best fixed candidate across 16 benchmarks, and up
to 2.1 points within an output group. In embodied scenarios, a router
trained on camera frames tracks the best VLA policy in each scene
condition and beats the best single policy by 1.1 points with 24% less
policy time. Routing’s most reliable gain is a cheaper route that is
good enough, raising efficiency over the reference by $`8.82\times`$ on
average: when candidates are close in quality and far apart in cost, the
reference and the quality tolerance fix the price, and a finite-sample
test certifies the quality floor. Three rules follow: (1) compare
candidates on the same inputs, and measure gains against the matched
best fixed candidate; (2) train routers on per-candidate outcomes, and
look for gains within task types; (3) fix the reference and the
tolerance before ranking routes, and certify the floor on independent
inputs.

## Appendix A Proofs and Formal Results

This appendix proves the results of Section 2 and Corollary 6, and
states, with short proofs, the formal results behind Sections 4.4 and
5.2. Except in the proofs of Propositions 3 and 4, where $`x`$ is the
input as in Section 2, we write $`x`$ for the router’s observed state
$`s`$ and $`\mathcal{X}`$ for its finite set of values.

### A.1 Proof of Lemma 1

###### Proof.

Write $`V=V_{g}`$, $`D=D_{g}`$, $`\bar{v}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{B}]`$, and
$`\bar{c}=\mathbb{E}[C_{B}]`$. The definitions give
$`\mathbb{E}[V]=\bar{v}+Q`$ and $`\mathbb{E}[D]=\bar{c}-\mathcal{S}`$,
so

```math
\eta-\eta_{B}=\frac{\bar{v}+Q}{\bar{c}-\mathcal{S}}-\frac{\bar{v}}{\bar{c}}=\frac{Q+(\bar{v}/\bar{c})\mathcal{S}}{\bar{c}-\mathcal{S}},
```

whose denominator $`\mathbb{E}[D]`$ is positive. Substituting
$`\bar{v}=\eta_{B}\bar{c}`$ gives, for every $`\lambda`$,

```math
Q+\eta_{B}\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{E}[V-\eta_{B}D],\qquad\mathbb{E}[V-\lambda D]=\mathbb{E}[D]\,(\eta-\lambda). \tag{4}
```

No independence or stationarity is needed; the quality condition follows
separately from $`\mathbb{E}[V]=\bar{v}+Q`$. For a stationary ergodic
sequence of rounds, the long-run ratio
$`\sum_{n<N}V_{n}/\sum_{n<N}D_{n}`$ converges almost surely to
$`\mathbb{E}[V]/\mathbb{E}[D]`$, a ratio of aggregates rather than the
average of $`V_{n}/D_{n}`$. ∎

### A.2 Proof of Theorem 2

Throughout, $`x`$ ranges over a finite set $`\mathcal{X}`$ of observable
states with router-independent probabilities $`\pi(x)>0`$; each state
offers a finite menu $`\mathcal{U}(x)`$ of candidates with expected
value $`v(x,u)`$ and expected cost $`c(x,u)>0`$; a router $`g`$ chooses
$`u`$ with probability $`g(u\mid x)`$. Let $`\mathcal{G}`$ be the set of
routers, a product of simplices and hence a compact polytope, and put
$`c_{\min}=\min_{x,u}c(x,u)>0`$. For $`g\in\mathcal{G}`$,

```math
\mathbb{E}_{g}[V]=\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}(x)}g(u\mid x)\,v(x,u),\qquad\mathbb{E}_{g}[D]=\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}(x)}g(u\mid x)\,c(x,u)\ \geq\ c_{\min},
```

$`\eta(g)=\mathbb{E}_{g}[V]/\mathbb{E}_{g}[D]`$, and for every real
$`\lambda`$

```math
\mathbb{E}_{g}[V-\lambda D]=\mathbb{E}_{g}[D]\,\bigl(\eta(g)-\lambda\bigr). \tag{5}
```

A router is deterministic if every $`g(\cdot\mid x)`$ is a point mass.
The reference policy is admissible if it is an element
$`g_{B}\in\mathcal{G}`$; then $`\eta(g_{B})=\eta_{B}`$ and
$`\mathbb{E}_{g_{B}}[V]=\bar{v}`$, so the reference satisfies the floor
$`\mathbb{E}[V]\geq\bar{v}-\epsilon`$ for every $`\epsilon\geq 0`$.

###### Proof.

_Two preliminary facts._ (F1) State-wise separation: for any real
numbers $`\phi(x,u)`$, the linear functional
$`g\mapsto\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{u}g(u\mid x)\phi(x,u)`$ on $`\mathcal{G}`$
is maximized exactly by the routers that, in every state $`x`$, put all
their mass on $`\argmax_{u}\phi(x,u)`$, and its maximum is
$`\sum_{x}\pi(x)\max_{u}\phi(x,u)`$. Indeed, for each $`x`$ the inner
sum is a convex combination of the numbers $`\phi(x,u)`$, hence at most
$`\max_{u}\phi(x,u)`$, with equality if and only if $`g(\cdot\mid x)`$
is supported on the maximizers; and $`\pi(x)>0`$. In particular a
deterministic maximizer exists. (F2) Sign of the Dinkelbach function:
applying (F1) with $`\phi=v-\lambda c`$,

```math
\Phi(\lambda)=\sum_{x}\pi(x)\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}(x)}\bigl[v(x,u)-\lambda c(x,u)\bigr]=\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{E}_{g}[V-\lambda D], \tag{6}
```

so by (5) and $`\mathbb{E}_{g}[D]>0`$, $`\Phi(\lambda)\geq 0`$ if and
only if some router has $`\eta(g)\geq\lambda`$, and $`\Phi(\lambda)>0`$
if and only if some router has $`\eta(g)>\lambda`$.

_Part (i)._ Each $`\phi_{x}(\lambda)=\max_{u}[v(x,u)-\lambda c(x,u)]`$
is the maximum of finitely many affine functions of $`\lambda`$ with
slopes $`-c(x,u)<0`$. It is therefore convex, piecewise linear and
continuous, and strictly decreasing: if $`\lambda<\lambda^{\prime}`$ and
$`u^{\prime}`$ attains $`\phi_{x}(\lambda^{\prime})`$, then
$`\phi_{x}(\lambda)\geq v(x,u^{\prime})-\lambda c(x,u^{\prime})>v(x,u^{\prime})-\lambda^{\prime}c(x,u^{\prime})=\phi_{x}(\lambda^{\prime})`$.
Moreover $`\phi_{x}(\lambda)\to+\infty`$ as $`\lambda\to-\infty`$, and
$`\phi_{x}(\lambda)\leq\max_{u}v(x,u)-\lambda c_{\min}\to-\infty`$ as
$`\lambda\to+\infty`$. The positive combination
$`\Phi=\sum_{x}\pi(x)\phi_{x}`$ inherits all these properties and has a
unique root $`\eta^{\star}`$. By (F2), $`\Phi(\eta^{\star})=0`$ yields a
router with $`\eta(g)\geq\eta^{\star}`$ and forbids
$`\eta(g)>\eta^{\star}`$; hence $`\eta^{\star}=\max_{g}\eta(g)`$, and
the maximum is attained. A router $`g`$ is efficiency-optimal if and
only if $`\eta(g)=\eta^{\star}`$, if and only if
$`\mathbb{E}_{g}[V-\eta^{\star}D]=0=\Phi(\eta^{\star})`$ by (5), if and
only if $`g`$ maximizes the $`\eta^{\star}`$-objective over
$`\mathcal{G}`$, if and only if, by (F1), in every state it uses only
candidates maximizing $`v(x,u)-\eta^{\star}c(x,u)`$.

_Part (ii): existence._ Let
$`\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}=\{g\in\mathcal{G}\colon\mathbb{E}_{g}[V]\geq\bar{v}-\epsilon\}`$,
a compact polytope that contains $`g_{B}`$ because $`\epsilon\geq 0`$.
Since $`\eta`$ is continuous on $`\mathcal{G}`$ (its denominator is at
least $`c_{\min}`$), it attains its maximum $`\eta^{\star}_{\epsilon}`$
on $`\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}`$.

_Part (ii): the multiplier._ Define
$`\Phi_{\epsilon}(\lambda)=\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}}\mathbb{E}_{g}[V-\lambda D]`$.
As a maximum over a compact set of affine functions of $`\lambda`$ with
slopes $`-\mathbb{E}_{g}[D]\leq-c_{\min}`$, it is convex, continuous and
strictly decreasing, tends to $`\pm\infty`$ as $`\lambda\to\mp\infty`$,
and has a unique root. By (5), $`\Phi_{\epsilon}(\lambda)\geq 0`$ if and
only if some $`g\in\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}`$ has $`\eta(g)\geq\lambda`$,
so the root is $`\eta^{\star}_{\epsilon}`$. Consequently a router is
constrained-optimal if and only if it solves

```math
\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\ f(g):=\mathbb{E}_{g}[V-\eta^{\star}_{\epsilon}D]\quad\text{subject to}\quad h(g):=\mathbb{E}_{g}[V]-(\bar{v}-\epsilon)\geq 0, \tag{7}
```

whose optimal value is $`\Phi_{\epsilon}(\eta^{\star}_{\epsilon})=0`$.
Both $`f`$ and $`h`$ are affine in $`g`$ and $`\mathcal{G}`$ is a
polytope, so (7) is a linear program; it is feasible ($`g_{B}`$) and
bounded. By linear-programming strong duality applied to the single
constraint $`h\geq 0`$, there is a multiplier $`\kappa\geq 0`$ such that

```math
\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\,\bigl[f(g)+\kappa\,h(g)\bigr]=0.
```

If $`g`$ is optimal for (7), then $`h(g)\geq 0`$ and $`f(g)=0`$, so
$`0\leq f(g)+\kappa h(g)\leq 0`$: hence $`\kappa h(g)=0`$ and $`g`$
maximizes $`f+\kappa h`$ over $`\mathcal{G}`$. Now
$`f(g)+\kappa h(g)=\mathbb{E}_{g}[(1+\kappa)V-\eta^{\star}_{\epsilon}D]+\kappa(\epsilon-\bar{v})`$,
so by (F1) every constrained-optimal router uses, in every state, only
candidates maximizing
$`(1+\kappa)v(x,u)-\eta^{\star}_{\epsilon}c(x,u)`$, that is,
$`v(x,u)-\lambda_{\epsilon}c(x,u)`$ with
$`\lambda_{\epsilon}=\eta^{\star}_{\epsilon}/(1+\kappa)`$, and so
maximizes $`J_{\lambda_{\epsilon}}`$; and
$`\kappa\,(\mathbb{E}_{g}[V]-\bar{v}+\epsilon)=0`$: if $`\kappa>0`$ the
floor binds at every optimal router. The value $`\kappa=0`$ is a valid
multiplier if and only if some efficiency-optimal router of part (i)
satisfies the floor, and then $`\eta^{\star}_{\epsilon}=\eta^{\star}`$.
∎

### A.3 Proof of Proposition 3

###### Proof.

Assume each $`W_{u}`$ is integrable, and write $`S_{1}=\sigma_{1}(x)`$
and $`S_{2}=\sigma_{2}(x)`$. The menu $`\mathcal{U}(S_{1})`$ is finite
and available at every finer state. By the tower property,
$`\mathbb{E}[W_{u}\mid S_{1}]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[W_{u}\mid S_{2}]\bigm|S_{1}\bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\max_{u^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}[W_{u^{\prime}}\mid S_{2}]\bigm|S_{1}\bigr]`$
for every $`u`$; taking the maximum over $`u`$ and then expectations
gives $`V^{\star}(\sigma_{1})\leq V^{\star}(\sigma_{2})`$. Let $`u_{1}`$
attain $`\max_{u}\mathbb{E}[W_{u}\mid S_{1}]`$. The two sides are equal
almost surely if and only if
$`\mathbb{E}[W_{u_{1}}\mid S_{2}]=\max_{u}\mathbb{E}[W_{u}\mid S_{2}]`$
almost surely given $`S_{1}`$, that is, if and only if one candidate is
optimal at almost every finer state; conversely, if a candidate $`u`$ is
optimal at almost every $`S_{2}`$ with $`\sigma_{1}=s_{1}`$, then
$`\mathbb{E}[W_{u}\mid S_{1}]`$ attains the right side, so equality
holds. The same argument with $`\sigma_{2}`$ replaced by the identity
gives $`V^{\star}(\sigma_{2})\leq V^{\star}(x)`$. Finally,
$`W_{u}\leq\max_{u^{\prime}}W_{u^{\prime}}`$ for every $`u`$, so
$`\max_{u}\mathbb{E}[W_{u}\mid x]\leq\mathbb{E}[\max_{u}W_{u}\mid x]`$
for every $`x`$, and taking expectations gives the last inequality; the
gap is the expectation of this nonnegative difference. It vanishes if
and only if the difference is zero for almost every $`x`$. If $`u(x)`$
attains $`\max_{u}\mathbb{E}[W_{u}\mid x]`$, the difference is
$`\mathbb{E}[\max_{u}W_{u}-W_{u(x)}\mid x]`$, the mean of a nonnegative
variable, which is zero if and only if $`W_{u(x)}=\max_{u}W_{u}`$ almost
surely given $`x`$; and if some candidate attains the maximum almost
surely given $`x`$, it also attains $`\max_{u}\mathbb{E}[W_{u}\mid x]`$.
∎

### A.4 Proof of Proposition 4

###### Proof.

(a) Fix $`x`$ and a candidate $`u`$, and let
$`\mu=\mathbb{E}[V_{u}\mid x]\in[0,1]`$. For a score $`m\in[0,1]`$, with
$`0\log 0=0`$, the expected cross-entropy is
$`-\mu\log m-(1-\mu)\log(1-m)`$, a strictly convex function of $`m`$
minimized at $`m=\mu`$, and the expected squared error is
$`\operatorname{Var}(V_{u}\mid x)+(\mu-m)^{2}`$, also minimized at
$`m=\mu`$. Hence $`m_{u}(x)=\mathbb{E}[V_{u}\mid x]`$, and routing to
$`\argmax_{u}m_{u}(x)`$ earns $`\max_{u}\mathbb{E}[V_{u}\mid x]`$ at
every $`x`$, whose expectation is $`V^{\star}(x)`$ at $`\lambda=0`$. (b)
Since $`\tfrac{1}{2}<b<a<1`$, candidate $`B`$ beats $`A`$ exactly when
$`V_{B}=1`$, an event of probability $`b`$, and loses otherwise; no ties
occur. So $`p_{B}=b>\tfrac{1}{2}>1-b=p_{A}`$, and the winner-label
router plays $`B`$, whose expected value $`b`$ is below $`a`$. The loss
$`a-b`$ approaches $`\tfrac{1}{2}`$ as $`b\downarrow\tfrac{1}{2}`$ and
$`a\uparrow 1`$. In the tie-break example, the first candidate wins only
when it succeeds and the second fails, with probability
$`0.9\times 0.5=0.45`$. ∎

### A.5 Proof of Proposition 5

#### Setting.

Let $`P`$ be the deployment law of a task. A task has a state $`x`$ in a
finite set $`\mathcal{X}`$, a menu $`\mathcal{U}(x)`$, a realized value
$`V_{u}\in[0,1]`$ and cost $`D_{u}>0`$ for every $`u\in\mathcal{U}(x)`$,
and the reference value $`Y_{B}\in[0,1]`$ and cost $`C_{B}`$. A router
$`g`$, possibly randomized, is scored by its expected delivered value
given the task, $`V_{g}=\sum_{u}g(u\mid x)V_{u}`$, which is $`V_{g(x)}`$
for a deterministic router, and $`Q_{g}=\mathbb{E}_{P}[V_{g}-Y_{B}]`$ is
its quality change. The reference is admissible: it is a router
$`g_{B}`$ with $`\mathbb{E}_{P}[V_{g_{B}}]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[Y_{B}]`$, so
$`Q_{g_{B}}=0`$. Two samples are independent: $`\mathcal{A}`$ is
arbitrary fitting data, and $`\mathcal{B}=(T_{1},\dots,T_{n})`$ consists
of $`n`$ i.i.d. tasks from $`P`$, each recording $`x_{i}`$, $`Y_{B,i}`$
and $`V_{u,i}`$ for every candidate used below, as in matched replay.

From $`\mathcal{A}`$ we fit $`\hat{\pi}(x)>0`$, $`\hat{v}(x,u)\in[0,1]`$
and $`\hat{c}(x,u)>0`$. For a router $`g`$ write
$`\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{g}[V]=\sum_{x}\hat{\pi}(x)\sum_{u}g(u\mid x)\hat{v}(x,u)`$,
define $`\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{g}[D]`$ likewise, and put
$`\hat{\eta}(g)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{g}[V]/\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{g}[D]`$ and
$`\hat{J}_{\lambda}(g)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{g}[V]-\lambda\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{g}[D]`$.
Let
$`\hat{\phi}_{x}(\lambda)=\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}(x)}[\hat{v}(x,u)-\lambda\hat{c}(x,u)]`$
and $`\hat{\Phi}=\sum_{x}\hat{\pi}(x)\hat{\phi}_{x}`$. Theorem 2(i),
applied to the fitted primitives, gives the root
$`\hat{\eta}^{\star}=\max_{g}\hat{\eta}(g)`$, and we assume
$`\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{g_{B}}[V]>0`$, so that $`\hat{\eta}^{\star}>0`$. A
_breakpoint_ of $`\hat{\phi}_{x}`$ is a point at which
$`\hat{\phi}_{x}`$ is not differentiable. Let
$`\lambda_{1}<\dots<\lambda_{K-1}`$ be the breakpoints of all
$`\hat{\phi}_{x}`$ that lie in $`(0,\hat{\eta}^{\star})`$, and set
$`\lambda_{0}=0`$, $`\lambda_{K}=\hat{\eta}^{\star}`$ and
$`I_{k}=(\lambda_{k-1},\lambda_{k})`$. Fix a strict order on each
$`\mathcal{U}(x)`$ (the tie-break), and let $`g_{k}(x)`$ be the first
maximizer of $`\hat{v}(x,u)-\lambda\hat{c}(x,u)`$ in that order, for
$`\lambda\in I_{k}`$. The sequence $`g_{1},\dots,g_{K}`$ is the _price
path_. A mixture $`wg_{k}+(1-w)g_{k+1}`$ is the router that, in each
state, plays $`g_{k}(x)`$ with probability $`w`$ and $`g_{k+1}(x)`$
otherwise.

###### Proposition 5 (full statement).

_(a) Path._ (1) Each $`g_{k}`$ is well defined, is the same for every
$`\lambda\in I_{k}`$, and maximizes $`\hat{J}_{\lambda}`$ over all
routers for every $`\lambda\in[\lambda_{k-1},\lambda_{k}]`$. Moreover
$`K\leq 1+\sum_{x}(|\mathcal{U}(x)|-1)`$. (2) For $`k<l`$,
$`\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{g_{k}}[V]>\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{g_{l}}[V]`$,
$`\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{g_{k}}[D]>\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{g_{l}}[D]`$ and
$`\hat{\eta}(g_{k})<\hat{\eta}(g_{l})`$; in particular
$`g_{1},\dots,g_{K}`$ are distinct.

_(b) Certification._ Let $`\alpha\in(0,1)`$ and $`\epsilon\geq 0`$, and
let $`p_{1},\dots,p_{K}`$ be functions of $`(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})`$
such that $`\mathbb{P}(p_{k}\leq\alpha\mid\mathcal{A})\leq\alpha`$
whenever $`Q_{g_{k}}<-\epsilon`$. Let
$`\hat{k}=\max\{k\in\{0,\dots,K\}:p_{j}\leq\alpha\text{ for all }j\leq k\}`$,
and let $`\hat{g}=g_{\hat{k}}`$ if $`\hat{k}\geq 1`$ and
$`\hat{g}=g_{B}`$ otherwise. Then

```math
\mathbb{P}\bigl(Q_{\hat{g}}<-\epsilon\mid\mathcal{A}\bigr)\leq\alpha\quad\text{almost surely, hence}\quad\mathbb{P}\bigl(Q_{\hat{g}}<-\epsilon\bigr)\leq\alpha.
```

With $`\hat{Q}_{k}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(V_{g_{k},i}-Y_{B,i})`$, the
Hoeffding $`p`$-value
$`p_{k}=\exp\{-n(\hat{Q}_{k}+\epsilon)_{+}^{2}/2\}`$ satisfies the
requirement. The conclusion holds for any finite family of routers and
any order that are fixed given $`\mathcal{A}`$.

###### Proof.

_Part (a)(1)._ Fix $`x`$. As a maximum of $`|\mathcal{U}(x)|`$ affine
functions with slopes $`-\hat{c}(x,u)<0`$, $`\hat{\phi}_{x}`$ is convex
and piecewise linear; its slope takes values in
$`\{-\hat{c}(x,u):u\in\mathcal{U}(x)\}`$ and strictly increases at each
breakpoint, so $`\hat{\phi}_{x}`$ has at most $`|\mathcal{U}(x)|-1`$
breakpoints. A breakpoint shared by several states is counted once, so
$`K-1\leq\sum_{x}(|\mathcal{U}(x)|-1)`$. On $`I_{k}`$ no
$`\hat{\phi}_{x}`$ has a breakpoint, so
$`\hat{\phi}_{x}(\lambda)=a_{x}-b_{x}\lambda`$ there. For each $`u`$,
$`\hat{\phi}_{x}(\lambda)-[\hat{v}(x,u)-\lambda\hat{c}(x,u)]`$ is affine
and nonnegative on $`I_{k}`$, so if it vanishes at one point of the open
interval $`I_{k}`$, it vanishes on all of it. Hence the maximizer set,
and its first element $`g_{k}(x)`$, are the same for every
$`\lambda\in I_{k}`$, and every maximizer has
$`(\hat{v}(x,u),\hat{c}(x,u))=(a_{x},b_{x})`$. By fact (F1) of Appendix
A.2, $`g_{k}`$ maximizes $`\hat{J}_{\lambda}`$ on $`I_{k}`$; since
$`\hat{J}_{\lambda}(g_{k})`$ and $`\hat{\Phi}(\lambda)`$ are continuous
in $`\lambda`$ and agree on $`I_{k}`$, they agree on its closure.

_Part (a)(2)._ Fix $`1\leq k\leq K-1`$. Let $`\mathcal{X}_{k}`$ be the
set of states for which $`\lambda_{k}`$ is a breakpoint; it is nonempty
by the definition of $`\lambda_{k}`$. For $`x\notin\mathcal{X}_{k}`$,
$`\hat{\phi}_{x}`$ is affine on
$`I_{k}\cup\{\lambda_{k}\}\cup I_{k+1}`$, so by (a)(1) the candidates
$`g_{k}(x)`$ and $`g_{k+1}(x)`$ have the same pair
$`(\hat{v},\hat{c})`$. For $`x\in\mathcal{X}_{k}`$,
$`\hat{c}(x,g_{k}(x))=-\hat{\phi}_{x}^{\prime}(\lambda_{k}-)>-\hat{\phi}_{x}^{\prime}(\lambda_{k}+)=\hat{c}(x,g_{k+1}(x))`$,
and both candidates attain $`\hat{\phi}_{x}(\lambda_{k})`$ by
continuity, so
$`\hat{v}(x,g_{k+1}(x))-\hat{v}(x,g_{k}(x))=\lambda_{k}[\hat{c}(x,g_{k+1}(x))-\hat{c}(x,g_{k}(x))]`$.
Write
$`(v_{j},d_{j})=(\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{g_{j}}[V],\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{g_{j}}[D])`$.
Because $`\hat{\pi}>0`$ and $`\lambda_{k}>0`$,

```math
d_{k+1}-d_{k}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}\hat{\pi}(x)\bigl[\hat{c}(x,g_{k+1}(x))-\hat{c}(x,g_{k}(x))\bigr]<0,\qquad v_{k+1}-v_{k}=\lambda_{k}(d_{k+1}-d_{k})<0.
```

Chaining consecutive steps gives $`v_{k}>v_{l}`$ and $`d_{k}>d_{l}`$ for
$`k<l`$. For efficiency, pick $`\lambda^{\prime}\in I_{l}`$. Optimality
of $`g_{l}`$ at $`\lambda^{\prime}`$ gives
$`v_{k}-v_{l}\leq\lambda^{\prime}(d_{k}-d_{l})`$, hence

```math
v_{l}d_{k}-v_{k}d_{l}=v_{l}(d_{k}-d_{l})-d_{l}(v_{k}-v_{l})\geq(d_{k}-d_{l})(v_{l}-\lambda^{\prime}d_{l})=(d_{k}-d_{l})\,\hat{\Phi}(\lambda^{\prime})>0,
```

because $`d_{k}>d_{l}`$ and $`\hat{\Phi}`$ is strictly decreasing with
root $`\hat{\eta}^{\star}>\lambda^{\prime}`$ (Theorem 2(i)). Dividing by
$`d_{k}d_{l}>0`$ gives $`\hat{\eta}(g_{l})>\hat{\eta}(g_{k})`$. Distinct
fitted costs make the routers distinct.

_Part (b)._ Condition on $`\mathcal{A}`$. Then $`g_{1},\dots,g_{K}`$,
their order, and the numbers $`Q_{g_{k}}`$ are fixed. Let
$`k_{0}=\min\{k:Q_{g_{k}}<-\epsilon\}`$, with $`k_{0}=\infty`$ if no
path router violates the floor. The reference cannot violate it, since
$`Q_{g_{B}}=0\geq-\epsilon`$; this is the only use of
$`\epsilon\geq 0`$. Suppose $`Q_{\hat{g}}<-\epsilon`$. Then
$`\hat{k}\geq 1`$ and $`g_{\hat{k}}`$ violates the floor, so
$`k_{0}\leq\hat{k}<\infty`$. By the definition of $`\hat{k}`$, every
$`p_{j}`$ with $`j\leq\hat{k}`$ is at most $`\alpha`$, and in particular
$`p_{k_{0}}\leq\alpha`$. Therefore

```math
\mathbb{P}\bigl(Q_{\hat{g}}<-\epsilon\mid\mathcal{A}\bigr)\leq\mathbf{1}\{k_{0}<\infty\}\,\mathbb{P}\bigl(p_{k_{0}}\leq\alpha\mid\mathcal{A}\bigr)\leq\alpha,
```

by the requirement on $`p_{k_{0}}`$, because
$`Q_{g_{k_{0}}}<-\epsilon`$. Taking expectations over $`\mathcal{A}`$
gives the unconditional bound. No condition is placed on the dependence
among $`p_{1},\dots,p_{K}`$, which share the sample $`\mathcal{B}`$.

For the Hoeffding $`p`$-value, condition on $`\mathcal{A}`$. Since
$`\mathcal{B}`$ is independent of $`\mathcal{A}`$ and $`g_{k}`$ is
fixed, the variables $`Z_{i}=V_{g_{k},i}-Y_{B,i}`$ are i.i.d., take
values in $`[-1,1]`$, and have mean $`Q_{g_{k}}`$. Put
$`t_{\alpha}=\sqrt{2\log(1/\alpha)/n}>0`$. Then $`p_{k}\leq\alpha`$ if
and only if $`\hat{Q}_{k}+\epsilon\geq t_{\alpha}`$. If
$`Q_{g_{k}}<-\epsilon`$, Hoeffding’s inequality for a range of length
two \[67\] gives

```math
\mathbb{P}(p_{k}\leq\alpha\mid\mathcal{A})\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\hat{Q}_{k}-Q_{g_{k}}\geq t_{\alpha}\mid\mathcal{A}\bigr)\leq\exp(-nt_{\alpha}^{2}/2)=\alpha.\qed
```

#### Other $`p`$-values, refined paths, and the certified price.

Validity does not use the monotonicity in part (a); the order matters
only for power, and part (a)(2) puts routers with high fitted value, and
therefore a small risk of violation, first. Any other valid $`p`$-value
can be used. The empirical-Bernstein bound of Maurer and Pontil \[68,
Theorem 4\], applied to $`(1-Z_{i})/2`$ with
$`Z_{i}=V_{g_{k},i}-Y_{B,i}`$, yields a valid $`p`$-value whose margin
shrinks with the sample variance of $`V-Y_{B}`$. Because validity needs
only a finite family in an order fixed by $`\mathcal{A}`$, mixtures
$`wg_{k}+(1-w)g_{k+1}`$ with decreasing $`w`$ may be inserted between
consecutive path routers; we insert $`w\in\{0.9,0.8,\dots,0.1\}`$ and,
at each tolerance studied, the fitted floor optimum of Theorem 2(ii)
computed on $`\mathcal{A}`$, which on our menus mixes two neighbouring
path routers. If $`\hat{k}\geq 1`$, the certified router maximizes
$`\hat{J}_{\lambda}`$ at $`\hat{\lambda}=\lambda_{\hat{k}}`$, the right
end of its interval on the path; we call $`\hat{\lambda}`$ the
_certified price_. It turns the choice of $`\lambda`$ into an output of
the reference, the tolerance $`\epsilon`$, and the level $`\alpha`$.

### A.6 Proof of Corollary 6

A candidate _calls the reference_ in state $`x`$ if it invokes the
reference with positive probability there. For a candidate $`u`$, the
set $`\Lambda_{x}(u)`$ of prices $`\lambda\geq 0`$ at which $`u`$
maximizes $`v(x,\cdot)-\lambda c(x,\cdot)`$ is a closed interval,
possibly empty, because
$`\lambda\mapsto v(x,u)-\lambda c(x,u)-\phi_{x}(\lambda)`$ is concave,
continuous, and nonpositive, and $`\Lambda_{x}(u)`$ is the set where it
vanishes. The admission price $`\lambda^{B}`$ is the largest point of
the finite union of the sets $`\Lambda_{x}(u)`$ over states and
reference-calling candidates ($`0`$ if the union is empty, $`+\infty`$
if it is unbounded).

###### Proof.

By Theorem 2(i), an efficiency-optimal router plays in every state only
maximizers of $`v(x,\cdot)-\eta^{\star}c(x,\cdot)`$; since
$`\eta^{\star}>\lambda^{B}`$, none of them calls the reference. By part
(ii), a floor-optimal router maximizes $`J_{\lambda_{\epsilon}}`$, so by
part (i) it plays only maximizers of
$`v(x,\cdot)-\lambda_{\epsilon}c(x,\cdot)`$, none of which calls the
reference when $`\lambda_{\epsilon}>\lambda^{B}`$. For the cascade $`u`$
with primary $`P`$, let $`P`$ also denote direct dispatch to it, with
value $`Y_{P}`$ and cost $`C_{P}\leq A`$. By Eq. (3),

```math
v(x,u)-v(x,P)=\mathbb{E}[(Y_{B}-Y_{P})\mathbf{1}_{H=0}\mid x]=:N,\qquad c(x,u)-c(x,P)=\mathbb{E}[A-C_{P}\mid x]+r\ \geq\ r,
```

with $`r=\mathbb{E}[C_{B}\mathbf{1}_{H=0}\mid x]>0`$. If $`u`$ maximizes
at $`\lambda\geq 0`$, then
$`v(x,u)-\lambda c(x,u)\geq v(x,P)-\lambda c(x,P)`$, so
$`\lambda\,[c(x,u)-c(x,P)]\leq N`$. Hence $`N\geq 0`$ and
$`\lambda\leq N/r`$, the bound stated. It holds for every verifier and
verifier cost, but it needs direct dispatch to $`P`$ on the menu: with
$`Y_{P}=0`$, $`A=C_{P}=1`$, $`Y_{B}=C_{B}=1`$, $`H=0`$, and a single
other candidate with cost 10 and value 0, the cascade maximizes at every
price although $`N/r=1`$. ∎

### A.7 Portfolio Comparisons

For a portfolio $`S`$ and a candidate $`m`$, let $`S^{+}=S\cup\{m\}`$,
with delivered values $`V_{S},V_{S^{+}}`$ and complete costs
$`D_{S},D_{S^{+}}`$ of positive mean on the same tasks. Lemma 1, with
$`S`$ as the reference, gives the paired gain

```math
G_{m}(S)=\mathbb{E}[V_{S^{+}}-V_{S}]-\eta(S)\mathbb{E}[D_{S^{+}}-D_{S}],\qquad\eta(S^{+})-\eta(S)=\frac{G_{m}(S)}{\mathbb{E}[D_{S^{+}}]}, \tag{8}
```

so adding $`m`$ improves efficiency if and only if $`G_{m}(S)>0`$,
whatever it changes in accepted quality, fallback composition, or
verifier behaviour. Let $`E_{m}`$ be the event that $`m`$ supplies a
sufficient output, and let an exact selecting verifier pick a sufficient
member whenever one exists, else call the reference. Then $`S`$ hits
with probability $`p(S)=\mathbb{P}(\bigcup_{i\in S}E_{i})`$, and the
_residual coverage_ of $`m`$ is
$`r_{m}(S)=\mathbb{P}(E_{m}\cap\bigcap_{i\in S}E_{i}^{c})=p(S^{+})-p(S)`$,
a joint property of the members’ failures that standalone success rates
do not determine.

###### Proposition 7 (Residual coverage and its correction).

Let $`S`$ deliver mean value $`v_{h}`$ on a hit and $`v_{\rm miss}`$ on
a miss, at primary cost $`a(S)`$ plus a fallback cost $`b`$ on a miss,
and let $`d_{m}(S)=a(S^{+})-a(S)`$. If $`S^{+}`$ has the same $`v_{h}`$,
$`v_{\rm miss}`$, and $`b`$, then $`G_{m}(S)`$ equals the
efficiency-aware residual coverage

```math
N_{m}(S)=r_{m}(S)\,[v_{h}-v_{\rm miss}+\eta(S)b]-\eta(S)d_{m}(S). \tag{9}
```

If instead $`S^{+}`$ shifts these conditional means by $`\delta v_{h}`$,
$`\delta v_{\rm miss}`$, and $`\delta b`$, then, with $`p=p(S)`$ and
$`r=r_{m}(S)`$,

```math
G_{m}(S)=N_{m}(S)+(p+r)\,\delta v_{h}+(1-p-r)\bigl[\delta v_{\rm miss}-\eta(S)\,\delta b\bigr]. \tag{10}
```

###### Proof.

$`S^{+}`$ hits with probability $`p+r`$. By total expectation,
$`\mathbb{E}[V_{S^{+}}-V_{S}]=r(v_{h}-v_{\rm miss})+(p+r)\delta v_{h}+(1-p-r)\delta v_{\rm miss}`$
and $`\mathbb{E}[D_{S^{+}}-D_{S}]=d_{m}(S)-rb+(1-p-r)\delta b`$.
Substituting into Eq. (8) gives Eq. (10), and with no shifts
$`G_{m}(S)=N_{m}(S)`$. ∎

### A.8 Retries and the Value of Routing Information

#### Planning retries.

A plan that retries after failures is a candidate, so Theorem 2 chooses
among plans; a plan’s value is computed backward over post-failure
histories. A history $`\mathfrak{h}`$ records the attempts so far and
their feedback. For a next attempt $`u`$, let $`a(\mathfrak{h},u)`$ be
its incremental cost, including verification and its longer context,
$`p_{\theta}(\mathfrak{h},u)`$ its acceptance probability, and
$`v_{\rm acc}(\mathfrak{h},u)`$ its accepted value, all conditional on
$`\mathfrak{h}`$, and let
$`J^{\rm direct}(\mathfrak{h})=\mathbb{E}[Y_{B}-\lambda C_{B}\mid\mathfrak{h}]`$
be the return of calling the reference now.

###### Proposition 8 (Planning on post-failure states).

The optimal remaining return $`\Pi`$ equals $`J^{\rm direct}`$ at the
horizon and satisfies

```math
\Pi(\mathfrak{h})=\max\Bigl\{J^{\rm direct}(\mathfrak{h}),\ \max_{u}\bigl[-\lambda a(\mathfrak{h},u)+p_{\theta}(\mathfrak{h},u)v_{\rm acc}(\mathfrak{h},u)+(1-p_{\theta}(\mathfrak{h},u))\,\mathbb{E}[\Pi(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})\mid\mathfrak{h},u,H_{\theta}=0]\bigr]\Bigr\}, \tag{11}
```

where $`\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}`$ extends $`\mathfrak{h}`$ by the rejected
attempt. An attempt is optimal if and only if it attains the maximum, so
its success probability enters only as conditioned on the history that
reaches it.

###### Proof.

Stopping returns $`J^{\rm direct}(\mathfrak{h})`$. Attempting $`u`$
costs $`a(\mathfrak{h},u)`$; with probability
$`p_{\theta}(\mathfrak{h},u)`$ the task ends with value
$`v_{\rm acc}(\mathfrak{h},u)`$, and otherwise it continues from
$`\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}`$, whose best continuation is worth
$`\Pi(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})`$. Since $`J_{\lambda}`$ is linear in value
and cost, attempting and then continuing optimally is worth the bracket,
a randomized choice cannot beat the better branch, and backward
induction from the horizon gives Eq. (11). ∎

Planning on marginal success rates replaces
$`p_{\theta}(\mathfrak{h},u)`$ by the unconditional rate of $`u`$, which
overstates later attempts when failures are positively correlated.

#### Buying routing information.

Let accepted outputs and the fallback preserve value, let the primary be
sufficient with probability $`s`$, and let $`0<a<b`$ be the costs of the
primary and of the fallback. Without information the router incurs the
lower of $`a+(1-s)b`$ (attempt, then escalate on failure) and $`b`$
(direct fallback). A signal $`Z`$ that moves the posterior to $`s_{Z}`$
saves, before its price,

```math
\min\{a+(1-s)b,\,b\}-\mathbb{E}\min\{a+(1-s_{Z})b,\,b\}, \tag{12}
```

and its _capture_ is this saving divided by the bound below.

###### Proposition 9 (The value of routing information).

For every signal $`Z`$, the saving (12) is at most
$`\min\{s(b-a),a(1-s)\}`$, which exact revelation of the sufficiency
event attains. A signal priced at $`k`$ is therefore worth buying only
if $`k<a(1-s)\leq a`$: it avoids at most one unsuccessful attempt per
task, whatever its accuracy.

###### Proof.

Put $`f(t)=\min\{a+(1-t)b,b\}`$ and $`h(t)=ta+(1-t)b`$. As a minimum of
two affine functions, $`f`$ is concave, and $`f\geq h`$ on $`[0,1]`$,
since $`f-h`$ is the smaller of $`a(1-t)`$ and $`t(b-a)`$. Since $`h`$
is affine and $`\mathbb{E}[s_{Z}]=s`$,
$`h(s)=\mathbb{E}[h(s_{Z})]\leq\mathbb{E}[f(s_{Z})]`$, so the saving
$`f(s)-\mathbb{E}[f(s_{Z})]`$ is at most
$`f(s)-h(s)=\min\{s(b-a),a(1-s)\}`$. Exact revelation has
$`s_{Z}\in\{0,1\}`$, where $`f=h`$, so it attains the bound; this is the
expected value of perfect information for two candidates and a binary
state \[59, 60\]. ∎

### A.9 Router Memory

A record store refines the router’s state with past outcomes. In an
idealized version, tasks fall into $`J`$ cells that share a record;
ACRouter’s embedding-keyed store \[4\] is a richer instance. Cells
arrive independently with probabilities $`\nu_{j}>0`$. At the start of a
round, each cell is uninformed ($`U`$) or holds an informative ($`I`$)
or erroneous ($`W`$) record. After the round, each record expires with
probability $`\delta_{j}`$, and the arriving cell receives an
informative write with probability $`\omega_{j}`$ or an erroneous one
with probability $`\psi_{j}`$, replacing its record; write types are
independent of the past. Put $`r_{j}=\omega_{j}+\psi_{j}>0`$ and
$`\alpha_{j}=(1-\delta_{j})(1-\nu_{j}r_{j})<1`$. Let $`v_{j}^{s}`$ and
$`c_{j}^{s}`$ be the router’s expected value and cost in cell $`j`$ and
record state $`s\in\{U,I,W\}`$, including the record update. Records are
measured against the uninformed router, which writes at the same cost
but routes as if uninformed and has efficiency
$`\eta_{U}=\sum_{j}\nu_{j}v_{j}^{U}/\sum_{j}\nu_{j}c_{j}^{U}`$, by the
gains $`g_{j}^{s}=(v_{j}^{s}-v_{j}^{U})-\eta_{U}(c_{j}^{s}-c_{j}^{U})`$.

###### Proposition 10 (Stationary memory gain).

Let $`\chi_{j}=\nu_{j}r_{j}/(1-\alpha_{j})`$ be the stationary coverage
of cell $`j`$ and $`e_{j}=\psi_{j}/r_{j}`$ its erroneous write fraction.
The stationary efficiency $`\eta_{\infty}`$ satisfies
$`\eta_{\infty}\geq\eta_{U}`$ if and only if

```math
\sum_{j}\nu_{j}\chi_{j}\bigl[(1-e_{j})g_{j}^{I}+e_{j}g_{j}^{W}\bigr]\ \geq\ 0. \tag{13}
```

If $`g_{j}^{I}>0>g_{j}^{W}`$, cell $`j`$ contributes positively if and
only if $`e_{j}<g_{j}^{I}/(g_{j}^{I}-g_{j}^{W})`$.

###### Proof.

A record before round $`n+1`$ was written in round $`n`$ or survived it,
so the probabilities of informative and erroneous records satisfy
$`i_{j,n+1}=\nu_{j}\omega_{j}+\alpha_{j}i_{j,n}`$ and
$`w_{j,n+1}=\nu_{j}\psi_{j}+\alpha_{j}w_{j,n}`$, with stationary values
$`i_{j}=\chi_{j}(1-e_{j})`$ and $`w_{j}=\chi_{j}e_{j}`$. Conditioning on
the arriving cell, the stationary surplus per round is
$`\mathbb{E}[V-\eta_{U}D]=\sum_{j}\nu_{j}\bigl[v_{j}^{U}-\eta_{U}c_{j}^{U}+i_{j}g_{j}^{I}+w_{j}g_{j}^{W}\bigr]`$,
whose uninformed terms sum to zero by the definition of $`\eta_{U}`$.
The result is the left side of Eq. (13), and it equals
$`\bar{c}_{\infty}(\eta_{\infty}-\eta_{U})`$ for the stationary mean
cost $`\bar{c}_{\infty}>0`$. The cell condition follows from
$`(1-e_{j})g_{j}^{I}+e_{j}g_{j}^{W}=g_{j}^{I}-e_{j}(g_{j}^{I}-g_{j}^{W})`$.
∎

### B.2 Splits

The full set has 104,050 decisions over 25 benchmarks, split 70/10/20
with seed 42 by source group, so identical inputs and items from one
source, such as the questions shared by MMMU-Pro and
VLM-ExecRouterBench, never cross splits; existing research splits are
kept, including RoboTwin’s split by task family (35 training, 5

development, and 21,069 test records; the router studies of Section 4
use the first release of 18 benchmarks (Table 2).

### B.3 Routers

Fable 5.1, a retired model, is excluded from every evaluation; routers
trained with it play Opus 5.5 in its place.

#### Capability-tree router.

Leaves are defined per task and labeled on training inputs by GPT-6.1
Sol, which sees the input, the output requirement, and the leaf
definitions, but no candidate outcomes. The classifier is Qwen3.5-4B
with LoRA (rank 8), trained for three epochs on all 18 benchmarks at
once with batch 16 and learning rates $`10^{-4}`$ (adapter) and
$`10^{-3}`$ (head); the development split selects the epoch. The leaf
table is fitted on training inputs by least squares over paired
candidate differences on shared inputs.

#### Outcome and type routers on 11 benchmarks.

Both use Qwen3.5-4B with LoRA (rank 16, $`\alpha=32`$) on every linear
layer of the language model, a final-token linear head, AdamW with
learning rates $`2\times 10^{-5}`$ (adapter) and $`3\times 10^{-4}`$
(head), 32 tasks per update, and six epochs, with seeds 42, 13, 2026,
and 7. The outcome router reads one (input, candidate profile) pair per
forward pass; its loss is the cross-entropy against the normalized
outcome plus four times the squared error of within-task score
differences. The type router predicts one of 68 capability types and
reads a global type-to-candidate table fitted on training inputs.

#### Robot router.

The outcome router uses Qwen3.5-4B with LoRA (rank 8), seven sigmoid
heads, three epochs, batch 16, and learning rates $`10^{-4}`$ (adapter)
and $`10^{-3}`$ (head); development routing success selects the epoch.
The winner-label classifier shares the backbone, inputs, and data, with
a seven-way softmax over the policy that succeeds, ties broken by
measured inference time.

#### Image-routing suite.

Thirty-one router designs, among them RouterDC \[35\], SCOPE \[19\],
IRT-based routers, $`k`$NN, MLP, and two-tower scorers, and Qwen3.5
label routers with 0.8B to 9B parameters, are trained on GenEval2 or
Qwen-Image-Bench prompts and tested on the same or the other benchmark:
124 configurations, with three seeds for stochastic methods. Each is
compared with the fixed generator by a paired bootstrap over prompts.

#### Untrained router.

Qwen3.5-4B, prompted with the input and the candidates, names the
candidate to run.

#### Winner-label routers on 16 benchmarks.

Qwen3.5-4B is fully fine-tuned to emit the label of the best candidate,
ties broken by cost, tokens, and a fixed order. The 12 recipes start
from the instruction-tuned or a continually pretrained model, with or
without fine-tuning on public routing data, and train per benchmark, on
all benchmarks at once, or both in sequence. Tables 7 and 8 give
per-benchmark results of the capability-tree router and of the
11-benchmark study.

### C.1 Data

CodeRouterBench \[4\] gives outcomes and token prices for eight models
(claude-opus-4-6, claude-sonnet-4-6, gpt-5.4, Qwen3-Max, Kimi-K2.5,

forecast are fresh calls through one OpenAI-compatible relay, with

main text reports quality changes in points. Intervals are 95%
percentile task-bootstrap intervals, paired for policy comparisons, and
efficiency is a ratio of averages.

### C.3 Portfolio Selection

Candidates are fitted on probing and evaluated on test tasks.
CodeRouterBench adds one of seven candidates, each with at least 20
passes and 20 failures, to portfolios of up to three models; RouterBench
adds ten candidates to 21 initial portfolios over ten fixed 70/30
splits. The selecting verifier is an oracle that commits to the best
passing member, else calls the reference, and every member call is
charged. Paired selection picks the candidate with the highest fitted
$`\hat{\eta}(S^{+})`$ (Eq. (8)); the controls rank residual coverage
$`N_{m}(S)`$ (Eq. (9)), an independence proxy that replaces $`r_{m}(S)`$
by $`(1-p(S))\mathbb{P}(E_{m})`$, the saving score $`r_{m}(S)b-d_{m}`$,
or marginal success (Table 13). Residual coverage evaluates the quality
term at the current portfolio’s conditional means, and those means
shift: adding a model lowers the miss-conditional value in 97% of
CodeRouterBench comparisons, which Eq. (10) restores. Adding
Qwen3.5-Plus to the pooled Qwen3-Max portfolio, for example, is
predicted to lose 1.4 score per dollar but gains $`+5.0`$ $`[3.5,6.6]`$,
because the fallback cost of the new failure set falls by 14%.
Cluster-bootstrap intervals for the differences in best-candidate
frequency, resampling CodeRouterBench dimensions or RouterBench source
families, all include zero. Over ten CodeRouterBench resplits, mean
relative regret at $`\theta=1`$ and $`0.5`$ is 0.04% and 0.06% for
paired selection, 0.29% and 0.05% for residual coverage, and 13.1% and
17.0% for marginal success.

### C.4 Verifiers and Overheads

On recorded responses of three primaries to 500 probing and 334 runnable
test tasks, we compare a static _Compiler_ gate, which compiles the
extracted answer and the official harness to bytecode and checks the
declared entry point without executing code; visible examples; hidden
tests, an oracle-like control; the released-score oracle; and a grok-4.6
judge on two primaries, charged at the primary’s blended token price or
at its list price of \$2 and \$6 per million input and output tokens
(Table 14). Holding outputs and the threshold fixed, the judge improves
efficiency exactly when its mean charge is below
$`\bar{V}_{\theta}/\eta_{B}-\bar{D}_{0,\theta}`$, where
$`\bar{V}_{\theta}`$ is the mean delivered score and
$`\bar{D}_{0,\theta}`$ the mean cost before the judge. For Qwen3-Max at
$`\theta=0.5`$ this break-even charge is \$0.00330 per task
$`[\$0.00285,\$0.00379]`$; at $`\theta=1`$ it is negative for both
primaries, so even a free judge does not help. Accepting every Qwen3-Max
output reaches $`19.65\times`$ but loses $`Q=-0.063`$
$`[-0.108,-0.024]`$, which the floor excludes.

Router inference is timed on one Apple M4 core and priced at AWS
Lambda’s x86 list price for a 2 GB function (\$$`3.33\times 10^{-5}`$
per second, us-east-1, September 2026). Per task, the TF-IDF router
takes 1.9 ms (0.06% of a Qwen3-Max call), a hidden-test sandbox run
0.15 s and \$$`5.2\times 10^{-6}`$ (2.0%), the grok-4.6 judge 39.0 s and
\$0.0159 at list price ($`63\times`$), and the DeepSeek-v4-pro forecast
1.89 s and \$0.000944 ($`2.4\times`$); charging router overhead leaves
every replay comparison unchanged at the reported precision. Opus takes
3.93 s per task against 6.23 s for Qwen3-Max, so in sequential seconds
direct dispatch reaches only $`0.58\times`$ (Qwen3-Max) and
$`0.50\times`$ (Kimi-K2.5) Opus’s efficiency, verified cascades
$`0.40`$–$`0.54\times`$, and the judge’s 39-second call lowers the ratio
to $`0.08\times`$.

### C.5 Routing Signals

The released CodeRouterBench routers expose their chosen model, which we
use as a binary signal $`Z`$ (chose Qwen3-Max), with posteriors
cross-fitted over ten half-splits of the test set; capture is the gross
saving divided by the perfect-information bound of Proposition 9 (Table
15). Classical routers have negative native capture because their
$`Z=0`$ tasks still satisfy $`\mathbb{P}(H=1\mid Z=0)>a/b=0.043`$, so
withholding the attempt forgoes profitable ones. For the LLM forecast,
DeepSeek-v4-pro reads the prompt and predicts whether Qwen3-Max and
Kimi-K2.5 pass all hidden tests on 600 probing and 600 test tasks; its
calibrated posterior reaches test AUROC 0.541 and 0.618 and a capture of
$`-0.037`$ $`[-0.225,0.077]`$, and at list price it costs more than the
attempt it could save in 22 of 24 states.

### C.6 Router Memory

This semi-synthetic study combines real outputs and verifier judgments
with constructed arrivals and retention: 2,044 probing and 867 test
tasks in 12 cells. With probability 0.25 the writer explores a uniformly
sampled model, and it writes the model’s identity to the arriving cell
when verification accepts; the newest record serves later tasks in that
cell, and uncovered cells use the prior, always-Opus or the
probing-fitted DimensionBest, which sends each dimension to its best
model. Writers use ACRouter’s doctest and syntax tools, locally executed
hidden tests, or a DeepSeek-v4-pro judge; we report hidden-test writes.
A write is erroneous when the accepted output is incorrect under the
released score; against that truth, hidden-test writes have TPR/FPR
0.960/0.205. Fifty streams of 60,000 rounds are measured over their last
10,000 rounds. With no expiration, the store reaches 135.8 score per
dollar but scores 0.034 below always-Opus and 0.116 below DimensionBest.

## Appendix D Limitations

The theory takes the law of states as given, so it does not cover states
that depend on the router’s own earlier choices, such as SciCode steps
that contain a model’s earlier code, and certification needs the
reference’s outcome on every certification input. Moreover, step-level
routing for long-horizon agentic tasks is more complex and deserves
deeper exploration.
