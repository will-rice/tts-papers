# Introduction

Speech and language are fundamental to human communication . Decades of neuroscience research have progressively characterized the neural pathways underlying speech perception and production, from early lesion-based localization accounts such as the classical findings of Broca and Wernicke and the connectionist framework proposed by Geschwind , to contemporary dual-stream models distinguishing ventral and dorsal pathways for speech comprehension and sensorimotor integration . These advances have established an understanding of how speech is represented and generated in the human brain.

More recent work has examined speech production at increasingly fine-grained resolutions, including single-neuron-level recordings . These breakthroughs, enabled by modern neural recording and surgical technologies, not only provide a deeper understanding of the human brain, but also create a new form of human communication, one in which we directly communicate our thoughts through neural impulses , an idea central to the development of _speech Brain–Computer Interfaces_ (speechBCIs). A clinical realization of this vision is the _speech neuroprosthesis_ (SN) , designed to restore communication for individuals who have lost the ability to speak due to conditions such as _amyotrophic lateral sclerosis_ (ALS) or paralysis. Current SN systems mostly rely on signals recorded from the motor cortex, reflecting articulatory motor intentions.

Most speechBCIs, including SN, adopt a cascaded paradigm that first decodes text from neural activity and subsequently synthesizes speech using an independent speech generator. While this architecture improves linguistic fluency through strong language model (LM) priors, it introduces important limitations. LMs with strong linguistic priors can improve fluency and grammatical coherence, but may also override neural evidence. Moreover, multi-stage processing pipelines introduce computational latency. Since speechBCIs are intended to function as primary communication devices , they must operate at human conversational rates without noticeable delays. In practice, such cascaded architectures:

1.  Increase inference latency.

2.  Propagate compounding errors across components.

Thus, they limit responsiveness in real-time communication.

In parallel, speech processing has moved toward unified end-to-end architectures in tasks such as automatic speech recognition (ASR) and speech-to-speech translation (SST) , reducing latency and mitigating compounding errors. These developments motivate us to explore whether a structured single-stage neural-to-speech model can balance intelligibility and latency in speechBCIs. However, unlike ASR and SST, speechBCIs are an emerging field with very limited data to train large-vocabulary models, making fully end-to-end training particularly challenging.

In this paper, we introduce a phoneme-informed bottleneck that bridges a brain content encoder and a speech decoder, enabling single-stage inference for neural-to-speech synthesis. The bottleneck leverages frame-level phoneme logits as an intermediate linguistic representation, providing linguistic guidance while avoiding explicit text decoding. To support data-efficient alignment between neural activity and _contextual phoneme representations_ (CPRs) from the TTS text encoder, we incorporate a lightweight and differentiable hidden Markov model (HMM) alignment module, with transitions and emissions parameterized by neural networks.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

- We introduce **Brain2Speech-Net**, a linguistically-informed neural-to-speech architecture that enables single-stage speech synthesis from intracortical brain signals, without intermediate text decoding.

- We incorporate phoneme-aware representations as a differentiable linguistic bottleneck, preserving phonemic structure while avoiding explicit text generation.

- We develop a lightweight differentiable deep-HMM that learns monotonic alignment between long neural recordings and contextual phoneme representations in a pre-trained TTS latent space, leveraging decoder acoustic priors for intelligible speech generation.

- Brain2Speech-Net enables intelligible, real-time speech, whereas cascaded models introduce latency and direct-speech-unit models remain largely unintelligible in limited-data settings.

To support reproducibility, we release code and pretrained models of Brain2Speech-Net[^1].

# Related work

## SpeechBCI task settings

Advances in neural activity recording has enabled decoding of speech and language representations directly from brain activity . Research in neurolinguistics has identified neural correlates of speech generation across different motor intentions ranging from imagined to attempted to overt speech . These modes are clearly compared in Fig. <a href="#fig:speech_modes" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:speech_modes">1</a>. In particular, speech neuroprostheses is intended to work in cases where there is attempted speech, in which the user plans an utterance and drives the motor cortex but cannot complete the articulation . This setting is dictated by the target population: users with ALS or anarthria retain speech-motor intent yet can no longer vocalize. Attempted speech also elicits stronger and more reliable motor-cortex activity than imagined speech, whose weak and variable drive is harder to decode . These properties make attempted speech the most practical setting for restoring communication, and we adopt it in this work. However, in this setting the participant produces no intelligible speech, so no aligned acoustic target exists for training. Most systems therefore sidestep this by decoding text as an intermediate representation, a paradigm known as brain-to-text decoding.

## Brain-to-text decoding

Brain-to-text decoding has been demonstrated across multiple recording modalities, including electrocorticography (ECoG) , fMRI , MEG , and fNIRS , with neural signals shown to encode phonetic, lexical, and articulatory information. More recently, single-neuron recordings using micro-electrode arrays (MEA) in motor cortex have revealed fine-grained phonetic representations . Building on these findings, the Brain-to-Text benchmark gave rise to a number of competitive spoken content decoding methods. Non-invasive work spans a range of task settings. From MEG, Dash et al. decode a closed set of five imagined and spoken phrases with convolutional networks over spectral-temporal features. Moving toward open vocabulary, Jayalath et al. extend MEG word classification to sequence-level decoding through language-model rescoring and predictive in-filling of out-of-vocabulary words. Notably, much of the performance improvement in these systems has been shown to emerge from language modeling rather than improvements in neural signal extraction .

## Brain-to-speech decoding

Closely related to text decoding of spoken content is the direct decoding of speech from neural signals. While most speech neuroprosthesis systems rely on first decoding text and then synthesizing speech using a text-to-speech system, more recent methods have explored direct speech synthesis .

BrainTalker addresses the low-resource problem of brain-to-speech synthesis by using a transfer learning approach with a self-supervised speech latent representation. Metzger et al.  use ECoG features and a bidirectional RNN to generate frame-level discrete speech units, which are then decoded into speech using a neural vocoder, achieving approximately $`25\%`$ WER on a 1024-word vocabulary. Wairagkar et al.  demonstrate the presence of prosody-related information in intracortical activity and highlight the advantages of retaining paralinguistic information in the generated speech. In this paper, we further explore direct speech synthesis and emphasize its importance for low-latency neural communication.

# Intracortical Neural Recordings and Preprocessing

We use the speech neuroprosthesis dataset released by Willett et al. , which contains intracortical recordings and parallel text transcriptions from a participant with ALS during prompted attempted speech. As the participant can no longer produce intelligible speech, these recordings capture the motor intentions underlying speech rather than its acoustic realization. Neural activity was recorded using four implanted microelectrode arrays and processed into binned threshold crossings and spike band power features across 512 channels. Willett et al.  found that activity in the motor cortex correlated strongly with phonetic content, and we therefore use the 256 motor-cortex channels, following prior work. As no intelligible acoustic recordings are available from the participant, we synthesize pseudo-speech targets from the prompted text using a pre-trained single-speaker VITS TTS model [^2], a high-quality end-to-end speech synthesis model. We intentionally select VITS as it does not use deterministic duration prediction, introducing stochasticity into the speech targets. This yields 8,800 training and 880 test utterances (5.71 hours total). Data statistics are summarized in Table <a href="#tab:data" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="tab:data">1</a>. These synthesized waveforms serve as training targets for neural-to-speech modeling.

# Brain2Speech-Net

Brain2Speech-Net is a single-stage neural-to-speech model that uses phoneme-aware representations as a differentiable linguistic bottleneck, as shown in Figure <a href="#framework" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="framework">2</a>. Intracortical recordings contain phonetic information but are limited in scale, while pre-trained TTS models provide strong acoustic priors from large speech corpora. By predicting phoneme-aware representations from neural signals, the model preserves neural evidence while aligning efficiently to _contextual phoneme representations_ (CPRs) in the TTS text-encoder latent space. Brain2Speech-Net is trained in two phases. In Phase 1, the brain content encoder is pre-trained to produce phoneme-aware representations from neural activity. In Phase 2, the full model, including the brain content encoder, is trained jointly to synthesize speech. Concretely, in Phase 1 Brain2Speech-Net maps time-binned neural features to frame-level phoneme-aware representations, while in Phase 2 it predicts a sequence of contextual phoneme representation (CPR) segments. A lightweight differentiable deep-HMM enforces monotonic alignment between neural frames and CPR segments, enabling training without frame-aligned targets.

At inference, speech is generated in one pass—neural signals $`\rightarrow`$ CPRs $`\rightarrow`$ waveform—without explicit text decoding.

**Phoneme bottleneck.** Frame-level posterior probability distributions over phoneme classes, originally introduced in automatic speech recognition (ASR) as outputs of acoustic models trained to predict phonetic units . Because phoneme representations provide a speaker-independent representation of linguistic content while largely discarding acoustic and prosodic variability, they have been widely adopted in voice conversion and low-resource speech generation scenarios . In Brain2Speech-Net, phoneme representations serve as a differentiable linguistic bottleneck between neural activity and speech synthesis. Intracortical recordings contain phonetic information but are limited in scale, whereas pre-trained TTS models encode strong acoustic priors learned from large speech corpora. By predicting frame-level posterior phoneme distributions from neural signals, we obtain a structured and uncertainty-aware representation that preserves neural evidence while enabling efficient alignment to contextual phoneme representations in the TTS latent space.

## Phase 1: Pre-training the brain content encoder

The brain content encoder takes time-binned neural features ($`\mathbf{x}_{0:T-1}`$) and the session-specific embedding ($`\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{session}}`$) as input and produces frame-level representations $`\mathbf{n}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{bc}}`$. The encoder is trained using the connectionist temporal classification (CTC) loss , with the ground-truth phoneme sequence ($`\mathbf{y}`$) as supervision.

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CTC}}
=
-\log p\!\left(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{x}_{0:T-1}, \mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{session}}\right)
```

The CTC objective enables learning frame-level phoneme-aware representations without requiring explicit alignment between neural frames and phoneme targets. To account for distribution shifts across recording sessions, we incorporate a learned session embedding that conditions the encoder on the recording session . After Phase 1, the encoder produces phoneme-aware representations that serve as input to the alignment module in Phase 2.

## Phase 2: Deep-HMM alignment

In Phase 2, we learn an alignment between the long neural representation sequence $`\mathbf{x}_{0:T-1}`$ and a much shorter sequence of contextual phoneme representations (CPRs) $`{\mathbf{z}_{0:N-1}}`$ extracted from the VITS text encoder. Our alignment model is motivated by prior work on neural-network parameterized HMMs for data-efficient monotonic alignment . We adopt a left-to-right, no-skip _hidden Markov model_ (HMM) to model monotonic alignment between neural frames and phoneme segments. Each HMM state corresponds to a phoneme segment position in the target CPR sequence (rather than a phoneme class), enabling segment-level alignment under limited data conditions. The brain content encoder is further fine-tuned during this phase. We use the VITS text encoder to obtain target CPR latents, as it parametrizes a conditional prior over latent speech representations during TTS training, providing a probabilistically well-defined and acoustically robust target space.

First, we map the neural frame representations ($`\mathbf{x}_{0:T-1}`$) to phoneme-aware representations ($`\mathbf{n}_{0:I-1}`$) using the brain content encoder followed by mapping to the frame embedding space ($`\mathbf{e}_i`$) by the brain frame encoder.

```math
\mathbf{n}_{0:I-1}
= \mathrm{BrainContentEnc}_{\psi}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T-1}, \mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{session}}),
```

```math
\mathbf{e}_i
= \mathrm{BrainFrameEnc}_{\theta}(\mathbf{n}_i)
```

The sequence $`\mathbf{e}_{0:I-1}`$ forms the observation sequence for the deep-HMM aligner, which assigns each neural time frame to a corresponding phoneme segment in the CPR sequence.\
**States and emissions.** Let $`k \in \{0, \ldots, N-1, N_{\mathrm{EOS}}\}`$ index phoneme segments, where $`N_{\mathrm{EOS}}`$ is a self-absorbing end state. Each state has an embedding $`\mathbf{v}_k`$ drawn from a learned lookup table. For each segment $`k`$, we define a reference vector $`\mathbf{c}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{state}}`$. During training, $`\mathbf{c}_k`$ is obtained by projecting the ground-truth CPR latent $`\mathbf{z}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{cpr}}}`$:

```math
\mathbf{c}_k = \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{tgt}} \mathbf{z}_k,
```

where $`\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{tgt}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{state} \times d_{\mathrm{cpr}}}`$ is learned. During inference, ground-truth CPRs are unavailable. Instead, $`\mathbf{c}_k`$ is obtained from a learned emission prototype table $`\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{K_{\max} \times d_{state}}`$ as $`\mathbf{c}_k = \mathbf{P}[k]`$, where $`K_{\max}`$ is the maximum number of latent alignment states per utterance. Frame embeddings are projected via $`\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{state} \times d_f}`$. The log-emission score for state $`k`$ at index $`i`$ is

```math
\log E_i(k) = - \left\lVert \mathbf{A}\mathbf{e}_i - \mathbf{c}_k \right\rVert^2.
```

**Transitions.** At index $`i`$, state $`k`$ may remain in $`k`$, advance to $`k{+}1`$, or transition to $`\mathrm{EOS}`$. Transition probabilities are predicted by a neural network $`h_\theta`$:

```math
\tau^{\mathrm{adv}}_{i,k}, \; \tau^{\mathrm{stop}}_{i,k}
= h_\theta(\mathbf{e}_i, \mathbf{v}_k),
```

```math
\tau^{\mathrm{stay}}_{i,k}
= 1 - \tau^{\mathrm{adv}}_{i,k} - \tau^{\mathrm{stop}}_{i,k}.
```

The model is constrained to enter $`\mathrm{EOS}`$ only from the final segment $`N{-}1`$.

## Forward–backward inference.

Forward–backward inference computes posterior state occupancies $`\gamma_{i,k}`$ using forward variables $`\alpha_i(k)`$, which accumulate evidence from past observations up to index $`i`$, and backward variables $`\beta_i(k)`$, which accumulate evidence from future observations until termination at $`\mathsf{EOS}`$ . These posteriors define a soft alignment that is used to pool frame embeddings into phoneme segment representations. The posterior of segment $`k`$ at index $`i`$ is

```math
\gamma_{i,k}
=
\frac{\alpha_i(k)\beta_i(k)}
{\sum_j \alpha_i(j)\beta_i(j)}.
```

## Posterior pooling and segment prediction.

The frame embeddings are passed to the _Posterior-Pooled Segment Predictor_ (PPSP), which first performs $`\gamma_{i,k}`$-weighted pooling from the deep-HMM to produce a sequence aligned in length with the VITS encoder targets and then predicts CPRs $`\hat{\mathbf{z}}`$. Training minimizes a _mean squared error_ (MSE) loss between $`\hat{\mathbf{z}}`$ and the ground-truth latents $`\mathbf{z}`$ over valid segments, with gradients propagated end-to-end through the soft alignments.\

```math
\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathrm{k}}
=
\mathrm{PPSP}_{\phi}\ \left(
\sum_{i=0}^{I-1} \gamma_{i,k}\,\mathbf{e}_i
\right),
\qquad k = 0,\ldots,N-1
```

```math
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}
&=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}
\left\lVert
\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{k}
-
\mathbf{z}_{k}
\right\rVert^2, \\
\\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{len}}
&=
- \log p(N \mid \mathbf{n}_{0:I-1}).
\end{aligned}
```

where $`N`$ is the number of valid phoneme segments and $`k`$ are the indices of the phoneme segments.

As $`N`$ is unknown at inference, an auxiliary segment count predictor is trained using a cross-entropy loss ($`L_{len}`$). In addition, a CTC loss is applied via a linear head over the brain content encoder features to prevent degradation of the learned linguistic bottleneck; gradients from both the CTC and segment count prediction losses are detached from the alignment objective and optimized independently. At inference, the predicted $`N`$ determines the HMM state sequence; emissions use learned prototypes, and predicted CPRs are passed to the VITS decoder for single-stage neural-to-speech synthesis.

# Experiments

## Data

We use the intracortical speech neuroprosthesis dataset of Willett et al. . From the official training split, 90% is used for training and 10% for validation. Following prior work in neural speech decoding , we evaluate in both _closed-set_ and _open-set_ conditions. In the closed-set setting, we uniformly sample 880 utterances from the training set across all recording sessions. This setting is particularly relevant for speech-BCI applications, where users often repeat previously practiced or frequently used phrases during daily communication. In the open-set setting, utterances are unseen during training, and we use the official test split which contains 880 utterances and is balanced across recording sessions.

## Model and training details

The brain content encoder is a 5-layer GRU applied to windowed neural features (kernel length $`32`$, stride $`2`$), producing phoneme-aware representations $`\mathbf{n}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{bc}}`$ with $`d_{bc}{=}41`$. The encoder is fine-tuned jointly with the Phase 2 alignment components using a lower learning rate ($`10^{-5}`$). The brain frame encoder maps $`\mathbf{n}_i`$ to embeddings $`\mathbf{e}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_f}`$ with $`d_f{=}256`$ using a linear layer followed by three residual MLP blocks (dropout $`p{=}0.1`$). The segment count predictor is a three-layer MLP ($`256 \rightarrow 128 \rightarrow K_{\max}`$, ReLU, dropout $`p{=}0.1`$) applied to an attention-pooled summary of phoneme-aware representations. Transitions are parameterized by a two-hidden-layer MLP ($`512 \rightarrow 256`$). The PPSP consists of posterior-weighted pooling followed by a two-layer segment head, a four-layer Transformer encoder ($`d{=}192`$, 4 heads, FFN dim $`768`$), and a residual refinement MLP. Per-state emission prototypes $`\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{K_{\max} \times d_{state}}`$ $`(d_{state} = 192)`$ are initialized via $`k`$-means clustering ($`k{=}K_{\max}`$, 10 initializations) on projected training CPR latents $`\{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{tgt}}\mathbf{z}_k\}`$ and re-clustered every 5 epochs. The auxiliary CTC loss is weighted by $`\lambda_{\mathrm{CTC}}{=}0.1`$. All parameters are optimized with Adam (batch size $`32`$, peak learning rate $`10^{-3}`$) using a 500-step linear warm-up.

## Baselines

We compare against cascaded brain-to-text-to-speech (NTS) pipelines and a direct neural-to-speech baseline. We use the same brain content encoder across the board. _NTS-Cascade (5-gram)_ decodes the phoneme-aware representations from the brain content encoder to text using a 5-gram language model (125k-word vocabulary trained on Switchboard text) with beam width 18, followed by VITS speech synthesis. _NTS-Cascade (5-gram + LLM re-rank)_ extends this pipeline by re-ranking a 100-best list from the 5-gram decoder using a large language model (OPT-8B ) before speech synthesis. _Direct-DSU synthesis_ predicts HuBERT discrete speech units directly from neural features using a bidirectional RNN and generates waveforms with a neural vocoder , following prior direct brain-to-speech unit approaches.

While discrete-unit-based approaches can perform well with large training datasets, relying solely on acoustic unit prediction under limited data often produces low-quality and unintelligible speech due to the lack of explicit linguistic structure.

## Evaluation metrics

We evaluate intelligibility using phoneme error rate (PER)  and word error rate (WER). PER is computed from phoneme sequences extracted from generated and reference speech using Wav2Vec 2.0-Large-LV60 fine-tuned for phoneme recognition, while WER is computed from transcriptions produced by the Whisper-medium ASR model . Spectral distortion is measured using mel-cepstral distortion (MCD) after DTW alignment, and latency is measured using real-time factor (RTF). For cascaded baselines, we also report WER on the decoded text against the ground-truth transcript (_Text_ WER). Subjective Mean Opinion Score (MOS) listening tests are also conducted.

## Closed-Set Intelligibility

On the closed set, Brain2Speech-Net substantially outperforms the direct single-stage DSU baseline, reducing PER from 0.758 to 0.109, WER from 1.011 to 0.068, and MCD from 6.004 to 4.632. These gains show that the phoneme-bottleneck approach is far more data-efficient than direct acoustic unit prediction. Although cascaded NTS models achieve the best absolute scores, Brain2Speech-Net approaches their spectral quality (MCD 4.632 vs. 4.415) and remains competitive in PER (0.109 vs. 0.078) and WER (0.068 vs. 0.059) while avoiding explicit text decoding. Notably, the ground-truth speech reference achieves a WER of 0.021, indicating that the remaining performance gap is approaching the lower bound imposed by the synthetic speech generation via TTS and the ASR-based evaluation pipeline.

## Latency

Cascaded pipelines operate above real time (RTF $`>`$ 1.6) due to sequential decoding and synthesis. Brain2Speech-Net runs at $`\sim 0.5`$, achieving faster-than-real-time generation with strong intelligibility. While DSU is faster ($`\sim`$<!-- -->0.06), its outputs are less intelligible. Thus, Brain2Speech-Net offers the most favorable accuracy–latency trade-off for deployment.

## Listening Experiments

We conducted an MOS intelligibility study with 20 English-speaking listeners on open-set utterances. Each system contributed 20 samples (80 total), presented in randomized order with the reference text; listeners rated intelligibility on a 1–5 scale (1 = poor, 5 = excellent). The listening results support our main finding. As shown in Figure. <a href="#fig:mos" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:mos">3</a>, Brain2Speech-Net achieves an MOS of 3.34, indicating that listeners found the speech reasonably intelligible and understandable. Cascaded models achieve higher intelligibility due to language modeling but operate with higher latency. In contrast, the DSU baseline runs faster but produces less intelligible speech when trained on small-scale data. Brain2Speech-Net is the only system that achieves both intelligible speech and real-time performance.

## Open-Set Generalization

On unseen utterances (Table <a href="#tab:results" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="tab:results">[tab:results]</a>), performance degrades for all models, but the gap between cascaded and single-stage systems widens. Brain2Speech-Net exhibits an increase in PER from 0.109 to 0.373 and WER from 0.068 to 0.472, reflecting the challenge of achieving generalization in an end-to-end model under limited data conditions. In contrast, NTS-Cascade (5-gram) maintains substantially lower WER (0.229), highlighting the stabilizing effect of explicit language-model decoding. Nevertheless, Brain2Speech-Net continues to significantly outperform the DSU baseline, demonstrating that the proposed differentiable phoneme interface remains robust as opposed to acoustic-only decoding. Given that the ground-truth speech reference achieves a WER of 0.025, the remaining gap highlights the challenges of learning open-vocabulary neural-to-speech mappings from limited intracortical training data.

## Compounding errors.

In the cascaded baselines, we see a clear trend of compunding errors. The neural-to-text decoder produces a given text WER, and passing that text through the downstream TTS synthesizer raises the error further. Speech WER exceeds text WER in both settings, rising from 0.037 to 0.059 on the closed set and from 0.197 to 0.229 on the open set. Each stage inherits the errors of the stage before it and cannot recover them, so the final speech is less accurate than the intermediate text it was synthesized from.

# Discussion

To our knowledge, Brain2Speech-Net is among the first single-stage neural-to-speech systems to remove text decoding while remaining intelligible for large-vocabulary decoding under the severe data constraints of intracortical speechBCIs. It is the only system in our comparison that is both intelligible and faster than real time.

We note two limitations. First, training and evaluation rely on synthesized pseudo-speech rather than real, recorded audio. The participant cannot produce intelligible speech due to their impairment, so no aligned recording of their neural activity and speech production exists. This practice is common in neural-to-speech research . However, it has a consequence: our objective metrics measure intelligibility and spectral fidelity against a synthetic reference, not a natural acoustic ground truth. They also do not capture the speaker identity or paralinguistic content of the user’s own voice. Second, open-set generalization remains a challenge. Performance degrades on unseen utterances, and the gap to cascaded pipelines widens. This reflects the stabilizing role of language-model priors, which our text-free design intentionally forgoes in exchange for low latency and freedom from compounding errors. We believe this is a fundamental data limitation, caused by the lack of large-scale neural datasets, especially intracortical data.

Despite these limitations, we believe Brain2Speech-Net takes a meaningful step toward restoring fluent, real-time communication for people with severe speech and motor impairments, for whom even short delays or rigid phrase sets impose a heavy daily burden. By demonstrating that intelligible, faster-than-real-time speech can be decoded directly from cortical activity without a text intermediary, this work points toward future assistive neuroprostheses that could give such individuals back a more natural and immediate voice; and once the underlying data limitations are overcome, we anticipate that this text-free approach can synthesize more faithful speech directly from neural activity without relying on LLM priors.

# Conclusion

We presented Brain2Speech-Net, a linguistically informed single-stage neural-to-speech framework that eliminates intermediate text decoding. By integrating a differentiable phoneme bottleneck with deep-HMM alignment into a pre-trained TTS model, the approach enables data-efficient training under limited neural data. Across objective metrics and listening evaluations, Brain2Speech-Net achieves strong intelligibility while maintaining low-latency generation. These findings establish single-stage neural-to-speech synthesis as a practical path toward intelligible communication directly from brain activity.
