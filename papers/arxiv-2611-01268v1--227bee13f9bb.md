# EZ-Ar-VC: Robust Arabic Voice Anonymization with Generalization across Dialects and Code-Switching

###### Abstract

Voice conversion (VC) models can be used to anonymize speech data while
preserving naturalness. Existing models, however, are mainly optimized
for English and other high-resource languages, and they fail to
generalize well to Arabic due to its distinct phonology. In this work,
we comprehensively evaluate existing zero-shot VC systems for Arabic
speech, identifying limitations in pronunciation preservation. We then
train an Arabic-centric model based on EZ-VC on diverse Arabic speech,
and demonstrate strong performance in intelligibility, naturalness, and
pronunciation preservation, while maintaining high speaker
dissimilarity. Through subjective evaluation, we further demonstrate its
robustness to code-switching and cross-dialectal conversion, as well as
its generalization to Arabic dialects with limited representation in the
training data. Our findings highlight the limitations of zero-shot VC
and the importance of target-language adaptation for robust voice
anonymization.

speaker anonymization, arabic, dialects, code-switching

## 1 Introduction

Speech synthesis systems have reached remarkable degrees of
intelligibility and naturalness that are indistinguishable from human
speech in the best cases. Voice Conversion (VC) systems are a subset of
speech synthesis models that can convert a source utterance to sound
like a target speaker, copying various speaker-specific characteristics.
Typically, these models work best with enough representation of the
target speaker, but recent zero-shot models have also shown competitive
performance with only a short target utterance \[8, 16, 11, 15\]. While
applications of voice conversion could be used constructively for media
and entertainment, there are several harmful use cases that could result
from target voice imitation. On the other hand, the same VC technology
could be utilized constructively as a means for Voice Anonymization (VA)
to protect user identity. In our work, we are interested in anonymizing
user data in speech datasets. Currently, large efforts in voice data
collection, particularly in medical or educational applications, are
kept proprietary due to privacy risks and the difficulty of perfectly
protecting user identities without losing important features in the
spoken utterances, such as accent and style. While existing zero-shot
models could be used for this purpose, their generalization and
robustness have not been fully demonstrated across different languages.

In this work, we rigorously evaluate VC models, particularly zero-shot
models, for Arabic speech. Arabic is a pluricentric language with
multiple regional dialects, spoken by over 300 million people as a first
language  \[12\]. While Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) is
medium-resourced, most dialctal Arabic (DA) varieties are low-resourced
and underrepresented in speech and language technologies. Arabic
speakers also code-switch with English and/or French, posing additional
challenges. Our evaluation of existing VC models exposes various
artifacts that limit their applicability for robust Arabic VA. For
instance, the recently proposed EZ-VC model \[15\], was shown to
generalize zero-shot across unseen languages. However, the evaluation
was limited to Indic and European languages. Our evaluation on Arabic
reveals that, while natural sounding, the converted speech is heavily
accented. We conduct a phoneme-level analysis and find that this
phenomenon is driven by sounds that are unique to Arabic and likely
underrepresented in the model’s training data, such as pharangeals,
emphatics, and uvulars. Other VC models also show artifacts affecting
intelligibility and naturalness.

These results underscore the importance of targeted efforts to preserve
the characteristics of spoken Arabic varieties. While some
Arabic-centric efforts exist \[24, 23\], these efforts treat VC as an
auxiliary task and do not specifically optimize for conversion and
anonymization. To close this gap, we introduce EZ-Ar-VC; a robust
Arabic-centric VA model based on EZ-VC and demonstrate its robustness
through objective and subjective evaluations. Our results show that the
resulting speech is sufficiently anonymized while preserving
intelligibility, naturalness, accent, and style. We also show that the
model generalizes to code-switching with both English and French.
EZ-Ar-VC is the first VC model shown to generalize robustly across
Arabic varieties. The model is made publicly available as an open-source
general-purpose Arabic VA tool to support privacy-preserving voice data
sharing. We provide our code, model checkpoint and demo samples here:
[https://github.com/EZ-AR-VC/EZ-AR-VC](https://github.com/EZ-AR-VC/EZ-AR-VC)

## 2 Related Work

Recent VC research has increasingly shifted toward any-to-any systems
that can generalize to unseen speakers. Approaches include discrete-unit
flow matching, such as EZ-VC \[15\], end-to-end TTS-VC hybrids such as
YourTTS \[8\], diffusion-based models such as Diff-HierVC \[11\] and
Seed-VC \[16\], and SSL retrieval/prompted vocoding, such as
kNN-VC \[6\] and Vec2wav 2.0 \[13\]. However, evaluations have
predominantly focused on English, European, and Indic languages. While
limited research in VA has addressed multilingual and code-switching
scenarios \[18, 17\], Arabic still remains largely underexplored.
ArVoice \[23\], a multi-speaker MSA corpus with seven human and four
synthetic voices, demonstrates VC, but its predominantly read MSA speech
provides limited conversational, dialectal, and code-switched coverage.
Abdullah et al  \[2\] used kNN-VC to augment dialect-identification
data; however, their evaluation targets dialect identification rather
than conversion quality. Our work fills this gap by benchmarking and
adapting VC models to Arabic and evaluating their robustness across
dialectal and code-switching variants. We provide a comprehensive
objective and subjective evaluation of speaker privacy, intelligibility,
pronunciation, naturalness, and style preservation.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Datasets

We construct our training and evaluation datasets to span MSA, dialectal
Arabic, and code-switched speech. We utilize unlabeled data for
training. For MSA, we use QASR \[19\] (2K hrs). For dialectal Arabic, we
use TunSwitch \[1\] (153 hours), covering Tunisian Arabic with instances
of code-switching with French, and ADI17 \[21\] ($`\sim`$3K hrs),
covering speech from 17 Arabic-speaking countries. For foreign
languages, we use 550 hours of LibriTTS English \[26\] and 500 hours of
French Common Voice speech \[5\].

We also utilize labeled data to construct source-speaker development and
test sets and target-speaker set, while allocating a subset of the data
for training. For MSA, we use ArVoice  \[23\] and MASC \[4\]. For
dialectal Arabic, we use Casablanca \[22\], covering Algeria, Egypt,
Jordan, Mauritania, Morocco, Palestine, UAE, and Yemen. We also use a
small proprietary in-house dataset of Emirati Arabic speech for
evaluation, referred to as RamsaLab. For code-switching, we use
ArzEn \[14\] for Egyptian-English, Mixat \[3\] for Emirati-English, and
TEDxTN \[7\] for Tunisian-French. We ensure no overlap between source
and target speakers.¹¹ 1 Given current limitations in Arabic ASR, we
restrict the source-speaker development and test sets to utterances with
a CER below 5%, enabling a more reliable intelligibility evaluation as
outlined in Section 3.3 Table 1 summarizes the labeled corpora
statistics across partitions.

### 3.2 Voice Conversion Models

We benchmark six zero-shot VC models: Diff-HierVC \[11\],
Seed-VC \[16\], EZ-VC \[15\], kNN-VC \[6\], YourTTS \[8\], and Vec2Wav
2.0 \[13\]. Additionally, we train Arabic-centric models based on EZ-VC
and kNN-VC (the best two baselines from preliminary listening tests).
For each architecture, we train two variants: (1) (Ar), trained on
$`\sim`$5K hrs of Arabic data; and (2) (AR+EN+FR), trained on $`\sim`$6K
hrs of multilingual data (English and French in addition to Arabic), as
described in Section 3.1.

EZ-VC: We build a diffusion-based unit-to-speech model following the
EZ-VC approach \[15\]. Discrete speech units are extracted from the 14th
layer of XEUS \[9\], which operates at 16 kHz. For the EZ-VC(AR)
setting, we train a 2K-cluster k-means model on 300 hours of Arabic
speech. For the EZ-VC(AR+EN+FR) setting, we train a separate 2K-cluster
model on the same Arabic data in addition to 50 hours each of English
and French. In both settings, we use F5-TTS \[10\] as the backbone and
extend its vocabulary with the corresponding discrete units,
initializing their embeddings by randomly sampling from the existing
F5-TTS vocabulary to facilitate faster convergence. Starting from the
F5-TTS model pretrained on 100K hours of speech, we fine-tune the two
unit-to-speech models for 150K steps.

KNN-VC: We implement the kNN-VC model \[6\], whichperforms
non-parametric voice conversion, retrieving acoustically similar frames
from the target speaker’s speech. We extract frame-level representations
from the 6th layer of a pretrained WavLM model and construct a target
speaker feature database from the available utterances. For each source
speech frame, the model retrieves the $`k=4`$ nearest target utterance
feature neighbors and uses their representation to construct a converted
feature sequence. We train a multi-speaker HiFi-GAN vocoder to
synthesise waveforms from the converted WavLM features. We follow the
original kNN-VC vocoder architecture and training configuration. All
audio is resampled to 16 kHz for WavLM feature extraction and vocoder
training.

### 3.3 Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate privacy using speaker dissimilarity and Equal Error Rate
(EER). For speaker dissimilarity, we extract speaker embeddings using a
WavLM-L-based ECAPA-TDNN model and compute cosine similarity between
generated and source/target audio. For EER, we constructed $`\sim`$170K
balanced ASV trials from 123 speakers across all datasets, reserving one
original utterance per speaker for enrollment and excluding it from
testing. Each converted test recording was paired with same-speaker and
different-speaker enrollment recordings from the same dataset. We use
the VoicePrivacy Challenge 2024 toolkit’s²² 2
[https://github.com/Voice-Privacy-Challenge/Voice-Privacy-Challenge-2024](https://github.com/Voice-Privacy-Challenge/Voice-Privacy-Challenge-2024)
pretrained ECAPA-TDNN model, cosine similarity scoring, and
SpeechBrain’s EER routine.

For utility, we assess intelligibility, with Word Error Rate (WER) and
Character Error Rate (CER) from an ASR model³³ 3
[https://huggingface.co/oddadmix/Whisperv3-tunisian-codeswitch](https://huggingface.co/oddadmix/Whisperv3-tunisian-codeswitch)
specialized in code-switched Tunisian-French for TEDxTN and Cohere
Arabic ASR model ⁴⁴ 4
[https://huggingface.co/CohereLabs/cohere-transcribe-arabic-07-2026](https://huggingface.co/CohereLabs/cohere-transcribe-arabic-07-2026)
for the remaining corpora as it is trained on Arabic and English. We
evaluate naturalness using UTMOS \[20\]. We also assess accentedness,
measuring the extent to which generated audio exhibits native-like
pronunciation. Following \[27\], we use phonetic posteriorgrams (PPGs)
to evaluate pronunciation distance. We extract PPGs from source and
generated audio using a phoneme recognizer \[25\], align them using
Dynamic Time Warping, and compute Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD)
between aligned pairs. We also report JSD across emphatic, uvular, and
pharyngeal classes, which are challenging Arabic phonetic structures,
and for vowels, motivated by vowel-duration deviations observed in our
internal evaluations.⁵⁵ 5 Emphatics are only represented by /D/, as /d/,
/s/, and /t/ are not recognized by the phonemizer.

### 3.4 Human Evaluation Setup

We conduct two human evaluation studies with the same ten annotators
recruited through Prolific.⁶⁶ 6 Each annotator received a compensation
of \$75, based on an hourly rate of \$12 with an average of 6.25 hours
of annotation work. The annotators are gender-balanced, aged 22-37
($`\mu=29`$, $`\sigma=5`$), and represent diverse nationalities,
including Egyptian, Moroccan, Palestinian, Saudi Arabian, Tunisian, and
Emirati.

Human Study 1: The first study compares the performance of six VC
systems: Vec2Wav 2.0, Seed-VC, kNN-VC baseline, kNN-VC (AR+EN+FR), EZ-VC
baseline, and EZ-VC (AR+EN+FR). We sample 20 monolingual Arabic
utterances evenly from ArVoice, ArzEn, Mixat, and TEDxTN, covering MSA,
Egyptian, Emirati, and Tunisian Arabic, with target speakers selected
from the same corpus. Each annotator rated all 120 generated samples on
six dimensions: speaker dissimilarity (between source and generated
audio), intelligibility, naturalness, accentedness, style preservation,
and audio quality, using five-point scales with predefined rubrics.

Human Study 2: We further evaluate the robustness of our best-performing
model along three directions: code-switching, cross-dialectal
conversion, and generalization to limited-resource dialects. In addition
to the 20 previous utterances, we extend the evaluation by: (a) adding
15 code-switched utterances evenly sampled from ArzEn, Mixat, and
TEDxTN, with same-corpus target speakers; (b) pairing the 35 monolingual
and code-switched utterances considered thus far with target speakers of
different Arabic varieties; and (c) adding 15 utterances evenly sampled
across Yemeni, Algerian, and Palestinian Arabic, representing dialects
with limited training data. Collectively, the study comprises 85
source-target pairs, that provide a balanced representation of gender
and language variants across source and target speakers. Each sample is
evaluated by all the annotators for the same dimensions as in Human
Study 1.

## 4 Evaluation and Analysis

### 4.1 Performance Across Models

We report the objective evaluation results in Table 2 and the subjective
evaluation results in Table 3. Overall, the baselines exhibit trade-offs
between privacy and utility across the evaluated systems. YourTTS
performs poorly across most dimensions, while Diff-HierVC shows no clear
strength in either privacy or utility, and thus were not included in our
human evaluation study. Vec2Wav 2.0 and EZ-VC both achieve strong
privacy, but at the expense of intelligibility and Arabic pronunciation.
Seed-VC provides better performance, demonstrating strong privacy and
intelligibility, although challenges remain in the pronunciation of
Arabic-specific phonemes. Conversely, kNN-VC provides the strongest
baseline for Arabic pronunciation, but is less effective than Seed-VC in
terms of privacy. For the trained Arabic-centric models, kNN-VC shows
only modest improvements over its baseline. In contrast, significant
gains are observed for EZ-VC, with EZ-VC (AR+EN+FR) performing best
overall on intelligibility and Arabic pronunciation while maintaining
effective speaker conversion (SIM$`{}_{\text{tgt}}`$) and privacy (EER).

The strength of EZ-VC(AR+EN+FR) is further demonstrated by the human
evaluation, where it ranks first in intelligibility, naturalness,
accentedness, style preservation, and audio quality, while maintaining
high speaker dissimilarity (4.02). According to our annotation rubric,
this corresponds to listeners perceiving the source and generated
speakers as ‘very different’, indicating strong privacy. Given the
performance of EZ-VC(AR+EN+FR) across evaluation dimensions, we identify
it as our best-performing model and refer to it as EZ-Ar-VC.

### 4.2 Robustness Evaluation of EZ-Ar-VC

Table 4 reports the results of Human Study 2, further evaluating
EZ-Ar-VC under three conditions: (a) monolingual versus code-switched
source speech; (b) different Arabic varieties for
source$`\rightarrow`$target conversion; and (c) additional Yemeni,
Algerian, and Palestinian Arabic source dialects. Results demonstrate
that EZ-Ar-VC remains robust across the challenging conditions of
code-switching, cross-dialectal conversion, and low-resource Arabic
dialects. Intelligibility remains consistently high (4.62-4.95),
regardless of code-switching dimension or source and target Arabic
variant mismatch. Naturalness is similarly robust. Speaker dissimilarity
remains above 4 in most cases, except for TEDxTN and ArzEn, where slight
degradation is observed, which could be due to greater similarity
between source and target speakers in the sampled data. The model also
generalizes well to the low-resource Algerian, Palestinian, and Yemeni
Arabic, maintaining intelligibility above 4.6 and scores above 4 for
most other dimensions. Overall, these results demonstrate the robustness
of the model across diverse linguistic conditions.

## 5 Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrated limitations of various open-source voice
conversion models and their failure to generalize to spoken Arabic
varieties. We analyzed the cause of this failure and revealed patterns
that relate to the lack of representation of target language phones,
leading to unnatural sounding accented speech. Our contribution lies in
revealing these limitations of zero-shot voice conversion, as well as
offering an open-source alternative model for Arabic voice anonymization
that is trained with diverse Arabic data and shown to generalize
robustly across different dialects. Through multi-dimensional subjective
analysis, the model is shown to preserve intelligibility, naturalness,
accent, and style, including for variants with limited resources and
instances of cross-lingual code-switching.
