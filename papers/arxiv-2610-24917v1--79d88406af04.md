# How Well Can LLMs Verbalize Mathematical Formulas? A Multi-Domain Evaluation

Peter Volbach, Tim Schlippe Affiliation: IU International University of
Applied Sciences  
Germany  
tim.schlippe@iu.org

###### Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown strong capabilities across
natural language tasks. However, their ability to generate accurate
written verbalizations of mathematical formulas has not yet been
systematically evaluated. Such verbalization is critical for
text-to-speech systems serving audio-based learning and visually
impaired users. Consequently, we systematically evaluated the formula
verbalization performance of five state-of-the-art LLMs across six
scientific domains. Since existing corpora do not jointly provide
multi-domain coverage, validated transcription variants, textual
context, and controlled formula complexity, we constructed Read-Aloud
Math, a balanced multi-domain corpus containing LaTeX formulas, manually
validated concise simple transcriptions and explanatory extended
transcriptions, multiple transcription variants, surrounding textual
context, domain labels, and formula complexity levels. Based on
multi-reference BLEU, GPT-5.2 achieved the highest score for simple
transcriptions (75.9% without textual context, 64.9% with textual
context). In the context-free setting, however, its score did not differ
significantly from those of Grok 4.1 Fast or Claude Sonnet 4.5. Claude
Sonnet 4.5 obtained the highest score for extended transcriptions
without textual context (36.5%), and Grok 4.1 Fast with textual context
(48.8%). For extended transcriptions, Statistics and Mathematics scored
highest across the two context settings, while Physics scored lowest.
The relationship between formula length and BLEU differed by
transcription type: For simple transcriptions, BLEU decreased
significantly with increasing formula length without context, while the
relationship was not significant with context. For extended
transcriptions, BLEU increased significantly with formula length in both
context settings. Our notation-level analyses showed that parentheses
and other grouping symbols were most frequently involved in
transcription differences, while variable names, capitalization, and
Greek letters were associated with lower BLEU scores. These findings
indicate that LLM-based formula verbalization is promising, but remains
sensitive to transcription type, textual context, and characteristics of
the underlying formulas, including domain, complexity, and notation.

## I Introduction

The growing use of audio-based learning materials, from audiobooks to
podcast-style lecture content, has created new opportunities for
flexible knowledge acquisition \[1, 2, 3\]. Students increasingly
consume educational content while commuting, exercising, or performing
activities where visual attention is unavailable \[4\]. In parallel,
text-to-speech (TTS) technology has become a standard interface for
digital text access.

Beyond educational convenience, accessible mathematics is crucial for
visually impaired individuals who rely on screen readers and TTS systems
to access scientific material \[5, 6\]. For these users, accurate and
unambiguous verbalization of mathematical formulas is essential
(although this work does not target a specific accessibility standard).

Despite these advances, mathematical formulas remain a persistent
challenge for TTS systems, particularly in science, technology,
engineering, and mathematics (STEM) education. The core problem lies not
in speech synthesis itself, but in determining what text should be
synthesized. Mathematical formulas require verbalization—the conversion
of symbolic mathematical notation into natural language text—before
speech rendering. Without this step, formulas may be omitted from audio
versions or verbalized in ways that prevent listeners from
reconstructing the original notation. Even advanced TTS systems often
struggle with intelligibility when processing mathematical
expressions \[7\].

Despite decades of research on mathematical formula verbalization, the
problem remains only partially solved, particularly when balancing
comprehensibility, conciseness, and domain-specific conventions across
diverse areas of mathematics and science. Traditional approaches to
formula verbalization have relied primarily on rule-based systems \[8,
9, 10, 6, 11, 12, 5, 13, 14\]. These systems apply deterministic
transformation rules that map mathematical symbols and structures to
predefined spoken verbalizations. While such approaches ensure
structural consistency and reproducibility, they operate largely at the
syntactic level and lack broader discourse awareness. As a result, they
struggle with context-dependent interpretations and domain-specific
conventions. For instance, $`x^{\prime}`$ may be read as “x prime” or
“the derivative of x”, depending on context.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong
capabilities in natural language understanding and generation, including
context-sensitive interpretation and adaptation to domain-specific
usage. This suggests that they may also be useful for context-aware
mathematical verbalization \[15, 7, 16\]. Unlike rule-based systems,
LLMs can condition their outputs on surrounding text, enabling more
flexible and semantically informed verbalization strategies.

Formula verbalization can serve two complementary goals: faithfully
rendering the symbolic structure and supporting comprehension in
audio-only settings through brief explanatory phrasing. Consequently, we
distinguish between concise simple transcriptions and explanatory
extended transcriptions, allowing us to evaluate both notation-oriented
and comprehension-oriented verbalization.

Our work addresses this verbalization challenge: Given a mathematical
formula in LaTeX format, what natural language text should be generated
for a TTS system to speak? We focus on LaTeX since it remains the
predominant format for authoring mathematical expressions in scientific
and educational documents. While we do not perform speech synthesis
itself, we evaluate how well LLMs can generate appropriate text
verbalizations that would enable effective audio rendering of
mathematical content. Specifically, we address the following three
research questions:

1.  1.  How accurately can state-of-the-art LLMs generate formula
        verbalizations from LaTeX representations?
2.  2.  What is the impact of textual context on formula verbalization
        performance?
3.  3.  How does formula verbalization performance vary across scientific
        domains, formula complexity levels, and notation features?

Throughout this paper, we use verbalization to refer to the general task
of converting mathematical notation into spoken language, and
transcription to refer to the specific textual outputs generated by this
process (simple transcriptions and extended transcriptions).

This paper makes two main contributions: First, we introduce Read-Aloud
Math, a balanced multi-domain corpus of mathematical formulas from six
scientific domains (Mathematics, Statistics, Physics, Chemistry,
Economics, and Computer Science), featuring multiple manually validated
variants of simple transcriptions and extended transcriptions and
surrounding textual context from Wikipedia articles, and make the corpus
publicly available¹¹ 1
[https://github.com/peter1706/verbalize-math-corpus](https://github.com/peter1706/verbalize-math-corpus).
Second, we present a comprehensive evaluation of five state-of-the-art
LLMs across domains, formula complexity levels, notation features, and
textual context.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section II reviews
related work. Section III describes our corpus construction methodology
and the experimental setup for LLM evaluation. Section IV presents our
evaluation results, and Section V concludes and indicates future
research directions.

## II Related Work

Research on mathematical formula verbalization spans rule-based systems,
statistical translation approaches, pre-trained sequence-to-sequence
models, and more recently LLMs.

### II-A Rule-Based Formula Verbalization

Early work established that intelligible verbalization requires explicit
modeling of hierarchical mathematical structure rather than linear
symbol rendering \[14\]. Rule-based systems such as MathSpeak,
ClearSpeak, i-Math, and subsequent adaptable rule engines \[5, 13, 12,
6, 11, 9, 17, 10\] provide deterministic and accessibility-oriented
outputs. While these systems ensure reproducibility and structural
transparency, they require extensive manual rule engineering and offer
limited adaptability to discourse context or domain-specific variation.

Grammar-based approaches provide an alternative to traditional
rule-based verbalization systems. The GF Mathematical Grammar Library,
developed within the WebALT and MOLTO projects, supports multilingual
mathematical verbalization from OpenMath representations using the
Grammatical Framework (GF), providing deterministic semantic
verbalization while requiring handcrafted grammars and semantic
representations \[18, 19\].

### II-B Machine Translation-Based Formula Verbalization

To reduce handcrafted rule design, formula verbalization has been framed
as a translation problem. Statistical and neural approaches learn
LaTeX–spoken mappings from parallel corpora \[8, 20\]. Transfer learning
further advanced this paradigm: MathReader \[16\] employs a T5-based
sequence-to-sequence model fine-tuned on MathBridge \[21\]. Related work
extends this approach to pipelines converting formula images into LaTeX
and spoken-English transcriptions, comparing fine-tuned
sequence-to-sequence models such as BART and T5 with GPT-based LLMs
\[22\]. Sequence-to-sequence models achieve lower error rates for the
LaTeX-to-text step. Overall, treating formula verbalization as a machine
translation task improves fluency but remains dependent on training data
coverage and domain scope.

### II-C LLM-Based Formula Verbalization

Recent studies suggest that state-of-the-art LLMs possess contextual
reasoning capabilities relevant to mathematical language. Newer
GPT-based systems generate plausible formula verbalizations \[15\], and
LLMs resolve context-dependent identifier ambiguities in scientific
documents \[23\]. However, inconsistency and reproducibility remain
concerns \[15\]. Downstream evaluations of mathematical text-to-speech
systems further demonstrate that intelligibility varies substantially
across expression categories \[7\], emphasizing the importance of
high-quality textual verbalization prior to speech synthesis.

### II-D Corpora for Formula Verbalization

Since existing corpora differ in scale, domain coverage, annotation
depth, and spoken alignment, they restrict which aspects of formula
verbalization can be evaluated. Large-scale web corpora such as
OpenWebMath \[24\] and InfiMM-WebMath-40B \[25\] preserve LaTeX formulas
in natural-language context at web scale but lack spoken alignments.
Tex80M \[26\] focuses on visual recognition of mathematical expressions.
Thinn et al. \[8\] constructed a LaTeX–spoken mathematics corpus from
textbooks, but it is limited to mathematics, does not incorporate
context, and does not report structural complexity characteristics.
Formula2Text \[22\] provides formula images with corresponding LaTeX
code and multiple spoken transcriptions, but targets image-based
transcription rather than controlled multi-domain verbalization
evaluation. MathBridge \[21\] provides large-scale LaTeX-spoken English
pairs and a limited surrounding-text window, but models only a single
automatically generated verbalization per formula. S2L \[27\] introduces
multilingual spoken mathematical data but targets audio-to-LaTeX
recognition rather than controlled text-based verbalization generation.
Earlier arXiv-based corpora such as the KDD Cup 2003 dataset \[28\]
demonstrated the feasibility of large-scale structured LaTeX
collections, yet were domain-specific and not designed for verbalization
research.

### II-E Research Gaps

Prior work shows that intelligible formula verbalization requires
preserving hierarchical structure and resolving notation-dependent
ambiguities \[14, 5\]. Rule-based and grammar-based systems provide
deterministic verbalizations but require handcrafted rules or grammars,
limiting scalability and contextual adaptability \[11, 9, 18, 19\].
Data-driven and neural approaches \[8, 20, 16\], including recent
LLM-based methods \[15, 23\], capture contextual dependencies and
generate fluent outputs. However, several gaps remain:

- •
  Multi-Level Transcription. Existing systems produce either minimal
  readings or explanatory outputs, without systematically distinguishing
  simple and extended transcriptions.
- •
  Multi-Domain Evaluation. Evaluations are typically restricted to
  single domains \[16\], despite substantial variation in notation
  across Mathematics, Physics, Statistics, Chemistry, Economics, and
  Computer Science.
- •
  Transcription Variant Coverage. Large-scale corpora such as MathBridge
  \[21\] provide only one canonical transcription per formula, although
  mathematical formulas allow multiple legitimate transcriptions.
- •
  Context-Aware LLM Evaluation. Systematic comparison of verbalization
  quality with and without surrounding document context across multiple
  LLMs is missing.
- •
  Complexity Stratification. Most corpora do not systematically vary
  formula complexity, making it difficult to analyze how performance
  changes across complexity levels.

These limitations motivate our multi-domain evaluation of
state-of-the-art LLMs, explicitly comparing context-free and
context-aware generation, incorporating both transcription types, and
analyzing performance across domains, formula complexity levels, and
notation features.

## III Experimental Setup

This section describes the construction and annotation of our corpus,
the evaluated LLMs and prompting settings, and the evaluation
methodology. The experimental design systematically varies transcription
type and textual context while controlling for scientific domain and
formula complexity.

### III-A Corpus Construction

We constructed Read-Aloud Math, a balanced multi-domain corpus of
mathematical formulas with manually validated transcriptions for
evaluating formula verbalization systems. Rather than maximizing scale,
it was designed for controlled analysis across domains and formula
complexity levels.

#### III-A1 Domain Selection and Data Source

Table I summarizes our corpus of 120 formulas (20 per domain) across six
scientific domains: Mathematics, Statistics, Physics, Chemistry,
Economics, and Computer Science. These domains represent major STEM
areas with distinct notational conventions. Mathematics and Statistics
primarily use abstract symbolic notation, Physics relies on Greek-letter
quantities, Chemistry employs subscripts and superscripts for molecular
structures, Economics features optimization notation, and Computer
Science includes algorithmic and complexity expressions.

Formulas were extracted from Wikipedia-derived documents in the
OpenWebMath corpus \[24\], which preserves both LaTeX expressions and
their surrounding article text. Restricting extraction to Wikipedia
ensures publicly accessible, community-reviewed scientific content and
coherent discourse.

To ensure systematic coverage of structural complexity, each domain
includes five formulas with 1--10, 11--20, 21--30, and 31--40 symbols.
Symbol count is obtained by parsing LaTeX with pylatexenc²² 2
[https://github.com/phfaist/pylatexenc](https://github.com/phfaist/pylatexenc),
normalizing the parsed output by inserting whitespace around operators,
and counting the resulting tokens (commands, operators, and identifiers
each count as one). This stratified sampling enables controlled analysis
of performance under increasing structural difficulty.

#### III-A2 Transcription Types and Variants

Each formula includes two complementary transcription types. Simple
transcriptions provide concise, notation-oriented readings using
standard mathematical terminology without explaining the meanings of
individual symbols. For example, $`E=mc^{2}`$ becomes “Capital E equals
m c squared”. Extended transcriptions preserve the formula structure
while adding brief explanatory wording and symbol interpretations
intended to improve comprehensibility in audio-only settings. The same
formula becomes “Energy E equals mass m times the speed of light c
squared”. Extended transcriptions were limited to approximately 1.5–3
times the length of their simple counterparts to avoid unnecessarily
verbose explanations.

For each formula, the final reference set contains up to five distinct
variants per transcription type to capture natural transcription
diversity and domain-specific preferences. These variants provide
multiple reference transcriptions for evaluation and reflect legitimate
variation in mathematical speech.

#### III-A3 Annotation Process

For each formula, each of the five LLMs later evaluated in our
experiments generated one initial transcription per transcription type:
Gemini 2.5 Flash (Google), DeepSeek V3.2 (DeepSeek), GPT-5.2 (OpenAI),
Claude Sonnet 4.5 (Anthropic), and Grok 4.1 Fast (xAI). Thus, five
initial simple and five initial extended transcriptions were generated
per formula. Each transcription was subsequently manually post-edited.
Two expert annotators independently reviewed all outputs using shared
annotation guidelines, which required each transcription to preserve all
symbols and structural relations, avoid unsupported meaning, and remain
natural for spoken rendering. Discrepancies were resolved through
discussion and consensus, and the final transcriptions were checked for
completeness, correctness, and naturalness.

Since different LLMs occasionally produced identical transcriptions and
post-editing could also yield identical outputs, some formulas contain
fewer than five distinct reference variants. This is reflected in the
average number of variants per transcription type in Table I. The same
OpenRouter-based request setup was used to generate both the initial
reference transcriptions and the evaluated outputs, with no explicit
generation parameters specified.

### III-B Evaluated LLMs and Experimental Design

We evaluated the five LLMs described above in February 2026. Although
all drafts were independently reviewed, post-edited, and validated by
two expert annotators, residual LLM-specific wording may increase
lexical overlap between an LLM’s output and the references, which we
consider when interpreting BLEU-based comparisons.

Each LLM was evaluated in two settings: context-free and context-aware.
In the context-free setting, only the LaTeX formula was provided,
whereas the context-aware setting additionally included surrounding
Wikipedia text. In the result tables, these settings are referred to as
“without textual context” and “with textual context”, respectively. For
both settings, we requested simple and extended transcriptions. Each LLM
generated one output per formula, transcription type, and context
setting, with no repeated generations.

All LLMs were accessed through the OpenRouter Chat Completions API in
February 2026 with the model identifiers google/gemini-2.5-flash,
deepseek/deepseek-v3.2, openai/gpt-5.2, anthropic/claude-sonnet-4.5, and
x-ai/grok-4.1-fast. Only the prompt was specified. Generation parameters
such as temperature, top-p, maximum output length, and reasoning
settings were left unspecified. OpenRouter automatically routed each
request to a provider hosting the requested LLM, which applied its own
defaults.

### III-C Prompting Strategy

We used four fixed prompt variants built from the same core
instructions, one for each combination of transcription type (simple or
extended) and context setting (context-free or context-aware). The
complete verbatim prompts are provided in the public repository. All
variants instructed the LLMs to verbalize the complete formula, produce
clear and natural spoken English, express numbers as words, state
capitalization explicitly, avoid LaTeX notation and mathematical
symbols, and return only the transcription. They differed only through
condition-specific additions: the context-aware variants additionally
provided the text preceding and following the formula and instructed the
LLM to use relevant contextual information, while the
extended-transcription variants additionally requested a concise but
more informative transcription with semantic clarification where
appropriate. The context-aware extended variant combined both additions.
All variants ended with the same self-check instruction asking the LLM
to verify that all prompt instructions had been followed. No
chain-of-thought or step-by-step reasoning was requested.

### III-D Evaluation

We evaluated transcription quality using BLEU computed with the
sacreBLEU implementation \[29\] (default 4-gram setting). We selected
multi-reference BLEU since mathematical formulas can have several valid
transcriptions, and the use of multiple manually validated reference
variants allows legitimate lexical variation to be captured while
providing a standardized and reproducible basis for comparing LLM
outputs. BLEU was computed separately for each formula and then averaged
across formulas, enabling paired comparisons across LLMs. All available
reference variants were provided simultaneously, and n-gram matches were
counted against the maximum reference frequency across variants,
following established machine translation practice. As BLEU measures
n-gram overlap with the reference transcriptions, it does not directly
assess semantic correctness or whether a listener could reconstruct the
original formula. In addition, as the number of available reference
variants differs slightly across formulas and domains, domain-level BLEU
comparisons may also be influenced by differences in reference coverage.

To assess whether differences between LLMs were statistically reliable,
we performed paired two-sided $`t`$-tests on formula-level BLEU scores.
Since all LLMs were evaluated on the same 120 formulas, comparisons were
paired by formula ID. Tests were conducted separately for each
transcription type and setting. Within each combination, we performed
four paired tests comparing the highest-scoring LLM with each of the
other four LLMs. As multiple significance tests increase the chance of
incorrectly identifying a difference as statistically significant, we
applied the Holm correction to these four $`p`$-values at an overall
significance level of $`\alpha=0.05`$.

To assess the relationship between formula length and LLM performance
beyond the four symbol-count ranges, we also used Spearman’s rank
correlation. We correlated each formula’s symbol count with its BLEU
score averaged across the five LLMs, separately for each transcription
type and context setting. The Holm correction was applied to these four
correlation tests at an overall significance level of $`\alpha=0.05`$.

To better understand which formula characteristics were challenging for
LLM-based formula verbalization, we additionally analyzed performance
across notation features extracted from the LaTeX representation using
rule-based pattern matching. Examples included fractions, subscripts,
superscripts, Greek letters, and optimization operators. We first
recorded how frequently these features occurred in the corpus and then
computed average formula-level BLEU scores for formulas containing each
feature. To complement this formula-level analysis, we extracted
token-level confusion pairs from aligned reference and generated
transcriptions. The resulting confusion pairs were grouped by notation
feature and analyzed separately for each transcription type and setting.

## IV Experiments and Results

This section reports the evaluation results and analyzes performance
differences across domains, formula complexity levels, notation
features, and context settings.

### IV-A Overall Performance Across Domains

Tables II and III report mean formula-level BLEU (%) scores across
domains and symbol-count ranges for the five evaluated LLMs: Gemini 2.5
Flash (Gem), DeepSeek V3.2 (Deep), GPT-5.2 (GPT), Claude Sonnet 4.5
(Clau), and Grok 4.1 Fast (Gr). Scores are presented for simple and
extended transcriptions under context-free and context-aware settings.
Bold values indicate the highest BLEU score within each domain or
symbol-count range and setting.

Table IV reports paired $`t`$-tests between the LLM with the highest
mean formula-level BLEU in each setting and the remaining LLMs.
Asterisks indicate significant differences after Holm correction at
$`\alpha=0.05`$.

For simple transcriptions without textual context, GPT-5.2 achieved the
highest mean formula-level BLEU (75.9%), followed by Grok 4.1 Fast
(73.3%) and Claude Sonnet 4.5 (71.3%). Paired $`t`$-tests showed that
GPT-5.2 significantly outperformed Gemini 2.5 Flash and DeepSeek V3.2,
but not Grok 4.1 Fast or Claude Sonnet 4.5.

Providing textual context reduced BLEU for simple transcriptions across
all LLMs. GPT-5.2 decreased from 75.9% to 64.9% (-14.5% relative), Grok
4.1 Fast from 73.3% to 60.2% (-17.9%), and Claude Sonnet 4.5 from 71.3%
to 61.1% (-14.3%). This suggests that concise structural readings
already closely matched the references, while textual context introduced
lexical variation that reduced n-gram overlap.

Extended transcriptions exhibited lower formula-level BLEU values due to
greater lexical diversity. Without context, scores ranged from 21.0%
(GPT-5.2) and 22.7% (DeepSeek V3.2) to 36.5% (Claude Sonnet 4.5). With
context, performance improved consistently: GPT-5.2 increased from 21.0%
to 42.7% (+103.3% relative), Grok 4.1 Fast from 35.6% to 48.8% (+37.1%),
Claude Sonnet 4.5 from 36.5% to 45.2% (+23.8%), and Gemini 2.5 Flash
from 29.1% to 31.8% (+9.3%). Paired $`t`$-tests found no significant
difference between the two highest-scoring LLMs, Claude Sonnet 4.5 and
Grok 4.1 Fast, in either context setting.

BLEU scores varied across scientific domains: For simple transcriptions
in the context-free setting, GPT-5.2 reached 80.8% in Physics and 77.5%
in Chemistry, while Claude Sonnet 4.5 reached 80.2% in Statistics and
79.0% in Economics. For extended transcriptions in the context-aware
setting, Grok 4.1 Fast led in Statistics (59.2%) and Mathematics
(54.4%), and Claude Sonnet 4.5 in Physics (44.8%). Since no domain-level
significance tests were conducted, these comparisons are descriptive and
may reflect differences in notation conventions and symbol usage across
disciplines.

### IV-B Impact of Formula Complexity

Performance across symbol-count ranges was non-monotonic rather than
declining uniformly with formula complexity. For simple transcriptions
in the context-free setting, average BLEU decreased from 80.2% for 1–10
symbols to 61.9% for 11–20, rose to 71.4% for 21–30, and fell to 63.8%
for 31–40 symbols. The context-aware setting showed a similar pattern.
Spearman’s rank correlations based on individual symbol counts assessed
the overall relationship between formula length and BLEU. For simple
transcriptions without context, BLEU decreased significantly as formula
length increased ($`\rho=-.237`$, $`p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.018`$). With
context, the relationship was weaker and not significant
($`\rho=-.095`$, $`p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.304`$). Thus, the higher BLEU for
the 21–30 symbol range does not indicate a reversal of the overall
trend, since each range contains only 30 formulas and its average
depends on which formulas it contains. For extended transcriptions, BLEU
tended to increase with formula length, and this relationship was
significant both without context ($`\rho=.515`$,
$`p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<.004`$) and with context ($`\rho=.259`$,
$`p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=.012`$).

### IV-C Notation-Level Analysis

Tables V and VI provide a more detailed analysis of notation-specific
behavior. Our corpus covers frequent structural features such as
brackets/grouping (94.2%), subscripts/indices (61.7%),
fractions/divisions (40.8%), and superscripts/exponents (41.7%).
Formulas containing structural features such as fractions/divisions,
superscripts/exponents, and summations generally achieved comparatively
high BLEU scores, while lower scores were observed for variable names,
capitalization, and Greek letters, suggesting greater challenges for
lexical choices and symbol interpretation. For extended transcriptions,
context improved performance for all analyzed features, with the largest
relative improvements for Greek letters (+41.7%), capitalization
(+34.1%), and variable names (+25.4%). Since notation features
frequently co-occur, these scores indicate associations rather than
isolated feature effects. Results for rare features such as integrals
and optimization operators should be interpreted cautiously.

Table VII complements the formula-level BLEU analysis by identifying the
notation features most frequently involved in token-level confusion
pairs. The reported percentages indicate the share of all confusion-pair
occurrences assigned to each notation feature within the respective
transcription setting. The five features shown in the table were
selected according to their total number of occurrences across all four
settings.

Across all four settings, brackets/grouping accounts for the largest
share of token-level confusion pairs (23.8–37.9%). Frequent aligned
pairs in this category include “open parenthesis” $`\leftrightarrow`$
“the quantity”, “open square bracket” $`\leftrightarrow`$ “the
concentration of”, and “open curly brace” $`\leftrightarrow`$ “the set
of”, where $`\leftrightarrow`$ denotes aligned tokens in the reference
and generated transcriptions. For example, “open parenthesis”
$`\leftrightarrow`$ “the quantity” indicates that the reference
explicitly verbalized the opening parenthesis, whereas the generated
transcription introduced the expression inside it with “the quantity”.
These expressions are therefore not semantic equivalents but different
verbalizations of the same part of the formula. Such cases show that
LLMs may describe expressions inside grouping symbols without explicitly
naming the symbols. Omitting opening or closing grouping markers can
make reconstructing the original notation more difficult. Other frequent
confusion pairs include “comma” $`\leftrightarrow`$ “and”, “times”
$`\leftrightarrow`$ “by”, “equals” $`\leftrightarrow`$ “to”, and “over”
$`\leftrightarrow`$ “by”. These pairs show that many remaining
differences arise from alternative lexical choices in mathematical
verbalization rather than incorrect structural interpretation.

The distribution of confusion pairs differs substantially between
transcription types: For simple transcriptions without textual context,
punctuation/separators account for 20.7% of all confusion-pair
occurrences, with “comma” $`\leftrightarrow`$ “and” being the most
frequent aligned pair. Their share decreases to 13.2% when textual
context is provided. For extended transcriptions, basic operators
account for 19.1% of all confusion-pair occurrences both with and
without textual context. Frequent operator-related pairs include “times”
$`\leftrightarrow`$ “by”, “times” $`\leftrightarrow`$ “and”, and
“equals” $`\leftrightarrow`$ “to”, indicating greater lexical variation
in extended transcriptions than in simple transcriptions.

Textual context also changes the distribution of confusion pairs within
simple transcriptions. The share assigned to fractions/divisions
increases from 6.9% without textual context to 16.0% with textual
context. Frequent pairs in this category include “divided”
$`\leftrightarrow`$ “over”, “over” $`\leftrightarrow`$ “by”, and “and”
$`\leftrightarrow`$ “over”. At the same time, the share assigned to
punctuation/separators decreases from 20.7% to 13.2%. These results do
not imply that textual context uniformly increases or decreases
transcription differences. Instead, they show that textual context
changes which notation features contribute most strongly to the observed
token-level differences.

### IV-D Scope and Limitations

The controlled design of our corpus enables systematic comparisons
across six scientific domains, four symbol-count ranges, two
transcription types, and two context settings. The study is limited to
120 English-language formulas from Wikipedia-derived documents, so
future work should examine other document types, languages, and broader
formula distributions. Multi-reference BLEU accounts for multiple valid
transcriptions but measures reference overlap rather than semantic
correctness or listener reconstructability. The same four fixed prompt
variants were used for all LLMs, sharing core instructions and differing
only through condition-specific additions for transcription type and
textual context. Each LLM produced one output per formula and condition,
so alternative prompt wording and repeated generations were not
examined. The same OpenRouter request setup was used throughout, with
generation parameters unspecified and provider routing handled
automatically, so results reflect the applied provider defaults. Some
notation features are rare and frequently co-occur, limiting
fine-grained attribution. Annotator disagreements were resolved by
consensus, with no separate pre-consensus agreement metric.

## V Conclusion and Future Work

Our work presents a systematic multi-domain evaluation of five
state-of-the-art LLMs for mathematical formula verbalization across
context settings, scientific domains, and formula complexity levels. The
evaluated LLMs achieved high reference overlap for concise structural
readings, with GPT-5.2 reaching the highest mean formula-level BLEU of
75.9% for simple transcriptions without context. This highlights the
potential of LLMs for notation-oriented formula verbalization.

For simple transcriptions, textual context increased lexical variation
and lowered BLEU scores. In contrast, extended transcriptions achieved
higher BLEU with textual context, suggesting that LLMs can use
surrounding context when generating more explanatory descriptions.

Performance varied across scientific domains, formula complexity levels,
and notation features. Formulas with variable names, capitalization, and
Greek letters tended to show lower BLEU than those with structural
features such as fractions, superscripts, and summations. Token-level
confusion analysis further showed that grouping symbols were most
frequently involved in transcription differences across all settings,
while other confusion pairs varied between simple and extended
transcriptions and with textual context. Overall, LLMs capture many
structural aspects of mathematical notation, while semantic
interpretation and explicit preservation of hierarchical grouping remain
key challenges.

Future work should complement BLEU-based evaluation with human
intelligibility and reconstruction studies assessing whether listeners
can recover the original formulas from generated speech. Further
directions include reasoning-oriented prompting, ensemble methods,
larger multi-domain corpora, and closer integration with text-to-speech
systems.
