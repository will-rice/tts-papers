# Fish Audio Drama 3 Technical Report

Fish Audio Team

###### Abstract

We introduce Fish Audio Drama 3, an open-source speech generation model
that turns text into directed performances, built on a 35B-parameter
mixture-of-experts backbone that activates 3B parameters per token. It
clones voices from multiple references, follows natural-language
direction, revises a take when the next turn gives a note, voices
multi-character scenes, edits existing speech by infilling, and
synthesizes long-form and streaming audio. Multi-turn revision, scene
dubbing, editing by infilling, and streaming text input are new in
Drama 3, all in one model. Drama 3 pairs the hybrid linear-attention
backbone with a new codebook multi-token-prediction audio decoder that
predicts the acoustic codebooks of each frame in dedicated layers that
attend over the entire sequence. The model is trained with a three-stage
pre-training recipe, instruction fine-tuning, and large-batch
reinforcement learning whose only reward is a single learned model of
human preference. Its inference engine decodes a single stream at 271
frames per second on one H200, against 177 for Fish Audio S2, although
Drama 3 has more than seven times as many parameters. The model weights
are available on [GitHub](https://github.com/fishaudio/fish-speech) and
[Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/fishaudio/drama-3), and inference
is available through
[SGLang-Omni](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang-omni). We highly
encourage readers to visit [https://fish.audio](https://fish.audio) to
direct their own performances.

## 1 Introduction

![](arxiv-2610-18010--8f55ac65ca78.figures/figure-1.webp)

Figure 1: Fish Audio Drama 3 turns text into directed performances. It
casts voices from references, follows natural-language direction,
revises a take when the next turn gives a note, stages multi-character
scenes, edits existing takes, and synthesizes long-form audio, streaming
speech as the text arrives.

Large-scale text-to-speech (TTS) models now read text aloud with
near-human intelligibility and speaker similarity (Anastassiou et al.,
2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Du et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026). The
applications that drive the field today—audiobooks and audio drama, film
and game dubbing, and interactive characters—ask not only for a faithful
reading but for a performance. A performance is shaped by direction, the
natural-language description of how a line is to be delivered. The
delivery of a line is described in words, refined over several takes,
placed within a scene among other characters, and corrected locally when
a single word is wrong.

Our previous model, Fish Audio S2 (Liao et al., 2026), took a first step
in this direction with inline natural-language instructions and native
multi-speaker generation. Four limitations remain. First, the
architecture was not built for long directed sessions, whose references
and earlier takes run to thousands of audio tokens. S2’s full-attention
backbone attends over all of them, and its per-frame fast autoregressive
module (Fast AR) (Liao et al., 2024) sees the references only through
one backbone state. Second, control is single-shot. An instruction
shapes one generation, and there is no way to give a note on a take the
model has already produced and have it redone. Third, generation is
all-or-nothing. Fixing one word inside a long take requires regenerating
the take, which changes everything around the word. Fourth,
post-training optimizes proxies. Reinforcement learning (RL) for TTS
commonly rewards low word error rate and high speaker similarity (Du
et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2026), yet an expressive performance
legitimately departs from both. A laugh, a long pause, or a whisper
changes exactly the transcript and timbre that such metrics expect.

In this report, we present Fish Audio Drama 3, a speech generation model
built around directed performance. Drama 3 keeps the discrete audio
tokenizer of S2 and redesigns the model, the task formulation, and the
post-training objective around it. Specifically, we introduce three key
technical innovations:

- •
  Hybrid Backbone with a Codebook-MTP Decoder. Drama 3 builds on a
  hybrid linear-attention language model (Katharopoulos et al., 2020;
  Yang et al., 2025; Qwen Team, 2026) with a sparse mixture-of-experts
  feed-forward network (Shazeer et al., 2017), and replaces the
  per-frame Fast AR of S2 with a codebook multi-token-prediction (MTP)
  decoder (Gloeckle et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2024), in which
  each residual codebook (Zeghidour et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2023) is
  predicted by a dedicated layer that attends causally over the entire
  sequence, including the fine acoustic detail of the reference audio.
- •
  Multi-Turn Revision, Scene Dubbing, Editing, and Streaming Text Input
  in One Model. Drama 3 learns tasks that S2 did not support, such as
  revising a take from a note in the next turn, dubbing a whole
  multi-character scene, editing speech by infilling, and speaking text
  as it streams in. Each is written as a conversation over the same
  token stream as cloning and directing, with the loss applied only to
  the model’s own turns (Section 3, _Conversation format_).
- •
  A Single Reward Model, No Rule-Based Rewards. Because expressive
  delivery breaks the transcription and similarity proxies above, we
  eliminate every rule-based reward. Reinforcement learning optimizes a
  single learned reward model (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al.,
  2022), a smaller speech language model of our own fine-tuned on human
  preference judgments, whose calibrated and bounded reward keeps reward
  hacking (Amodei et al., 2016; Gao et al., 2023a) observable. We train
  with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024)
  without a Kullback–Leibler (KL) penalty and on very large batches,
  which in our development was more stable than small batches with many
  steps.

These innovations directly enable three functional breakthroughs
(Figure 1):

- •
  Directable Performance. Drama 3 follows inline and scoped tags, style
  descriptions, and free-form director’s notes. Directing is multi-turn.
  When a take is not what the director wanted, the next turn gives a
  note such as \[softer and warmer versus previous\] and the model
  redoes the take, with the earlier takes kept in context.
- •
  Scenes and Dialogue. The model casts several voices from reference
  clips and performs multi-speaker dialogue and multi-character scenes
  in a single pass, with explicit control over line boundaries; a
  character’s lines may be in different languages, in the same voice.
- •
  Editing and Long-Form Synthesis. The model replaces a word or a
  sentence inside existing speech while keeping the surrounding audio,
  and synthesizes long-form audio line by line or in streaming mode.

Drama 3 is served by an inference engine designed for its hybrid
backbone and codebook-MTP decoder (Section 4). Its codebook-MTP decoder
runs once per frame instead of once per codebook, its experts run in the
block-scaled FP8 of the RL rollouts, and the engine removes the kernel
launches that CUDA graphs leave in place, optimizes kernels on the
shapes of live serving, and separates prefill from decode so that new
requests do not stall running streams. On one H200, it decodes a single
stream at 271 frames per second against 177 for S2, although it has more
than seven times as many parameters.

We evaluate Drama 3 on public benchmarks (Section 5). For directable
performance, each revision round on the Step-Audio-Edit benchmark (Yan
et al., 2025b) raises its emotion and style accuracy. For voice cloning,
it has the lowest error rate of all compared systems in Chinese, Dutch,
English, German, and Turkish on the MiniMax Multilingual Testset (Zhang
et al., 2025). For editing on the Ming-Freeform-Audio-Edit
benchmark (Yan et al., 2025a), it has a lower error rate than the infill
API of Cartesia Sonic-3 in English and Chinese, and in Chinese a higher
speaker similarity than dedicated editing models on insertion and
substitution.

To accelerate research and lower the barrier to expressive speech
generation, we publicly release the model weights on
[GitHub](https://github.com/fishaudio/fish-speech) and [Hugging
Face](https://huggingface.co/fishaudio/drama-3), with inference
available through
[SGLang-Omni](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang-omni). To our
knowledge, Drama 3 is the largest open-weight model dedicated to speech
generation to date, by total parameters. We also highly encourage
readers to direct their own performances at our official site
[https://fish.audio/](https://fish.audio/).

## 2 Architecture

![](arxiv-2610-18010--8f55ac65ca78.figures/figure-2.webp)

Figure 2: Fish Audio Drama 3 architecture. The backbone reads the system
prompt, the target text, and then its own audio frames, each entered as
a fused embedding of its codes 0 and 1. The codebook-MTP decoder runs
over every position on top of the backbone’s hidden state. Its four
shared layers take that state, the predicted code 0, and the input
frame’s codes 2–9, which bypass the backbone, and MTP Layers 1–9 then
predict the remaining codes one at a time. Codes are predicted wherever
the next token is an audio frame, every layer attends causally along
time, and the audio tokenizer decoder turns the ten codes into a
waveform.

Drama 3 keeps the overall design of S2, in which an autoregressive
language model reads interleaved text and audio tokens and predicts the
next frame’s semantic code, and a second module predicts its remaining
acoustic codes. It changes three components (Figure 2): a hybrid
linear-attention backbone replaces full attention, only two of the ten
codebooks are fed back into the backbone, and a time-causal codebook-MTP
decoder replaces the per-frame Fast AR of S2’s Dual-AR design (Liao
et al., 2024). Drama 3 has no audio encoder, so reference and prompt
audio enter only as audio tokens.

### 2.1 Audio Tokenizer

We reuse the S2 audio tokenizer unchanged. Built on the Descript Audio
Codec (Kumar et al., 2023), it encodes 44.1 kHz audio into frames of ten
codebooks at about 21.5 frames per second. The first codebook is
semantic, and the remaining nine are residual vector
quantizers (Zeghidour et al., 2022) that add progressively finer
acoustic detail. It is fully causal, so encoding and decoding both
stream. We write $`q^{(k)}_{t}`$ for the code of codebook
$`k\in\{0,\dots,9\}`$ at frame $`t`$. Each frame occupies one backbone
position, so a second of speech costs about 21.5 positions regardless of
the number of codebooks.

### 2.2 Backbone

The backbone is initialized from Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (Qwen Team, 2026), of
which we use only the language model, a 40-layer hybrid that stacks
blocks of three Gated DeltaNet layers (Yang et al., 2025), a gated form
of linear attention (Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2024), and
one gated full-attention layer (Qiu et al., 2025), a design that suits
long-form speech. The Gated DeltaNet layers carry the history in a
constant-size state and only one layer in four keeps a key–value (KV)
cache (Pope et al., 2022), while the full-attention layers keep exact
retrieval over the whole context, such as the timbre of a reference clip
or a line far back in a script. Every layer uses a mixture-of-experts
(MoE) feed-forward network (Shazeer et al., 2017) that routes each token
to 8 of 256 experts and adds a shared expert (Dai et al., 2024), so only
about 3B of the 35B backbone parameters are active per token. We replace
Qwen3.5’s native MTP layer (Qwen Team, 2026), which predicts the token
after next, with our own codebook-MTP decoder (Section 2.4).

### 2.3 Partial Codebook Fusion

S2 fed all ten codebooks back into its backbone by summing their
embeddings, a scheme it calls Multi-Codebook Fusion (Liao et al., 2026).
Drama 3 feeds back only the semantic and the first acoustic codes, to
limit exposure bias (Bengio et al., 2015; Ranzato et al., 2016) from its
own sampled codes while still giving the backbone a coarse view of the
acoustics of both the reference audio and its own generated speech. At
the position of audio frame $`t`$, the backbone input is

```math
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigl(\mathbf{E}^{\text{LM}}\bigl[s(q^{(0)}_{t})\bigr]+\mathbf{E}^{(1)}\bigl[q^{(1)}_{t}\bigr]\Bigr), \tag{1}
```

where $`\mathbf{E}^{\text{LM}}`$ is the backbone’s token embedding,
$`s(\cdot)`$ maps a semantic code to one of the 4,096 semantic tokens
added to the Qwen3.5 vocabulary, $`\mathbf{E}^{(k)}`$ is the slice of
one learned codebook-embedding table reserved for codebook $`k`$, and
$`1/\sqrt{2}`$ keeps the fused embedding at the scale of a single one.
Text positions use $`\mathbf{E}^{\text{LM}}`$ alone, and codebooks
$`q^{(2)}_{t},\dots,q^{(9)}_{t}`$ bypass the backbone and enter the
decoder’s first layer, described in Section 2.4. In Figure 2, the
fed-back pair is the stacked 0/1 chips above Generated Audio Codes; the
Partial Codebook Fusion boxes turn each pair into one fused embedding
(orange), which is also how reference audio enters the token stream.

### 2.4 Codebook-MTP Decoder

At the position of frame $`t`$, the backbone predicts the next frame’s
semantic code $`q^{(0)}_{t+1}`$, and the codebook-MTP decoder predicts
its nine acoustic codes $`q^{(1)}_{t+1},\dots,q^{(9)}_{t+1}`$ at the
same position (Figure 2, right). The decoder stacks 13 layers of one
design, with four shared layers followed by nine per-codebook layers,
one for each acoustic codebook.

Layer anatomy. Every decoder layer merges the hidden state from below
with an injected code embedding and runs one transformer block (Vaswani
et al., 2017):

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{(\ell)}_{t} \displaystyle=\operatorname{Merge}^{(\ell)}\bigl(\mathbf{e}^{(\ell)}_{t},\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)}_{t}\bigr)=\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\bigl[\operatorname{RMSNorm}(\mathbf{e}^{(\ell)}_{t});\,\operatorname{RMSNorm}(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)}_{t})\bigr], \tag{2}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{t} \displaystyle=\operatorname{RMSNorm}\Bigl(\operatorname{Block}^{(\ell)}\bigl(\mathbf{u}^{(\ell)}_{1:t}\bigr)\Bigr)=:\operatorname{Layer}^{(\ell)}\bigl(\mathbf{e}^{(\ell)}_{t},\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)}_{t}\bigr),
```

where $`t`$ indexes positions in the whole sequence (each audio frame
occupies one position, so $`t`$ is also its frame index),
$`\ell\in\{1,\dots,13\}`$, $`\mathbf{h}^{(0)}_{t}`$ is the backbone’s
final hidden state, and $`\mathbf{e}^{(\ell)}_{t}`$ is the code
embedding injected by layer $`\ell`$; the decoder runs over every
position of the sequence, text included, and at a position that predicts
no audio frame $`\mathbf{e}^{(\ell)}_{t}`$ is zero (except for the
input-frame codes that the first shared layer adds, described in
Equation 3) and no loss is taken. Here $`\operatorname{RMSNorm}`$ is
root-mean-square normalization (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019),
$`\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}`$ is a bias-free projection of the concatenation
from $`2d`$ to the backbone’s width $`d`$, and
$`\operatorname{Block}^{(\ell)}`$ is one transformer layer with gated
full attention (Qiu et al., 2025) and a dense SwiGLU feed-forward
network (Shazeer, 2020) at the backbone’s width, whose causal
self-attention covers positions $`1,\dots,t`$, and
$`\operatorname{Layer}^{(\ell)}`$ denotes the whole layer. The MTP
layers are stacked along the codebook axis instead of shifted along
time, so they differ from the MTP modules that predict several future
frames at once (Wang et al., 2025b; Xie et al., 2026). Each layer
attends causally over the whole sequence, including the reference audio,
so fine codes are predicted from full context rather than from one
summary vector.

Shared layers. At position $`t`$, whose input is frame $`t`$, the
backbone predicts the semantic code $`q^{(0)}_{t+1}`$ of the next frame,
and the decoder completes that frame at the same position. Each of the
four shared layers, $`\ell=1,\dots,4`$, shown as Shared Layers
$`\times`$4 in Figure 2, takes the hidden state from below and injects
the semantic code of the frame being completed. The first one also
receives codes 2–9 of the input frame, which the backbone never saw:

```math
\mathbf{e}^{(\ell)}_{t}=\mathbf{E}^{(\ell)}_{\text{s}}\bigl[q^{(0)}_{t+1}\bigr]+\mathbf{1}[\ell=1]\,a_{t}\sum_{k=2}^{9}\mathbf{E}^{(k)}\bigl[q^{(k)}_{t}\bigr], \tag{3}
```

where $`\mathbf{E}^{(\ell)}_{\text{s}}`$ is layer $`\ell`$’s own table
for the semantic code (the hook from code 0 into the Shared Layers box
in Figure 2), $`\mathbf{1}[\cdot]`$ is the indicator function,
$`a_{t}=1`$ if the input at position $`t`$ is an audio frame and $`0`$
if it is text, and $`\mathbf{E}^{(k)}`$ is the codebook-embedding table
of Equation 1. The second term is the plain, unscaled sum of the
embeddings of codes 2–9 (the chip stacked behind the backbone’s hidden
state at the foot of each audio column in Figure 2). A shared layer has
no output head, and its only output is the hidden state of Equation 2,
which the layer above takes as its input,

```math
\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{t}=\operatorname{Layer}^{(\ell)}\bigl(\mathbf{e}^{(\ell)}_{t},\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)}_{t}\bigr),\qquad\ell=1,\dots,4, \tag{4}
```

and $`\mathbf{h}^{(4)}_{t}`$ is the input of the first codebook-MTP
layer. Together they fuse the backbone’s hidden state, the semantic
information of the current frame, and the acoustic detail carried over
from the previous frame into a single representation. All nine
per-codebook layers then predict their codebooks from this one shared
representation, with richer temporal and acoustic context than the
backbone state alone.

Per-codebook layers. The nine per-codebook layers (MTP Layers 1–9;
$`\ell=4+k`$ for $`k=1,\dots,9`$) have the same structure plus an output
head, with no weights shared across codebooks. MTP Layer $`k`$ takes the
hidden state from below and injects the code chosen just before it,
codebook $`k-1`$ of the frame:

```math
\mathbf{e}^{(4+k)}_{t}=\mathbf{E}^{(k)}_{\text{c}}\bigl[q^{(k-1)}_{t+1}\bigr], \tag{5}
```

where $`\mathbf{E}^{(k)}_{\text{c}}`$ is the layer’s own table (for
$`k=1`$ the code is code 0, the straight arrow past the shared box in
Figure 2). Its hidden state follows Equation 2 as in the shared layers,

```math
\mathbf{h}^{(4+k)}_{t}=\operatorname{Layer}^{(4+k)}\bigl(\mathbf{e}^{(4+k)}_{t},\mathbf{h}^{(3+k)}_{t}\bigr),\qquad k=1,\dots,9, \tag{6}
```

starting from the shared layers’ output $`\mathbf{h}^{(4)}_{t}`$; unlike
a shared layer, it has an output head, which turns that state into
codebook $`k`$ of the frame:

```math
q^{(k)}_{t+1}\sim\operatorname{softmax}\bigl(\mathbf{W}^{(k)}_{\text{out}}\,\mathbf{h}^{(4+k)}_{t}\bigr). \tag{7}
```

Each layer thus reads the code just chosen and emits the next, up to
code 9, while its attention (Equation 2) still sees the whole sequence
up to position $`t`$. Reference frames receive the same injections, with
their loss switched off, so every layer sees all ten codebooks of the
reference audio.

Training and inference. In training, every code is
teacher-forced (Williams & Zipser, 1989) from the ground truth, so the
decoder is one extra causal pass over the whole sequence, parallel over
time. At inference, each frame takes one backbone step and then 13
single-position decoder steps, and the head of each per-codebook layer
is sampled before the next layer runs (Section 4). The sampled
$`q^{(0)}`$ and $`q^{(1)}`$ of frame $`t+1`$ form the next backbone
input (Equation 1); its codes 2–9 go to the first shared layer as in
Equation 3.

Comparison with S2. Codec language models handle the codebook axis in
one of three ways: by delaying or flattening codebooks in the main
stream (Copet et al., 2023); with a small depth transformer inside each
frame (Lee et al., 2022; Défossez et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2026); or
with models that run over the whole sequence once per codebook stage,
either non-autoregressively (Wang et al., 2023) or
autoregressively (Wang et al., 2025a; Cremer & Cremer, 2026). S2’s Fast
AR is of the second kind, a separate four-layer transformer that loops
over one frame’s codebooks, conditioned on one projected backbone
state (Liao et al., 2026). The codebook-MTP decoder is of the third kind
but is one decoder rather than a chain of separate models. To our
knowledge, it is the first whose per-codebook layers are full-width,
attend causally along time over the backbone’s own sequence, and pass
the hidden state upward as DeepSeek-V3’s MTP modules do (DeepSeek-AI
et al., 2024). It differs from S2 in three ways.

- •
  Context. It reads the fine codes of the reference audio and of all
  previous frames directly, whereas the Fast AR sees them only through
  the backbone state, so acoustic detail such as the reference’s timbre
  reaches every codebook prediction.
- •
  Training layout. It runs on the backbone’s own sequence, reusing its
  rotary position embeddings (Su et al., 2021) and attention masks, so
  every codebook of every frame is trained in one extra causal pass,
  whereas the Fast AR is trained on separate per-frame codebook
  sequences.
- •
  Capacity. It gives every codebook a full-width layer, embedding, head,
  and KV cache over the whole sequence of its own, whereas the Fast AR
  shares four layers across all codebooks and keeps only per-frame
  scratch state.

## 3 Training

Figure 3: Drama 3 training pipeline. A pre-trained Qwen3.5 LLM goes
through three pre-training stages, then instruction SFT and preference
RL.

Drama 3 is initialized from a pre-trained Qwen3.5 language model (Qwen
Team, 2026) with a newly initialized codebook-MTP decoder (Section 2)
and trained in three pre-training stages (Section 3.1), one stage of
supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on instructions (Section 3.2), and
reinforcement learning (RL) from human preference (Christiano et al., 2017) (Section 3.3), as Figure 3 shows.

Conversation format. Every task is written as a conversation in the
ChatML-style chat markup of the base model, in which the system turn
holds the task instruction and the voice references for zero-shot
cloning (Wang et al., 2023), the user turn holds the text to speak, and
the assistant turn holds the audio. Loss covers only the assistant
turns, so reference audio is context only. Stages 1 and 2 of
pre-training concatenate the references as in S2; Stage 3 either
concatenates them or gives each clip a numbered block pairing its
speaker-tagged transcript with its audio, at random; and from SFT on,
every sequence uses these numbered blocks, the indexed reference layout
(Appendices A and B).

### 3.1 Pre-training

Data. Pre-training data is transcribed speech, all of which passes
quality control first, and text documents, which keep the backbone’s
language ability. In total, pre-training processes several hundred
billion tokens. The language coverage is that of S2, about 80 languages
and dialects (Liao et al., 2026).

Stages. Stages 1 and 2 teach the model to turn text into audio codes;
their sequences are at most 8K tokens long and of two kinds, text
documents and multi-turn TTS conversations (Figure 7). Stage 2 continues
on the same sequences with the batch doubled (Smith et al., 2018), which
lowers gradient noise, and leaves the learning-rate decay to Stage 3.
Stage 3 extends the model to whole recordings, streaming input, and
speech editing. It raises the maximum sequence length to 16K tokens and
adds long-form synthesis of a whole recording from its transcript, as
one segment (Figure 8) or line by line (Figure 9), streaming interleave
(Figure 10), and speech infilling (Figure 14). Throughout, samples are
packed with document masking (Zhao et al., 2024) into rows of 128K
tokens, and the model is optimized with Muon (Jordan et al., 2024; Liu
et al., 2025a).

Objective. Let $`\mathcal{T}`$ be the supervised text positions,
including the closing \<\|im*end\|\> of each audio segment and the line
separators, and $`\mathcal{S}`$ the supervised positions whose target is
a semantic code $`q^{(0)}*{t}`$; then

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{text}}=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\log p_{\theta}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t}),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\text{sem}}=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\log p_{\theta}\bigl(q^{(0)}_{t}\mid x_{<t}\bigr), \tag{8}
```

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cb}}=\frac{1}{9}\sum_{k=1}^{9}\Bigl(-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\log p_{\phi}\bigl(q^{(k)}_{t}\mid\mathbf{h}_{<t},\mathbf{q}_{<t},q^{(<k)}_{t}\bigr)\Bigr),\qquad\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{text}}+0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{sem}}+0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{cb}}, \tag{9}
```

where $`p_{\theta}`$ is the backbone’s output distribution, $`p_{\phi}`$
the decoder (Section 2), $`\mathbf{h}_{<t}`$ the backbone hidden states
at earlier positions, $`\mathbf{q}_{<t}`$ all ten codebooks of earlier
frames, and $`q^{(<k)}_{t}`$ the lower codebooks of the current frame.
Each term is a mean over its own bucket, so the text–speech balance does
not drift with batch composition; the semantic and codebook terms share
one per-frame mask, and $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{cb}}`$ back-propagates into
the backbone. In pre-training and SFT, an auxiliary load-balancing
loss (Lepikhin et al., 2020) keeps the MoE router from sending most
tokens to a few experts.

### 3.2 Instruction SFT

A single SFT stage starts from the Stage-3 checkpoint and trains on
sequences of up to 32K tokens in the indexed reference layout
(Section 3, _Conversation format_). It teaches the model to follow
directions and to perform dialogue and scenes. We choose the SFT recipe
by the teacher-forced negative log-likelihood (NLL) of the semantic
tokens of held-out real speech, each clip cloned from other clips of its
voice, rather than by training loss.

Instruction formats. As in S2, directions are natural language in the
user text, in the forms of Appendix C: point tags such as \[laughs\] or
\[pause\], which insert a vocal event or a pause at that position;
scoped tags such as \<whisper\>...\</whisper\>, which set the delivery
of the enclosed words only; style prefixes (Tian et al., 2026), which
set the delivery of the whole line; and director’s notes on intent,
pacing, or emphasis, each phrased in several templates and registers.
Directing sessions are multi-turn. When a take is not what the director
wanted, the next turn gives a note relative to it, such as \[softer and
warmer versus previous\], and the model produces a new take, with the
earlier takes kept in context as audio (Figure 12). Multi-speaker
dialogue gives each voice one or more reference clips and switches
voices in the user text with speaker tokens (Figure 11). Whole-scene
dubbing describes the characters in text and gives every line up front,
joined by supervised separators, so that the delivery of a line can
depend on the lines that follow (Figure 13). Plain cloning, long-form,
infilling, and text-only chat sequences (Appendix A and Figure 14) stay
in the mixture so that the pre-training abilities are kept.

### 3.3 Reinforcement Learning from Human Preference

RL starts from the SFT checkpoint and aligns Drama 3’s takes with human
preference over expressive requests.

A single reward model. S2 combined three rewards: a transcription-error
reward, a speech-quality model, and a speaker-similarity reward. The
transcription-error reward compares what an automatic speech recognition
(ASR) system hears with the script, but laughs and long pauses
legitimately change what it hears. The speech-quality model scores the
whole utterance at once, so it cannot reward fine-grained control. The
speaker-similarity reward scores a whispered or shouted line lower
against a neutral reference. Each of these rewards either penalizes the
expressiveness we want or cannot see it, so Drama 3 scores every take
with a single learned model of human preference and uses no other
reward.

Reward model. The reward model (RM) is a smaller speech language model
of our own, similar in design to Drama 3 and pre-trained with the same
audio tokenizer and sequence format through the end of Stage 2, with a
scalar head on the final hidden state of the take’s closing token. It
reads exactly the policy’s prompt followed by the take’s audio tokens,
so no tokenizer decoder, ASR, or speaker model runs in the reward path;
because it sees the references and the script, voice consistency and
content fidelity remain part of what it judges. It is trained on human
preference judgments over pairs of takes with the Bradley–Terry
loss (Bradley & Terry, 1952)

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}=-\bigl[\,y\log\sigma(s_{a}-s_{b})+(1-y)\log\sigma(s_{b}-s_{a})\bigr]+10^{-3}\bigl(s_{a}^{2}+s_{b}^{2}\bigr), \tag{10}
```

where $`s_{a},s_{b}`$ are the RM’s outputs for takes $`a`$ and $`b`$,
$`\sigma`$ is the logistic function, and $`y`$ is the preference label
smoothed (Szegedy et al., 2015) as in conservative direct preference
optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023; Mitchell, 2023). Each raw
score is standardized by a frozen anchor, clipped, and turned into an
expected win rate among the $`n`$ scored takes sampled for the same
prompt:

```math
z_{i}=\operatorname{clip}\Bigl(\frac{s_{i}-\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}},-3,3\Bigr),\qquad R_{i}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\sigma(z_{i}-z_{j}), \tag{11}
```

where $`s_{i}`$ is the RM score of take $`i`$, and
$`\mu_{0},\sigma_{0}`$ are a mean and a standard deviation fitted once
on held-out takes and then kept fixed. With the anchor fixed, a drift of
the whole batch stays visible in the mean $`z`$, and with the clip, a
take gains nothing past three standard deviations. Here
$`\sigma(z_{i}-z_{j})`$ estimates the probability that take $`i`$ beats
take $`j`$, and the sum runs over the other $`n-1`$ takes of the group,
$`j\neq i`$, so $`R_{i}`$ is the rate at which take $`i`$ beats the rest
of its group. The RM does not score a take with no audio, which stays in
its group with reward zero and is left out of this comparison.

Sampling. For each prompt, the inference engine (Section 4) draws
$`G=8`$ takes at temperature 1 by plain ancestral sampling, with no
top-$`p`$ (Holtzman et al., 2020) or top-$`k`$ (Fan et al., 2018)
truncation, so that the takes follow exactly the probabilities the
engine reports. Length is limited by a sampling budget rather than a
reward. Each take may run to about 2.5 times the expected duration of
its own script, and no take may exceed 4,096 frames, about 190 s, which
bounds runaway takes.

Policy optimization. We use Group Relative Policy Optimization
(GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) with the mean-centered advantages of
Dr. GRPO (Liu et al., 2025b) and no KL penalty or reference model.
Groups whose reward standard deviation is below 0.05 are dropped, a
filter similar to the dynamic sampling of Decoupled Clip and Dynamic
Sampling Policy Optimization (DAPO) (Yu et al., 2025) but without
re-sampling. With rewards $`R_{i}\in[0,1]`$ (Equation 11),

```math
A_{i}=R_{i}-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}R_{j},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{|y_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_{i}|}\min\Bigl(\rho_{i,t}A_{i},\ \operatorname{clip}\bigl(\rho_{i,t},1-\epsilon_{\text{low}},1+\epsilon_{\text{high}}\bigr)A_{i}\Bigr), \tag{12}
```

where
$`\rho_{i,t}=\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x_{i},y_{i,<t})/\pi_{\text{eng}}(y_{i,t}\mid x_{i},y_{i,<t})`$
is the proximal policy optimization (PPO) ratio (Schulman et al., 2017),
$`x_{i}`$ is the prompt of take $`i`$, $`\pi_{\theta}`$ is the
probability that the backbone being trained gives the token,
$`\pi_{\text{eng}}`$ is the probability of the same token reported by
the inference engine that sampled it, $`\mathcal{B}`$ holds the takes of
the kept groups, and $`y_{i}`$ is the sequence the backbone samples for
take $`i`$, made of the semantic code $`q^{(0)}_{t}`$ of every frame and
the closing \<\|im*end\|\>. Unlike in S2, the codebook-MTP decoder,
which produces the nine acoustic codes of each frame, stays frozen
during RL and receives no RL loss. Because each rollout batch gets one
optimizer step, the trainer’s weights at the update are the weights that
sampled the batch, and the trainer routes each token to the experts that
the engine chose, as in rollout routing replay (Ma et al., 2025), so
$`\rho`$ departs from 1 only through numerical train–inference
mismatch (Qi et al., 2025). The rollout engines run the routed experts
in block-scaled 8-bit floating point (FP8) (Micikevicius et al., 2022),
re-quantized after every weight sync, while the trainer runs in bfloat16
(BF16), and this mismatch grows with that precision gap. We bound this
departure with the asymmetric clip of DAPO,
$`\epsilon*{\text{low}}=0.2`$ and $`\epsilon\_{\text{high}}=0.28`$. We
keep GRPO’s per-take length normalization, which Dr. GRPO removes.

Large batches, few steps. Each step uses 1,536 prompts $`\times`$ $`G`$
takes, drawn from diverse expressive requests that carry all of their
reference clips, at a constant Muon learning rate without weight decay,
and the schedule is short. Without a KL penalty or weight decay, few
steps at a small constant learning rate bound how far the policy moves,
and large batches average each step over many prompts, so a step follows
broad preference rather than the particulars of a few prompts. In
addition, this setup avoids the drift toward reward
over-optimization (Gao et al., 2023a) that small-batch, many-step
schedules showed in our RL development. Recent large language models
such as MiMo-V2.6 (Xiaomi MiMo Team, 2026) likewise use very large RL
batches.

## 4 Inference Engine

A streaming request, or stream, plays to its listener while it is
generated, so its frames must arrive at least as fast as the codec plays
them, about 21.5 per second (Section 2). Serving a request has two
phases, a prefill that reads its text and reference audio in one pass,
and decoding, one step per frame. One GPU serves many streams at once,
in rounds that are each a prefill of new requests or one decode step for
all running streams. We report the per-stream decode rate, the frames
per second each stream receives, at a given concurrency, the number of
streams a GPU serves at once.

Drama 3 differs from S2 in generation structure, model size, and serving
load, and we rewrote the critical path of the inference engine to match.
Its generation structure shortens the sequential path of each frame and
reduces the weights it reads (Section 4.1). We remove the kernel
launches and host waits that CUDA graphs (Gray, 2019), which replay a
whole step at once, leave in place (Section 4.2). The kernels that stay
are tuned to the shapes that serving produces (Section 4.3), and
arriving requests no longer stall the streams already playing
(Section 4.4).

### 4.1 Generation Structure

Streaming batches are usually small, so a step does little arithmetic
per weight it reads: decoding is bound by memory bandwidth, and the time
of a frame is set mainly by the weights it reads. In the Dual-AR design
of S2, the four-layer Fast AR makes ten passes after the backbone step,
so a frame runs 40 layer passes in sequence and reads the Fast AR’s
weights again on every pass (Figure 4a). At batch size 1, a frame of S2
therefore reads about 15.6 GB of BF16 weights, two bytes per parameter,
the equivalent of about 7.8B parameters, although S2 has about 4.6B.
Drama 3 reads less for two reasons. First, its codebook-MTP decoder
(Section 2.4) runs each of its 13 layers once per frame, so the
decoder’s weights are read once (Figure 4b). Second, its routed experts
are stored in block-scaled FP8, with one scale per $`128\times 128`$
block, so each expert parameter takes one byte instead of two. This is
the format in which the RL rollouts already run (Section 3.3), so
serving adds no quantization of its own and runs with the numerics of RL
sampling. A frame of Drama 3 thus reads about 6.0 GB of weights
(Table 1), less than two fifths of what S2 reads, with more than seven
times as many parameters.

At a concurrency of 1, Drama 3 decodes 271 frames per second on one
NVIDIA H200 GPU (NVIDIA Corporation, 2026a), against 177 for S2 measured
the same way. On one NVIDIA B300 GPU (Aubrey & Stam, 2025), it decodes
295 frames per second; there the experts use the microscaling FP8 format
(MXFP8) (Open Compute Project, 2023), converted from the same FP8
weights. The one-byte experts matter at larger batches too, where the
time of a step still depends mainly on the bytes it moves: on the H200,
the FP8 experts raise the per-stream decode rate at a concurrency of 32
by 36% over BF16 experts.

### 4.2 Kernel Launches Below CUDA Graphs

A step is a sequence of kernels, small programs that the host CPU
launches on the GPU, and every kernel costs twice: the host pays to
launch it, and the GPU pays a fixed cost to start and drain it. CUDA
graphs record a whole step once and replay it without the host issuing
kernels one by one, and programmatic dependent launch (PDL) (NVIDIA
Corporation, 2026d) lets a kernel start while the kernel it depends on
is still finishing; neither removes the fixed cost on the GPU. Before
the changes below, we took a census of Drama 3 decoding a single stream
on a B300, counting every kernel in a frame with CUDA graphs, PDL, and
piecewise prefill graphs (the prefill captured in pieces) enabled. The
median frame took 4.0 ms and ran 1,171 kernels, with only 0.09 ms of
gaps between them, so the gaps that graphs and PDL target were already
closed. Moving all the data the frame needs would take 0.76 ms at the
GPU’s memory bandwidth, one fifth of the frame; the other four fifths
went mostly to starting and draining kernels: about 830 kernels moved
almost no data yet took 2.7 ms. That time shrinks neither with more
bandwidth nor under graphs or PDL; the only remedy is fewer kernels.

Most of these 830 kernels come from auxiliary operations that a
general-purpose serving framework adds around the computation, such as
mask writes, tensor copies, layout changes, and type conversions. We
trace every kernel in the graph back to the framework operator that
produced it and decide whether its work is needed, then delete the
kernel or fold its work into an adjacent compute kernel. Fewer kernels
is not the goal in itself: the roughly 320 launches per step in the MoE
layers are the expert computation and stay. A kernel that fused the
router’s general matrix multiplication (GEMM), softmax, and top-8
selection into one launch took 14.5 $`\mu`$s, against 7.5 $`\mu`$s for
the kernels it would have replaced, so it was rejected. Under PDL, saved
kernel time also does not fully reach the wall clock: changes that saved
0.218 ms of kernel time per step gained 0.14–0.16 ms in serving. Every
change is therefore accepted on a same-machine A/B comparison, the old
and the new engine serving the same load on the same GPU, and Table 2
lists the accepted changes.

| Change                                                 | Kernels or time                          | Gain           | GPU, concurrency |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- | -------------- | ---------------- |
| Auxiliary decode kernels removed                       | 1,429 $`\to`$ 1,278 kernels per step     | +1.7%          | B300, 32         |
| Gated DeltaNet decode fused; sampler counter folded in | $`-`$100 kernels per step                | +4.2%          | B300, 1          |
| Gated DeltaNet prefill launches merged                 | 23 $`\to`$ 13 kernels per layer          | +4.4%          | B300, 128        |

Gains are in the per-stream decode rate.

Auxiliary kernels in decode. Every MoE layer launched kernels to write a
mask for the padding rows and to copy the shared expert’s input, every
Gated DeltaNet layer copied the input of its gated normalization into a
contiguous layout, and the routing weights were converted to another
data type. Now the routing kernel writes the mask, the copy is made only
when the shared expert computes in place, the normalization reads the
strided input directly, and the conversion is gone: 1,429 kernels per
decode step become 1,278, and the per-stream rate at a concurrency of 32
rises 1.7%. The Gated DeltaNet decode, previously three kernels and a
gated RMSNorm per layer, is now one kernel that updates the convolution
window, applies the gated delta rule, and normalizes, with bit-identical
output. The sampler advances its random-number counter inside the
sampling kernel instead of in a launch of its own. These two changes
remove 100 kernels per step at every concurrency, and the per-stream
rate at a concurrency of 1 rises 4.2%; the total per step still varies
with concurrency, mainly through the attention kernels, which are chosen
by batch size.

Auxiliary kernels in prefill. The Gated DeltaNet prefill operator
launched 23 kernels per layer, most of them copies, reads and writes of
per-request state, and index computations. It now reads the projection
tensor by stride, writes the boundary states and initializes the state
snapshots in one launch each, and computes its indices once per forward
pass rather than in every layer: 13 launches per layer, and the
per-stream rate at a concurrency of 128 rises 4.4%. Each prefill round
also produces the first frame of every new request, through semantic
sampling, the 13 decoder layers, and nine codebook samplings, about 100
kernels that the host launched one by one outside the graph. Capturing
this chain into CUDA graphs, together with the Gated DeltaNet prefill
chain, cut the host time per prefill round from 30.9 to 14.7 ms and
raised the per-stream rate at a concurrency of 32 by 15.3% (H200,
earlier development model); the Gated DeltaNet prefill operator now runs
entirely inside the prefill graph.

A wait outside the graph. In each prefill round, the attention backend
built its execution plan, the schedule of the round’s attention work,
from indices copied back from the GPU, a copy that waited for all queued
backbone work and left the GPU idle. We now build the plan from lengths
that the host already tracks, and the GPU idle time per prefill round at
a concurrency of 128 fell from 9.75 to 0.86 ms, a gain of about 7% in
the per-stream rate. Removing the wait exposed a data race: the plan’s
inputs reach the GPU by an asynchronous copy from a pinned host buffer,
host memory the GPU reads directly, and the old wait had kept the next
round from overwriting that buffer before the copy finished. Each
workspace, the buffers of one round, now has a CUDA event, a marker the
GPU records when it has finished reading, that fences the buffer with no
waiting in steady state.

Rounds that fell out of the graph. A CUDA graph helps only a round that
matches the form it was captured for. Every captured graph was guarded
by a condition on the batch size or the number of new requests, and a
round that failed the condition launched its kernels one by one. Nine
requests arriving at once were enough to cause this fallback. The
scheduler now admits only rounds that the prefill graphs can replay, and
the largest captured decode batch equals the admission limit, the most
streams the scheduler lets in, so every admitted round runs inside a
graph.

### 4.3 Shape-Driven Kernel Optimization

General-purpose kernel libraries are tuned for generic matrix shapes,
the sizes of the matrices a kernel multiplies. The shapes in TTS serving
are set by the workload: decode batches are small and fall into a few
sizes, the matrices are thin, the sampler touches only a small slice of
the vocabulary, and the Gated DeltaNet geometry is fixed by the model. A
kernel tuned for shapes that serving never produces gains nothing there,
so kernel optimization starts from the serving workload and iterates a
loop of five steps (Figure 5).

Figure 5: Shape-driven kernel optimization loop. An engineer writes the
contract for a round; a coding agent captures the serving shapes,
profiles, writes candidate kernels, and tests them, first on the
operator interface and then inside the engine. A candidate that fails
returns to profiling, and one that passes is deployed. WER: word error
rate.

_Shape capture_ reads the GEMM dimensions $`(M,N,K)`$ and batch sizes of
every operator from serving logs and graph replays; $`M`$, the number of
rows, is the batch size in decode. At a concurrency of 32, for example,
91.8% of decode batches have 31 or 32 rows, so a kernel tuned only for
$`M\leq 16`$ gains almost nothing in serving. _Profiling_ attributes the
time of each frame to its kernels with the profiler NVIDIA Nsight
Systems (NVIDIA Corporation, 2026c) and measures with Nsight
Compute (NVIDIA Corporation, 2026b) how far each kernel is from the
roofline (Williams et al., 2009), the ceiling set by the GPU’s memory
bandwidth or arithmetic rate. In a single-operator test at Drama 3’s
serving dimensions and batch size 256, for example, 97% of the memory
traffic of the Gated DeltaNet decode kernel is the full read and write
of the recurrent state, already at 81% of peak bandwidth. Faster
arithmetic cannot help such a kernel; only fewer bytes can, so on the
B300 the state is stored in BF16. _Optimization_ writes candidate
kernels from scratch or adapts upstream ones. _Interface testing_ checks
the operator alone for bitwise agreement with a reference and times it
with a cold L2 cache, one that does not already hold the weights, so
that the cache does not flatter small batches. To keep the cache cold,
the test rotates weight copies of at least twice the capacity of the L2,
the GPU’s on-chip cache, inside the graph. _In-engine validation_
accepts a candidate inside the inference engine on token-level agreement
and on the word error rate (WER, Section 5) at every concurrency level,
and the serving A/B comparison decides deployment.

The last two steps are kept apart because their verdicts often disagree;
we recorded nine such cases. The Gated DeltaNet prefill kernel, for
example, was 3.4 times faster as a kernel from the start but lost to the
existing one in three serving comparisons, because of graph capture,
state movement, and a cold compile at run time. Once these were
resolved, it became the default and raised throughput at a concurrency
of 256 by 11.1%.

Each round of the loop is executed by a coding agent in its own
workspace, a separate copy of the code, under a written contract in
which an engineer sets the goal, constraints, validation commands, and
deployment criteria. A second agent re-derives each conclusion from the
primary evidence, and only candidates that pass every check are
deployed. The FP8 MoE kernels of Drama 3 were developed and iterated in
this loop with the Kernel Design Agents workflow (NVIDIA Research,
2026). The FP8 MoE kernel cuts the MoE layer time at $`M=256`$ by 19.2%
and raises the per-stream rate in serving at a concurrency of 1 by 3.0%.

### 4.4 Prefill–Decode Disaggregation

The changes so far make each frame cheaper; this one keeps new requests
from interrupting the streams already playing, since decoding that falls
behind playback is heard as a stall. When one GPU serves both phases,
every arriving request makes the scheduler insert a prefill round, and
during that round no running stream emits a frame (Figure 6a). On
single-GPU workers, these rounds were the main cause of interrupted
streams at high concurrency. A request whose reference audio is already
cached needs only a short prefill, but at high request rates such
prefills arrive very often, and the longer prefill of a cold request,
whose reference audio is not cached, stalls every stream at once.

Prefill–decode disaggregation (PD) (Zhong et al., 2024; Patel et al., 2024) runs the two phases on different GPUs (Figure 6b). A PD unit
consists of a load balancer, a prefill process, and a decode process.

Figure 6: Schematic timelines of streaming on one GPU (a) and with
prefill–decode disaggregation (b). On one GPU, every prefill pauses all
running streams, and the long prefill of a cold request, one whose
reference audio is not cached, causes a playback stall. With
disaggregation, the prefill GPU runs the same prefills, each finished
prefill writes its KV and recurrent state into the decode GPU, and the
streams play without gaps.

When a prefill finishes, its KV entries and its Gated DeltaNet states
are written within the node straight into the paged KV cache (Kwon
et al., 2023) and the state buffers of the decode process, in packed
transfers with an end-to-end checksum. The transfer uses NVLink fabric
handles on B300 and a PCIe peer-to-peer proxy on NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000
GPUs (NVIDIA Corporation, 2026e). Each role, prefill or decode, captures
only the CUDA graphs it needs, so a unit becomes ready in 47 s instead
of 148 s.

PD already runs in the production service of Drama 3 on RTX PRO 6000
GPUs. A capacity test on one RTX PRO 6000 node applies a closed-loop
streaming load (each finished stream is replaced by a new one) that
reproduces the production mix. The bar is no playback stall and a
per-stream rate of at least 35 frames per second at the first
percentile, that is, for all but the slowest one percent of streams.
Under this bar, a unit of one prefill and one decode GPU carries 24
streams per GPU, averaged over both, against about 10 for a single GPU.
In a production canary, a trial on live traffic with PD units and
single-GPU workers behind the same router, the PD decode GPUs carry
about twice as many streams as the single-GPU workers. The first
percentile of their per-stream rates is 55 frames per second, against
45–47 for the single-GPU workers. No PD stream falls below the service
floor of 42 frames per second, whereas a small share of single-GPU
streams does. The character error rate (CER, Section 5) of PD streams is
equivalent to that of single-GPU serving, a difference of 0.02 points.

## 5 Evaluation

We evaluate Drama 3 on public benchmarks. Multilingual cloning is
measured on the MiniMax Multilingual Testset (Zhang et al., 2025).
Editing by infilling is measured on Ming-Freeform-Audio-Edit (Yan
et al., 2025a), and revision from a note in the next turn on the
Step-Audio-Edit benchmark (Yan et al., 2025b). All results come from a
single Drama 3 model, whereas most baselines are built for one kind of
task. We also analyze what the single preference reward changes
(Section 5.2) and measure cloning from several references and streaming
synthesis.

### 5.1 Evaluation Setup

Drama 3 is run with the prompt formats of Appendix B and with each
benchmark’s own reference audio, reference text, and target text,
sampling at temperature 0.7 with top-$`p`$ 0.7 and top-$`k`$ 30. Outputs
that fail simple signal checks, such as silent output, too little
speech, runaway length, or long stretches of noise or silence, are
regenerated and rescored. Baseline numbers are quoted from their papers,
except where a table marks a baseline as run by us. Unless a section or
table states otherwise, error rates follow each benchmark’s official
protocol, with the character error rate (CER) for Chinese, Japanese, and
Korean and the word error rate (WER) for the other languages. Unless
stated otherwise, we transcribe Chinese with Paraformer-zh (Gao et al.,
2023b) and the other languages with Whisper-large-v3 (Radford et al.,
2023), and speaker similarity (SIM) is the cosine similarity of the
speaker embeddings of WavLM-large (Chen et al., 2022) with an ECAPA-TDNN
head (Desplanques et al., 2020), the speaker model of Seed-TTS-Eval.
Where the conditions of Drama 3 and the baselines differ on a benchmark,
we state the difference where the benchmark is introduced.

### 5.2 Preference Reward vs. Rule-Based Rewards

Reinforcement learning in Drama 3 optimizes a single learned reward of
human preference and uses no transcription or speaker-similarity reward
(Section 3.3). The rewards it leaves out penalize delivery in the
requested style. In Expresso (Nguyen et al., 2023), each of four
speakers reads the same sentences in a default style and in several
other styles. We pair every styled reading with the same speaker’s
default reading of the same sentence, so the two readings of a pair
differ only in style. With one default-style recording of each speaker
as the SIM reference, every style lowers SIM below the default reading,
by 0.019 for clearly enunciated reading and by 0.310 for whispering, so
a similarity reward prefers the default reading in 62.0% to 99.7% of the
pairs, depending on the style (Table 3). Reading with a laugh raises WER
by 0.79 points, so a WER reward penalizes laughing.

The reward model of Drama 3 instead responds to the direction. The voice
reference is a default-style clip of the same speaker. We score both
readings of each pair with the reward model twice, once with the style
written as a direction in the syntax of Appendix C, such as
\<whisper\>...\</whisper\> or \[laughing\], and once with the sentence
alone. With the direction, the reward model prefers the whispered
reading in 62.2% of pairs and the laughing reading in 64.4%, against
10.6% and 27.1% without it, whereas a similarity reward prefers the
default reading in 99.7% and 93.9% of these pairs. In every style, the
direction raises the share of pairs in which the reward model prefers
the styled reading (Table 3).

### 5.4 Streaming Synthesis

Each sentence is synthesized on its own in two modes: streamed, where
its audio is sent out chunk by chunk while the rest is still being
generated, and non-streamed, where the whole take is sent out once it is
finished. We do this for all 1,088 Seed-TTS-Eval test-en sentences.
Streaming costs no quality: on the same sentences, streamed takes reach
a WER of 1.86 and non-streamed takes 1.83, and both reach a SIM of
0.640.

### 5.5 Editing by Infilling

Drama 3 edits a recording by infilling (Le et al., 2023; Peng et al.,
2024). We align the source audio to its transcript with
Qwen3-ForcedAligner-0.6B (Shi et al., 2026). The edit window runs from
the first to the last word that differs between the source and target
texts, with characters as words in Chinese. A deletion leaves no new
text, so its window also covers the unchanged word before it, or after
it at the start of the clip. Drama 3 regenerates the audio inside the
window with the infilling prompt of Figure 14, which holds the full
target text and the audio tokens of the source before and after the
window. The whole token sequence, with the new tokens inside the window,
is then decoded to audio.

Ming-Freeform-Audio-Edit, which we shorten to Ming-Freeform, asks for
deletions, insertions, and substitutions in English and Chinese. Each
item gives a source recording, its transcript, an edit instruction, and
the target text, and Drama 3 edits from the target text. The basic split
has instructions of limited variety, and the larger full split, which
the benchmark’s evaluation script calls open, has more natural and
varied instructions and more substitutions. We score the outputs with
the protocol of CosyEdit (Chen et al., 2026b) and CosyEdit2 (Chen
et al., 2026a), which reports WER, SIM against the source recording, and
MAE$`{}_{\text{D}}`$. MAE$`{}_{\text{D}}`$ is the mean absolute
difference between the DNSMOS P.835 overall score (Reddy et al., 2021)
of the edited clip and that of its source, and it is small when the edit
leaves the perceived quality of the recording unchanged. Table 7

### 5.6 Multi-Turn Revision

The Step-Audio-Edit benchmark (Yan et al., 2025b) clones a voice and
then asks for up to three rounds of revision toward a target emotion or
speaking style, or for one round that adds a paralinguistic event, such
as a laugh or a breath, at a marked position. Iteration 0 is plain
cloning, and each later iteration revises the most recent take.
Gemini-2.5-Pro (Comanici et al., 2025) judges every take with the
official prompts, and we score its answers with the official scripts.
For emotion and style, the judge names the one emotion or style it
hears, and the accuracy is the share of takes named with their target.
The emotion labels are the five targets and a neutral one. For style,
the official script counts only the takes named with one of the seven
target styles. Where the official script has no valid takes for a
speaker and a style, that pair is left out of the average. For the
paralinguistic event, the judge gives a score from 1 to 3, where 1 means
the event is missing or misplaced, 2 means it is present with a flaw
such as a faint sound or an audible join, and 3 means it is clear, in
place, and seamless. Drama 3 revises with a note in the next turn, such
as \[happier versus previous\], and keeps the original reference and the
most recent take in context, which corresponds to the Prompt-Fixed
setting of the Step-Audio-EditX paper, so we compare with
Step-Audio-EditX in that setting. Paralinguistic events are added by
inserting a point tag such as \[coughs\] at the target position and
infilling the audio there.

Table 9 shows that every revision round raises the emotion and style
accuracy of Drama 3, from 55.5 to 58.2 on emotion and from 76.5 to 81.8
on style over three rounds. Adding the event with a point tag and
infilling lifts its paralinguistic score from 1.32 to 2.32.

## 6 Conclusion

In this report, we present Fish Audio Drama 3, a speech generation model
that turns text into directed performances. It clones voices from
multiple references, follows natural-language direction, revises a take
when the next turn gives a note, performs multi-character scenes, edits
existing speech by infilling, and synthesizes long-form and streaming
audio. These capabilities rest on three design choices. A hybrid
linear-attention mixture-of-experts backbone is paired with a codebook
multi-token-prediction decoder (Section 2) whose layers attend over the
whole sequence, so acoustic detail is predicted with direct access to
the reference audio and all previous frames. Multi-turn revision, scene
dubbing, editing by infilling, and streaming text input are supported by
one model, each written as a conversation over the same token stream
(Section 3, _Conversation format_). Reinforcement learning (Section 3.3)
uses a single learned human-preference reward and no rule-based rewards,
which would penalize laughs, pauses, and whispers. Serving these
capabilities is the work of the inference engine (Section 4), which runs
the codebook-MTP decoder once per frame and the experts in the
block-scaled FP8 of the RL rollouts, removes the kernel launches that
CUDA graphs leave in place, optimizes kernels on the shapes of live
serving, and separates prefill from decode so that new requests do not
stall running streams. On one H200, Drama 3 decodes a single stream at
271 frames per second against 177 for S2, with more than seven times as
many parameters. We evaluate this single model on voice cloning,
streaming synthesis, editing by infilling, and multi-turn revision,
mostly on public benchmarks (Section 5). We hope Fish Audio Drama 3
serves as an open foundation for expressive, directable speech
generation.

## Appendix A Pre-training Sequence Formats

Figures 7–10 show the TTS conversation of Stages 1 and 2 and the
long-form, line-separated, and streaming sequences of Stage 3; text
documents are plain text. {{...}} stands for a span of audio frames, and
long lines are broken at spaces for display. \<\|voice\|\> is the
modality token that opens an audio turn. The system instructions are the
ones in use; all other text is illustrative. In every sequence, loss
falls only on the audio frames of the assistant turns, on the separators
of the line-separated form, and on the closing \<\|im_end\|\>. Speech
infilling uses the layout of Figure 14; its loss covers either the whole
middle or only its first and last few frames and the closing token, so
the seams are always supervised.

 

```ltx_verbatim
<|im_start|>system
convert the provided text to speech reference to the following:

Text:
<|speaker:0|> I told you already, the train leaves at seven.

Speech:
{{reference clip}}<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
<|speaker:0|> [sighs] Fine. If we miss it, it’s on you. [audio tokens: 60] [language: en]<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<|voice|>{{utterance 1}}<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
<|speaker:0|> Seven sharp. I’ll be at the door.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<|voice|>{{utterance 2}}<|im_end|>
```

 

Figure 7: Stages 1–2: a multi-turn TTS conversation.

The sequence of Figure 7 teaches plain synthesis and cloning.
Consecutive utterances of one recording follow each other as user and
assistant turns, with any reference clips in the system turn (Section 3,
_Conversation format_), and the user text may carry point tags and
language and duration hints.

 

```ltx_verbatim
<|im_start|>system
convert the provided text to speech reference to the following:

Text:
{{transcript of a clip from later in the recording}}

Speech:
{{that clip}}<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
<|speaker:0|> {{transcript of the recording up to the clip}}<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<|voice|>{{the recording up to the clip}}<|im_end|>
```

 

Figure 8: Stage 3: long-form synthesis. The reference clip is cut from
later in the same recording.

The sequence of Figure 8 voices a long recording from its transcript in
one turn. Its reference clips are cut from later in the same recording
and the target stops where the first clip begins, so no audio is both
context and target. With several clips, the system turn either
concatenates them as in S2 or gives each its own numbered block, at
random. Some of these sequences have no reference; the system turn then
holds only the instruction and the loss covers the whole recording.

 

```ltx_verbatim
<|im_start|>system
convert the provided text to speech reference to the following:

Text:
{{transcript of a clip from later in the recording}}

Speech:
{{that clip}}

convert the provided text to speech; the lines are separated by <|audio_separator|>
— synthesize each line in order and output <|audio_separator|> between adjacent audio
segments<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
It was late.
<|audio_separator|>
The hall was empty.
<|audio_separator|>
Nobody answered.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<|voice|>{{line 1}}
<|audio_separator|>
{{line 2}}
<|audio_separator|>
{{line 3}}<|im_end|>
```

 

Figure 9: Stage 3: line-separated synthesis. The reference clip precedes
the line instruction in the system turn.

The sequence of Figure 9 voices one text line by line. The separator
\<\|audio_separator\|\> joins the lines in the user turn, and the model
emits one audio segment per line with the separator between segments;
its reference clips are chosen as in Figure 8, and some of these
sequences carry none. The separators are supervised outputs, so the
model learns where segments end and how many there are. The separators
act as anchors between the text and the audio. If the model skips or
repeats a sentence inside one segment, it still starts the next segment
from the next line, so the mistake does not spread; and if the number of
segments differs from the number of lines, the run-on is detected.

 

```ltx_verbatim
<|im_start|>system
streaming speech continuation with one-chunk lookahead split by <|endofturn|><|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
It was late<|endofturn|>and the hall was empty.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<|voice|>{{"It was late"}}<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
and the hall was empty.<|endofturn|>Nobody answered.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<|voice|>{{"and the hall was empty."}}<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
Nobody answered.<|endofturn|><|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<|voice|>{{"Nobody answered."}}<|im_end|>
```

 

Figure 10: Stage 3: streaming interleave.

The sequence of Figure 10 is for text that arrives in pieces. Each user
turn holds the chunk to voice and, after \<\|endofturn\|\>, the chunk
that follows, so the model knows what comes next while voicing the
current chunk; an empty next chunk marks the last round. Chunks are
consecutive spans of the transcript.

## Appendix B Prompt Examples

Figures 11–14 render the main prompt formats in full. As in Appendix A,
{{...}} stands for a span of audio frames. In the backbone stream, each
frame occupies one position, entered as the fused embedding of its codes
0 and 1; codes 2–9 enter the decoder as side input (Section 2.3). The
system instructions of the cloning, directing, and infilling prompts are
shown exactly as used; all other text is illustrative, and long lines
are broken at spaces for display. Loss applies only to assistant turns
(Section 3, _Conversation format_), in a spoken turn to all ten
codebooks of every supervised frame and to the closing \<\|im_end\|\>;
the infilling format additionally weights its loss toward the seams
(Appendix A). At inference, the prompt ends with the last \<\|voice\|\>,
and the model generates frames until it emits \<\|im_end\|\>.

 

```ltx_verbatim
<|im_start|>system
convert the provided text to speech reference to the following:

[Reference 1]
Text: <|speaker:0|> I told you already, the train leaves at seven.
Speech: {{voice A, clip 1}}

[Reference 2]
Text: <|speaker:0|> Fine. But if we miss it, that’s on you.
Speech: {{voice A, clip 2}}

[Reference 3]
Text: <|speaker:1|> Seven? Nobody said anything about seven.
Speech: {{voice B}}<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
<|speaker:0|> [whispering] Don’t wake the baby.
<|audio_separator|>
<|speaker:1|> [laughing] Okay, okay, I’m going.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<|voice|>{{turn 1}}<|audio_separator|>{{turn 2}}<|im_end|>
```

 

Figure 11: Cloning a two-speaker dialogue from three reference clips,
two of one voice.

Figure 11 casts two voices from three reference clips. Speaker tokens
are global to the request (Section 3, _Conversation format_). The
identity in \<\|speaker:S\|\> is independent of the block number, so
References 1 and 2, two clips of one voice, carry the same speaker
token, and Reference 3 introduces the second voice.

 

```ltx_verbatim
<|im_start|>system
convert the provided text to speech reference to the following:

[Reference 1]
Text: <|speaker:0|> Honestly, I thought we had a real shot this year.
Speech: {{reference clip}}<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
<|speaker:0|> [with heavy, deflated sadness] The results just came in. We didn’t make it.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<|voice|>{{take 1}}<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
<|speaker:0|> [softer and warmer versus previous] The results just came in. We didn’t make it.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<|voice|>{{take 2}}<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
<|speaker:0|> Direction: more amused than before; drop the hurt.
Line: The results just came in. We didn’t make it.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<|voice|>{{take 3}}<|im_end|>
```

 

Figure 12: Multi-turn directing, in which each later turn gives a note
on the previous take and the model produces a new one.

In Figure 12, turn 1 gives an absolute direction as a style prefix,
turn 2 a relative note as a bracketed prefix, and turn 3 a relative note
in the director’s-note register. At inference, the earlier takes are the
audio that the model generated in previous turns, so each note is
interpreted against what the listener actually heard.

 

```ltx_verbatim
<|im_start|>system
Scene: a small office on a Monday morning; the weekly meeting is about to start.
Characters:
- Mr. Carter: steady male voice, about 40, formal.
- Emma: young female voice, sleepy, half talking to herself.
Voice every line of the script in order, with <|audio_separator|> between consecutive lines.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
Mr. Carter: [clears throat][serious] Good morning, everyone. Let’s get started.
<|audio_separator|>
Mr. Carter: [reading from a sheet] First item: the launch date.
<|audio_separator|>
Emma: [softly][to herself] Here we go again...<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<|voice|>{{line 1}}<|audio_separator|>{{line 2}}<|audio_separator|>{{line 3}}<|im_end|>
```

 

Figure 13: Multi-character scene dubbing from text descriptions.

In Figure 13, whose content is illustrative, the whole scene is one turn
and every line is given up front. Characters are cast from their text
descriptions rather than from reference audio, each line starts with its
speaker’s name, and the separators are supervised outputs, so the model
learns to emit exactly one audio segment per line, also when one
character speaks consecutive lines (lines 1 and 2). The line-separated
long-form format uses the same layout, with the lines of one text joined
by separators in the user turn and one audio segment per line with
supervised separators in the output. Only the scene and character
descriptions are absent.

 

```ltx_verbatim
<|im_start|>system
fill in the missing part of the speech; the suffix follows <|fim_suffix|> and the prefix follows
<|fim_prefix|><|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
<|speaker:0|> The demo is Thursday, not Friday. [audio tokens: 14]
<|fim_suffix|>{{audio of ", not Friday."}}<|fim_prefix|>{{audio of "The demo is"}}<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<|voice|>{{new audio of "Thursday"}}<|im_end|>
```

 

Figure 14: Speech editing by infilling.

Figure 14 edits a recording by replacing one spoken word (“Wednesday”
becomes “Thursday”). The prefix audio is the original recording before
the edited word and the suffix audio is the recording after it, while
the user turn carries the full new transcript, including the replacement
word. Following the suffix-prefix-middle ordering of fill-in-the-middle
training (Bavarian et al., 2022), the suffix comes before the prefix, so
the prefix sits directly next to the generated audio and the model
continues from it; the optional duration hint gives the length of the
new span in frames.

## Appendix C Control Syntax Reference

Table 10 lists the controls that Drama 3 reads in its prompts.
Structural tokens, written \<\|name\|\>, are single vocabulary entries;
every other control is ordinary text. Section 3 names the training stage
at which each format is introduced, and Appendix B shows complete
prompts.

[TABLE]

Table 10: Drama 3 control syntax.

Conventions. A control is a short span in square or angle brackets,
typically a few words, without nested brackets or line breaks; text in
round parentheses is spoken. The direction controls (point tags, scoped
tags, style prefixes, director’s notes, and relative notes) are free
natural language, so any wording can be used and Table 10 only gives
examples. A point tag acts at its position, a scoped tag on the enclosed
span, and a style prefix, director’s note, or relative note on the whole
line. Language and duration hints condition the output and are never
spoken; the language hint is optional and names the main language of a
request, and each line is spoken in the language of its own text.
