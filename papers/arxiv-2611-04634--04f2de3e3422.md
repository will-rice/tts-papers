## 1  Introduction

Speech deepfakes pose a significant security threat nowadays. A
suspicious speech recording can raise several related questions: whether
it is synthetic, which system produced it, and whether another recording
came from the same source. Consider a voice message submitted as
evidence. A deepfake detector can tell an analyst that it is probably
synthetic, but the analyst’s next questions are harder: which system
produced it, whether it came from the same system as another message in
the case, and on what grounds the detector believes so. The answers link
a recording to a tool, and through the tool to a person or a campaign; a
conclusion that cannot be explained cannot be contested.

Research has made the first question tractable: detectors trained on
large and diverse corpora reach low error rates across many benchmarks
\[2, 3, 1\]. Source attribution, verification, and explanation have also
been studied. For a forensic workflow, open detectors leave three
problems.

1.  1.  One score answers one question. Source tracing assigns a recording
        to a closed set of known systems or attributes \[4, 5\], while
        comparing two recordings needs a separate Siamese network \[6, 7\].
        Each further question costs another model, trained on its own data.
2.  2.  Explanations cannot serve as evidence. Audio language models (ALMs)
        for deepfakes add explanations \[8, 9, 10\], but these are free text
        written or distilled by a teacher model. Whether a stated
        observation is true of the recording is not checked, and whether the
        decision depends on it is not tested. Such an explanation can be
        fluent and wrong, or correct and irrelevant to the decision \[11,
        12\].
3.  3.  It is unclear where to invest. Speech-recognition encoders have been
        argued to discard the artifacts of synthesis \[13\], and source
        labels are often assumed to help detection \[8\] without controlling
        for the number of authenticity labels or training steps. Which part
        of an ALM limits detection, and which limits comparing recordings,
        has not been measured.

VeriSpoof addresses all three problems in a single model: it unifies
detection, attribution, recording-condition analysis, localisation and
source verification, and accompanies every decision with rationales that
can be checked statement by statement against the signal. For the first,
it casts every forensic question as a question with candidate answers
written as sentences and reads the likelihood of each answer as a score,
so one model detects spoofs, names the generator, its acoustic model and
vocoder, describes the recording channel and verifies whether two
recordings share a source (Figure 3). For the second, it writes
rationales in fixed fields, each with a target computed from the exact
audio the model hears, so that every statement has a check without
trusting the model. Training on counterfactual rationales further
ensures that the decision follows the stated evidence (Figure 2). For
the third, we run controlled studies that change one component or one
form of supervision at a time on the same test trials, measuring which
part of the model limits detection and which limits source comparison.

Both models are released: VeriSpoof-Base, trained on the official
training splits of eight corpora, and VeriSpoof-Plus, which extends Base
with twelve additional corpora and a larger balanced training mixture
(§4), together with the inference code with a vLLM \[14\] backend and a
hosted demo. We also release VeriSpoof-Bench, the evaluation suite of
this report: 54 test subsets with 177,852 items covering detection,
attribution, source verification, recording conditions, grounded
reasoning, and faithfulness, with scoring scripts and the per-item
outputs of both models.

## 2  Related work

#### Speech deepfake detection

Most detectors pair a self-supervised front-end with a light back-end
\[15, 16, 17\]. Recent work improves generalization through spectral
residuals \[18\], high-order feature interactions \[19\],
style–linguistics mismatch \[20\], dedicated pre-training \[13\], or
data composition at scale \[2, 3\]. Evaluation has moved from ASVspoof
\[21, 22\] and In-the-Wild \[23\] to multi-set boards such as
Speech-DF-Arena \[1\] and SpoofRadar \[24\]. These systems return one
score per recording; VeriSpoof returns its detection score as the
likelihood of its own answer and is evaluated on both boards.

#### Source tracing and verification

Source tracing assigns a recording to a known system or to attributes
such as its acoustic model and vocoder \[4, 5\], on corpora with many
generators such as MLAAD \[25\] and SpoofCeleb \[26\]. Dedicated Siamese
or two-branch networks score whether two recordings come from the same
generator \[6, 7\]. We pose this comparison to the model that also
detects and explains, on open-set protocols with unseen generators or
architectures.

#### Audio language models for deepfakes

HoliAntiSpoof unifies detection, method recognition and localization
\[8\], ThinkOmni generates reasoning with localization \[9\], and ICLAD
prompts an ALM with comparison examples \[10\]. However, general-purpose
ALMs \[27, 28, 29, 30\] are not trained for these tasks. Their
rationales, where present, are free text from a teacher model and are
not checked against the signal: ICLAD reports hallucinated explanations,
and ThinkOmni’s training rationales are written by a model shown the
labels. SpeakerLLM \[31\] unifies speaker profiling, recording
conditions and verification reasoning for speakers.

#### Faithful explanations

In NLP, an explanation is faithful when the decision depends on it,
which is tested by editing the rationale and observing the decision
\[32, 12, 11\]. We apply such tests to audio, with edit forms held out
from training, and train for them with counterfactual rationales.

### 3.1  Problem Formulation: Forensic Questions as Answer Scoring

Let $`u`$ denote the input, either a single recording $`x`$ or a pair
$`(x,x^{\prime})`$, and $`q`$ a question with a set of candidate answers
$`\mathcal{A}_{q}`$ each written as sentences. An ALM with parameters
$`\theta`$ assigns each answer $`a\in\mathcal{A}_{q}`$, the likelihood
$`p_{\theta}(a\mid u,q)`$ of its tokens. For a binary yes–no question,
let $`a^{+}`$ and $`a^{-}`$ denote the positive and negative answers; we
read the score,

```math
s_{q}(u)={}\log p_{\theta}(a^{+}\mid u,q)-\log p_{\theta}(a^{-}\mid u,q). \tag{1}
```

which can be evaluated by equal error rate (EER) and thresholded at a
chosen false-alarm rate. Questions with more answers are scored by the
accuracy of the most likely one. VeriSpoof answers four families of
questions (Figure 5): _authenticity_; _attribution_ of the generator,
acoustic model or vocoder; _recording condition_ (noise, reverberation,
codec, post-processing); and _source verification_ on pairs (same
generator, acoustic model or vocoder?). No question has its own output
head. For detection, for example, the question is “Is this recording
genuine human speech or spoofed speech? Options: bona fide; spoof.” and
the two answers are full sentences (“This recording is a spoof: it is
synthetic or converted speech, not genuine human speech.”); the released
models label a recording spoofed when $`s_{q}(x)>0`$. Figure 10 shows
the template of every question with an example answer.

Source verification is not the same as comparing attributes: two
recordings from different systems can share every attribute class (e.g.,
generator family, acoustic model and vocoder), so a model that answers
“same source” whenever the attributes agree fails on such pairs.
Source-verification test sets therefore balance three strata: SAME,
REVERSAL (different systems, every attribute class shared) and DIFF, so
that attribute agreement alone does not reveal the answer.

### 3.2  Model

#### Perception in two resolutions

A Qwen3-ASR encoder \[33\] reads an 8 s window of the recording. A
learned weighted sum of the hidden states of all 25 of its layers is
passed to an audio tokenizer with two outputs: 16 utterance tokens,
which carry what is constant across the recording and serve attribution
and verification, and 52 frame tokens of 0.16 s, which preserve temporal
information. Time markers every 0.8 s are inserted between frame tokens
as text, so that the LLM can state spans in seconds. The encoder is
adapted with DoRA \[34\] ($`r{=}16`$) during the first training stage
only and frozen afterwards. For a pair, the tokens of both recordings
are concatenated in the prompt.

#### LLM and read-out

The LLM is MiniCPM5-2B \[35, 36\], adapted with LoRA \[37\] ($`r{=}16`$,
$`\alpha{=}32`$) on the attention projections. The model has 2.85 B
parameters, of which 29.7 M are trained (§6.9). The model is trained
with next-token prediction on the answer tokens and answers are scored
by Eq. (1). Two auxiliary losses support perception: a one-class
authenticity loss \[38, OC-softmax;\] on the pooled audio tokens, which
pulls bona fide speech towards a learned centre, and a per-token head
that classifies each frame token as genuine or synthesized from the
LLM’s hidden state. The per-token head gives a synthetic-speech profile
over time at no extra cost and serves as a reference for how much
localization information the hidden states carry (§6.6). The model has
two output modes: a one-step answer scored by Eq. (1), and a rationale
followed by a decision, described in §3.3.

### 3.3  Rationales written from what the model hears

For forensic use, a decision must come with evidence that can be
verified and contested, so VeriSpoof explains its decisions with
rationales whose every statement can be checked independently of the
model. A rationale $`r=(r_{1},\dots,r_{k};d)`$ is a sequence of
statements in fixed fields followed by a decision $`d`$. Each statement
type $`\tau`$ has a checker
$`c_{\tau}(x,r_{i})\in\{\text{true},\text{false}\}`$ that does not
involve the model:

- •
  observations (bandwidth class, SNR, speech level, clipping, digital
  silence) are checked against measurements on the waveform;
- •
  channel_status (noise, reverberation, codec) against the logged
  recording condition;
- •
  localization (synthesized spans in seconds, or none) against labelled
  spans;
- •
  source_profile (generation method, acoustic modelling, waveform
  generation) against generator metadata.

A rationale is _grounded_ if every statement has a checker, so its
correctness can be measured statement by statement. Channel statements
describe the recording, not its authenticity, and are declared
non-evidential. The data loader computes them directly from the exact
window the model receives after augmentation: it measures observations
on the waveform, takes channel statements from the augmentation log,
maps labelled spans to window time, and reads the source profile from
generator metadata. Each target is therefore true of its input, and the
decision, written last, follows from the localization: a recording is
labeled spoofed if and only if at least one synthesized span is stated,
and genuine otherwise. The decision $`d`$ is the final generated token
sequence after the rationale fields; it is read as a score by Eq. (1)
when thresholding is needed, or taken as the generated text directly.
Verification rationales have the fields channel_status, fingerprint,
profile_compatibility and decision; they state the fingerprint verdict
before comparing attributes, because the attribute comparison can
mislead the decision on REVERSAL pairs (§6.7). Figure 11 shows one
output of each kind in the Appendix.

### 3.4  Three training stages

Training has three stages, each with its own trainable parts (Table 19).

1.  1.  Perception alignment (PA) trains the tokenizer and the encoder’s
        DoRA adaptors on detection and source questions with the LLM frozen,
        so that the audio representation itself learns the relevant
        information before the LLM can compensate for weak audio tokens. The
        representation study of §6.3 is run at this stage for the same
        reason.
2.  2.  Forensic question tuning (QT) adds LoRA on the LLM and trains it on
        all perception questions, a form of multi-task instruction tuning:
        acoustic model, vocoder, source profile, recording condition, and
        pair questions on generator, acoustic model, vocoder and the
        authenticity of both recordings.
3.  3.  Grounded rationale tuning (RT) introduces detection and verification
        rationales and counterfactual examples at a lower learning rate. To
        preserve the one-step answering ability acquired in QT, the
        question-answering tasks of QT are replayed throughout this stage.

Each question has several paraphrases. Two models share this recipe and
differ in their training data (§4): VeriSpoof-Base, the model of all
controlled studies, and VeriSpoof-Plus. Counterfactual examples and
their training procedure are described in §3.5.

### 3.5  Counterfactual training

Counterfactual training is applied in stage 3 (grounded rationale
tuning). Grounded rationales alone do not make the decision depend on
the rationale: the model can decide from the audio and write a matching
rationale beside it. To make the decision follow the rationale, in 20%
of the rationale examples we give the model a rationale whose
localization is reversed (a labelled span replaced by “none”, or “none”
replaced by a span) and train only the decision that follows. The model
is never taught to state something false about the audio. It is taught
that the decision must follow the localization it is given. We further
test faithfulness with interventions: a statement $`r_{i}`$ is replaced
by $`\tilde{r}_{i}`$ and the model decides again. The decision is
faithful if it follows an edited localization (“no synthesized segment”
$`\Rightarrow`$ genuine) and remains the same when an edited
non-evidential statement is edited. Besides the edit form used in
training, we test held-out forms: a paraphrased localization, an
inserted span, and contradicted channel or observation statements.

### 3.6  Inference

One-step answers are scored by the likelihood of each candidate answer
sentence; rationales are generated greedily. The released package
exposes detection (detect), rationales with the per-token profile
(explain), source verification at the generator, acoustic-model or
vocoder level (same_source), verification rationales (compare), and
closed questions on recording conditions and on generators from a list
the user supplies. A vLLM \[14\] backend generates rationales from the
same prompt embeddings (§6.9).

## 4  Training data and recipe

Figure 4 summarizes the training data of both models.

#### VeriSpoof-Base

Base uses the official training splits of eight detection corpora,
ASVspoof 2019 LA \[21\], ASVspoof 5 \[22\], SpoofCeleb \[26\], MLAAD
\[25\], STOPA \[5\], DFADD \[39\], LibriSeVoc \[40\] and CodecFake+
\[41\], and the partially spoofed corpora PartialSpoof \[42\], HAD
\[43\] and PartialEdit \[44\] for rationales: about 0.38 M utterances.
No evaluation split of any corpus is used. Base serves every controlled
study of this report, so that its results are not tied to a particular
choice of extra data.

#### VeriSpoof-Plus

Plus keeps the recipe and adds twelve corpora, spoofed speech from
ASVspoof 2015 \[45\], ADD 2022 train / dev \[46\], Fake-or-Real \[47,
normalized version;\], ReplayDF \[48\], SpeechFake \[49\], WaveFake
\[50\] and Codecfake \[51, codec conditions F01–F06;\], and bona fide
speech from a Common Voice 15.0 subset \[52\], LibriSpeech \[53\],
AISHELL-3 \[54\], GigaSpeech \[55\] and CN-Celeb2 \[56\], 4.0 M
utterances more. Its spoofed half is balanced over 211 sources (one
generator of one corpus; MLAAD generators grouped into 82 architecture
families) and its bona fide half over 18 datasets, so that the large
added corpora do not dominate.

#### Held-out generators

The MLAAD generators of the unseen-model and unseen-architecture
verification protocols are removed from all training data, including
four SpeechFake systems that are among them (ChatTTS, FireRedTTS,
Parler-TTS, StyleTTS 2). Codecfake and CodecFake+ are different corpora:
CodecFake+ has a codec re-synthesis part (“CodecFake+” in our tables)
and a part generated by codec-based speech generators (CoSG), whose
generators are unseen in training.

#### Augmentation

Training copies carry labelled noise MUSAN \[57\], reverberation and
codecs, including narrowband codecs, drawn anew for every example
because the encoder runs inside the training loop. The
recording-condition questions and the channel_status statements are
trained on these labels. Post-processing (silence trimming or padding,
gain with clipping, high- or band-pass filtering) is applied identically
to both classes, without naming it in the answer, which removes channel
shortcuts (§6.4); on the corpora added for Plus, augmentation is applied
without naming it either.

#### Recipe

Base trains 8k / 20k / 10k steps in the three stages at a global batch
of 32 on one 48 GB GPU, about 14 GPU-hours (§6.9). Plus runs perception
alignment for six balanced epochs (79.2k steps) at a global batch of 64
on two GPUs and keeps the step counts of the other two stages. Learning
rates fall from $`10^{-4}`$ in perception alignment to
$`2{\times}10^{-5}`$ (LoRA) in rationale tuning. Questions are sampled
by task weights; the full settings are in Table 19 and the training data
in Figure 4.

## 5  Evaluation suite

#### Public detection boards

For detection we score every line of the 14 official protocols of
Speech-DF-Arena \[1\]: ASVspoof 2019 LA (LA19), 2021 LA and DF (LA21,
DF21) \[58\], ASVspoof 5 (ASV5), In-the-Wild (ITW) \[23\], Fake-or-Real
(FoR), Codecfake (Codec), ADD 2022 tracks 1 and 3 (A22T1, A22T3), ADD
2023 rounds 1 and 2 (A23R1, A23R2) \[59\], DFADD, LibriSeVoc (LSV) and
SONAR, 2.34 M trials in total. We report the pooled EER, which uses one
threshold over all sets and decides the board ranking, and the mean over
sets, and compare with the board’s public snapshot of 31 Oct 2026. We
also score the 16 sets of the SpoofRadar v1.0 protocol \[24\], which
adds XMAD-Bench \[60\] in seven languages and SpoofCeleb, with its
pooled metrics (Table 17). We did not submit to either leaderboard; our
numbers are computed with the boards’ own protocol files.

#### VeriSpoof-Bench

The other questions have no public benchmark, so we built one and
release VeriSpoof-Bench. It has 54 subsets with 177,852 items in six
groups (Figure 5, Figure 9): up to 10,000 items per closed-answer
subset, drawn once and used for every model and checkpoint, so
differences between columns of a table are paired. Source verification
on MLAAD is split by how far the generators of a pair are from training:
seen generators, unseen models of a seen architecture, and unseen
architectures. Degraded copies come in two kinds: aug. copies apply
noise, reverberation and codecs drawn as in training, and cond. copies
carry one logged condition per recording, against which channel
statements are checked. Rationales are evaluated on 500 utterances per
set, and verification rationales on pairs balanced over the SAME,
REVERSAL and DIFF strata. +proc marks copies with post-processing.

#### Metrics

Yes–no questions are scored by EER on the score of Eq. (1); questions
with more answers by the accuracy of the most likely option. Observation
statements are correct when the stated class matches the measured one
(SNR within 10 dB, speech level within 5 dB, bandwidth and clipping
classes). Localization is scored by the IoU of stated and labelled
spans, and the per-token head by F1 of the synthesized class on the
0.16 s grid. Faithfulness is the share of decisions that follow an
edited localization (follow rate) or stay unchanged under an edited
non-evidential statement (stay rate).

#### Baselines

Detection is compared with the open-weight systems of Speech-DF-Arena
and SpoofRadar; proprietary systems, whose training data are
undisclosed, are listed for reference. No open system answers the source
questions, so we train a pair probe on the frozen anti-spoofing encoder
DF-Arena-1B with the Base training data, and prompt six general audio
LLMs zero-shot with closed answer options \[28, 61, 30, 29, 62, 63\].

#### Controls

Controlled comparisons change one factor at a time with all else fixed
and use the same test trials. The supervision study (§6.5) fixes its
decision rule before the results and reports paired, class-stratified
bootstrap intervals.

mean EER (1.79%) and pooled EER (2.14%) of all open-weight systems; the
next best, DF-Arena-1B, reaches 5.66% and 9.52%, and every other
open-weight system, including four listed only on SpoofRadar, has a mean
EER above 5%. Inserted into the public snapshot, Plus ranks third of 28
systems by pooled EER, behind two proprietary systems, and Base fourth
(Figure 1a). Base is trained on the official training splits of eight
corpora; Fake-or-Real, Codecfake, ADD and SONAR have no training data in
Base, and their EERs are its main gap to the systems above it. The score
is the likelihood of the model’s own answer, so answering in sentences
costs neither a detection head nor accuracy.

#### SpoofRadar and unseen languages

On the 16 sets of the SpoofRadar v1.0 protocol (Table 2), both models
have a lower pooled EER (Base 4.23%, Plus 4.04%) and higher accuracy and
macro-F1 than every open-weight system on the published leaderboard, the
best of which reaches 9.96%. XMAD-Bench \[60\], seven languages whose
genuine speech comes from M-AILABS \[71\], MASC \[72\], VoxPopuli \[73\]
and AISHELL-3, is unseen by both models apart from 1,001 AISHELL-3
recordings, and is detected by Base at 0.24% EER and by Plus at 1.33%.

For Plus, however, the pooled EER (4.04%) is far above the mean of its
set EERs (1.65%), because one threshold does not fit every set. At the
pooled threshold, 92.9% of the genuine Russian audiobook recordings of
XMAD-Bench (M-AILABS) are judged spoofed, as are 32.2% and 15.4% of the
Spanish (M-AILABS) and Arabic (MASC) ones, although within each language
the two classes are separated at $`\leq`$1.95% EER. Base misclassifies
at most 3.1% of the genuine recordings of any language. The data added
for Plus thus shift its scores on unseen genuine speech without making
it less separable, a calibration cost that pooled metrics expose and
per-set EER hides. A deployment on a new language or domain should
therefore set its threshold on genuine speech of that domain.

#### The speech-recognition encoder keeps the evidence

Probes on frozen features (Figure 6a) show that Qwen3-ASR gives the best
source verification of seven encoders and detection close to the best
anti-spoofing encoder, while audio tokenizers lose much of both.
Repeated under the current protocol against the strongest anti-spoofing
encoder, DF-Arena-1B (Table 20), Qwen3-ASR with a learned layer mix
verifies sources better (mean EER 24.7 vs. 26.4%, better on 11 of 15

most clearly on ASVspoof 2021 LA, CoSG and In-the-Wild; the best single
layer of Qwen3-ASR is layer 12 of 25 for both tasks. The view that
speech-recognition encoders discard the artifacts of synthesis \[13\]
does not hold for this encoder.

#### Detection depends on how features are used; source identity does not

With the encoder fixed, a ladder of changes in how its features are used
(Table 8a, Figure 6b) lowers clean detection EER from 4.97 to 1.84% and
ASVspoof 5 from 9.56 to 3.10%. The largest single steps are fusing all
25 encoder layers (ASVspoof 5 8.54 $`\to`$ 5.80), encoder DoRA (degraded
detection 8.39 $`\to`$ 6.33) and narrowband codecs in augmentation
(ASVspoof 5 5.95 $`\to`$ 4.27). Clean source verification stays between
16.4 and 19.0% throughout. Local, fragile synthesis artifacts gain from
varied channels and multi-layer detail, whereas source identity is a
global property the features already expose. The last step,
post-processing applied to both classes, is the best for detection but
raises source-verification EER (17.00 $`\to`$ 18.19 clean); we keep it
because it removes the channel shortcuts of §6.4.

detector and the worst comparer, as relating two token sequences needs
recall that such attention lacks. Qwen2.5-1.5B \[81\] has the best clean

names the acoustic model less often (0.77); MiniCPM5-2B is best on

### 6.4  Channel shortcuts

Without post-processing in training, the model reads channel properties
as evidence (Table 9): a 2 kHz high-pass makes 11.6–26.8% of spoofs pass
as genuine, and 0.5 s of digital silence or +10 dB gain with clipping
makes 12.4 and 15.6% of genuine ASVspoof 5 speech look spoofed. Applying
the same post-processing to both classes reduces these to 1.7–8.4%.
Labelling the post-processing in the answer gives no consistent further
gain (1.9–8.9%), so what removes the shortcut is that both classes share
the channel, not that the model is told about it; we keep the unlabelled
form. Gain with clipping remains the largest error (8.4%).

### 6.5  Does knowing the source help detection?

![](arxiv-2611-04634--04f2de3e3422.figures/inline-13.svg)

Figure 7: Source supervision per test set (a) and per ASVspoof 5 attack
(b; A17–A32 are the attack identifiers of the ASVspoof 5 evaluation
set): EER without source questions minus EER with them (95% bootstrap
intervals; $`>0`$: source questions help). Circles: same number of
authenticity questions (B); squares: same number of training steps
(B^(′)); shaded: the $`\pm 0.5`$ equivalence margin.

A spoof labelled with its generator says more than “fake”, but earlier
evidence that source questions help detection did not match authenticity
labels or steps \[8\]. We compare the multi-task model A (Base after
question tuning) with two authenticity-only models: B sees as many
authenticity questions as A, and B^(′) trains as many steps and so sees
five times more. For each test set and ASVspoof 5 attack we compute
$`D=\mathrm{EER}_{B}-\mathrm{EER}_{A}`$ with a paired bootstrap interval
and apply a rule fixed before the results: helps (interval above zero),
hurts (below), equivalent (inside $`\pm 0.5`$) or inconclusive.

#### Per label it helps; at equal compute it mostly breaks even

Against B (Figure 7, circles; Table 10), source supervision helps on 8
of 16 sets and 12 of 16 ASVspoof 5 attacks and hurts on no attack, most
on codec-based generators (CoSG $`+2.8`$, CodecFake+ $`+6.2`$) and
degraded copies. Against B^(′) (squares), most differences become
equivalent or inconclusive: source labels act mainly by making each
example more informative. More binary examples help where the fakes are
whole-utterance and the variation is in the channel (In-the-Wild
$`-0.28`$, LibriSeVoc aug. $`-1.94`$ against B^(′)).

#### For neural-codec fakes it is necessary

With five times more authenticity questions, B^(′) still has 12.6% EER
on CodecFake+ against 2.2% for A ($`D=10.4`$ \[8.7, 11.6\]). Codec
resynthesis resembles lossy compression of real speech, so a binary
label gives no reason to separate them, whereas asking which codec
produced a recording does; this complements the finding that
authenticity-only detectors encode generator identity implicitly \[2\].

#### An open cost on ASVspoof 2021 LA

ASVspoof 2021 LA is the one set where source supervision hurts ($`-1.6`$
against B, $`-1.3`$ against B^(′)). The loss sits in three attacks
unseen in training (A10, A11 and A18, attack identifiers of ASVspoof
2019 LA that ASVspoof 2021 LA reuses) and grows from question tuning to
rationale tuning (3.40 $`\to`$ 5.17%, Table 3); it is not a calibration
shift and not tied to one codec. DFADD and LibriSeVoc are at 0% EER for
every model and cannot separate the conditions.

#### Most statements are right

Speech level is stated correctly for at least 95% of recordings and SNR
for 88–99% (Table 11); clipping is weakest on ASVspoof 5 (0.73), and the
bandwidth class is the least reliable statement (0.64–0.91). Probes show
that finer bandwidth classes are not recoverable from the audio tokens
(38% for 0.5 kHz bins), so coarse classes are the resolution the model
can support. A wrong statement fails its check, a signal that free-text
explanations lack: in Figure 2 the bandwidth statement fails while the
span and the decision hold.

#### Spans: the frames carry the evidence, writing them is the bottleneck

Stated spans reach IoU 0.58–0.61 on HAD and PartialEdit, where a
recording has one or few edited segments, but only 0.25 and 0.31 on
PartialSpoof and LlamaPartialSpoof \[83\], which have many short ones.
The per-token head on the same hidden states reaches F1 0.74 and 0.82
there, so the frames are separable and writing many short spans as text
is the bottleneck. Span IoU also varies with the LLM (0.25–0.64 on HAD),
whereas the per-token head varies little (0.89–0.91; Table 12): the LLM
decides how precisely spans are written, not whether the frames carry
the evidence.

#### Reasoning catches what the one-step answer misses

The decision after reasoning agrees with the one-step answer on
97.6–99.6% of whole-utterance spoofs of known corpora, and where they

one-step answer summarizes the whole recording, whereas writing the
localization first makes the model inspect it frame by frame. Unlike the
chain-of-thought of ThinkOmni, which lowered accuracy \[9\], our
intermediate steps are perception targets with checks.

useful where the training data of the one-step answer do not cover the
fake. In practice, the one-step score suits screening at scale, and a
rationale suits the recordings an analyst has to decide on.

### 6.8  The decision follows the stated evidence

Without counterfactual training, the model told that no segment was
synthesized still answered “spoof” for 42% of unseen MLAAD recordings
(Figure 8b); with it, the decision follows the edit on every set. This
carries over to edits never seen in training (Figure 8c): a paraphrased
opposite localization is followed in 99.5% of cases (73.8% without
counterfactual training), an inserted span in 100%, and the decision
stays unchanged in 100% when channel or observation statements are
contradicted. The decision thus depends on the localization, which is
the statement that should carry it, and not on the statements declared
non-evidential. The model is never trained on a false statement about
the audio, and the statement accuracies of Table 11 are those of the
model trained with counterfactual examples.

### 6.9  Compute: training and running on small GPUs

#### Only 1% of the parameters are trained

VeriSpoof trains 29.7 M of its 2.85 B parameters: the audio tokenizer
(17.85 M, all stages), the encoder DoRA adapters (3.24 M, perception
alignment), the LLM LoRA adapters (8.60 M, question and rationale
tuning) and the per-token head (6 k); a one-class head of 0.53 M is used
in training only. The encoder and the LLM stay frozen in bf16 and hold
no gradients or optimizer state, so the training state of the adapters
(fp32 copy, gradients and Adam moments) takes about 0.5 GB. Memory is
set by the frozen weights, about 5.3 GiB in bf16, and by activations.

#### Training

Table 15 gives the peak memory logged in our runs on one 48 GB L40S. The
released Base was trained without gradient checkpointing, which peaks at
21–45 GiB and takes about 14 GPU-hours for the three stages (logged
throughput 50, 24 and 18 examples per second). With gradient
checkpointing, runs of the same model and stages at the same micro-batch
of 32 peak at 11–17 GiB, so the full recipe, global batch included, fits
a single 24 GB GPU such as an RTX 3090 or 4090, at a lower speed.
Activations grow with the micro-batch, about 0.17–0.34 GiB per example;
we estimate that a micro-batch of 8 with gradient checkpointing,
accumulated to the same global batch, needs 7–9 GiB per stage, within a
12 GB GPU, and a micro-batch of 1–2 approaches the 5.3 GiB of frozen
weights. Offloading the frozen weights to CPU memory, for example with
DeepSpeed ZeRO-Offload, would lower the floor further at the cost of
speed. These settings change the micro-batch only, not the recipe; we
have not trained at them. The code runs in bf16, which needs an Ampere
or newer GPU (RTX 30-series, A10, L4 or later); GPUs without bf16 such
as the T4 or V100 would need fp16 or fp32, which we have not tested.

#### Inference

All 2.85 B parameters are loaded in bf16, about 5.3 GiB. Scoring one 8 s
recording and generating a rationale of a few hundred tokens add well
under 1 GiB of activations and cache, so we estimate 6–7 GiB for single
recordings: an 8 GB GPU such as an RTX 3060 Ti or 4060 is enough, and
about 12 GB covers batched detection (16 recordings per batch by
default). Quantizing the LLM to 8 or 4 bits would lower this further,
which we have not evaluated. Rationales are the expensive output, as
they are generated; the vLLM backend generates them from the same prompt
embeddings, with the audio front end in PyTorch (Table 16). Decisions
are identical to the reference implementation on 64 of 64 recordings and
32 of 32 pairs; the wording of borderline measurements can differ.

## 7  Conclusion

Speech forensics can be cast as scored question answering with grounded
and faithful rationales. VeriSpoof reads every answer as a score, writes
rationales whose statements can be checked against the signal, and is
trained so that its decision follows the evidence it states. With one
set of weights it is the best open-weight detector on Speech-DF-Arena
and SpoofRadar, verifies the source of recordings from unseen
generators, attributes generators and describes recording channels.
Controlled studies show where to invest: the representation and how it
is trained decide what is detected, the LLM decides how well recordings
are compared and how evidence is worded, and supervision that
distinguishes how audio was produced adds what more binary labels
cannot, at least for neural-codec fakes. Its rationales catch partial
fakes that one score misses and determine the decision even under edits
never seen in training. We release both models, the inference code and a
demo, and the evaluation protocol for the questions beyond detection.
