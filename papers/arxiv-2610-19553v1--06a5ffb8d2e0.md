# Speaking Longer Than Learned: Training-Free Long-Form Generation for Full-Stream Text-to-Speech

Jeseung Lim    Jiseong Woo    Heeseung Kim ^(†)^(†)thanks: \$†\$
Corresponding Author.

###### Abstract

Recent full-stream text-to-speech (TTS) models enable low-latency
personalized synthesis as input text arrives. However, sustaining
generation beyond the lengths seen during training without additional
training remains largely unexplored. We systematically analyze
VoXtream2, which extends generation by periodically discarding past
context while retaining the acoustic prompt. We find that this strategy
does not prevent degradation: pronunciation accuracy deteriorates near
the end of the training range due to the range of positions in which the
model operates rather than the loss of past context, while speaker
similarity gradually declines throughout generation. Based on these
findings, we propose two training-free modifications that reduce the
attention window and strengthen attention to the acoustic prompt.
Experiments show that these modifications preserve pronunciation
accuracy and speaker similarity throughout long-form generation while
retaining the naturalness of the original model. Ablations further show
complementary effects of the two modifications.¹¹ 1 Project page:
[https://voxtend.github.io](https://voxtend.github.io)

## 1 Introduction

Recent text-to-speech (TTS) systems support zero-shot voice cloning and
expressive speech generation \[4\], while growing demand for spoken
conversational systems has increased interest in streaming TTS. Recent
approaches enable synthesis before the complete input text is available
through interleaved speech-text modeling \[1\], direct autoregressive
generation from incoming text \[13\], or streaming acoustic
decoding \[4\]. These advances have made low-latency TTS increasingly
practical for real-time conversational systems.

Beyond short utterances, many applications require long-form synthesis
over multiple sentences or paragraphs \[10\]. Concatenating
independently synthesized segments can introduce speaker inconsistencies
and boundary artifacts \[5\], while long-form paired speech data remains
scarce. Consequently, synthesis beyond the sequence lengths observed
during training becomes a length extrapolation problem. Similar
challenges have been studied in large language models (LLMs) through
additional long-context training or training-free context
management \[15\]. StreamingLLM \[15\] retains a few initial attention
sink tokens together with a bounded window of recent tokens to enable
inference beyond the training context.

Extending generation beyond the sequence lengths seen during training
has also been explored in long-form TTS. Approaches based on additional
training improve length extrapolation through positional modeling or
alignment, while others explicitly train on long-duration speech for
long-form generation \[10\]. Training-free approaches instead avoid
additional long-form training; MagpieTTS-LF \[5\] extends TTS models
through stateful chunk generation and attention priors. More recently,
long-form synthesis with streaming text input has also been explored,
although existing approaches require additional training to operate with
a bounded streaming context \[9\]. To the best of our knowledge,
training-free length extrapolation for a pretrained full-stream TTS
model under continuous long-form generation has not been explored.

In this work, we tackle training-free length extrapolation in continuous
long-form generation using VoXtream2 \[13\], whose official
implementation extends generation beyond the training length by
periodically resetting the key-value cache while retaining the acoustic
prompt and recent frames, following the attention sink concept \[15\].
However, this mechanism alone cannot sustain long-form generation:
pronunciation accuracy degrades near the end of the training range,
while speaker similarity declines gradually throughout the utterance. We
find that the former stems from the positions at which the model
operates rather than from the cache reset itself, while the latter
persists even when these positions are avoided. Based on these findings,
we shrink the attention window to keep the model within positions
encountered during training and bias attention toward the acoustic
prompt, the only intact explicit speaker reference in the context.

We first show that the loss of speaker similarity is not unique to
VoXtream2, but also occurs in open-source TTS models trained on
long-form speech. Applied to VoXtream2, our methods preserve both
pronunciation accuracy and speaker similarity throughout long-form
generation with streaming text input. Human evaluation confirms
comparable naturalness, while ablations on attention window size and
bias design show complementary effects on long-form stability.

## 2 Method

### 2.1 VoXtream2

VoXtream2 \[13\] is a full-stream TTS model conditioned on reference
speech, which is encoded into Mimi codec tokens \[2\] and serves as an
acoustic prompt. It consists of a Phoneme Transformer (PT), a Temporal
Transformer (TT), and a Depth Transformer (DT). PT incrementally encodes
phonemes, TT autoregressively predicts semantic and duration tokens from
these embeddings and previous audio tokens, and DT generates the
remaining codebooks. Each duration token combines a shift state
$`s\in\{0,1,2\}`$, indicating the base phoneme advancement, with a
binary phoneme-count state $`u\in\{0,1\}`$, where $`u=0`$ and $`u=1`$
correspond to assigning one and two phonemes to the current frame. The
effective phoneme advancement per frame is $`s+u\in\{0,1,2,3\}`$.
VoXtream2 is trained on random $`50`$s crops ($`625`$ frames at
$`12.5`$Hz).

During long-form generation, the key-value (KV) cache keeps growing and
must be bounded. StreamingLLM \[15\] showed that a bounded cache can
remain stable by retaining the first few tokens, known as attention
sinks, together with the most recent ones. The official VoXtream2
implementation follows a similar idea in its SinkAttention module. TT
indexes cached frames by local position and holds at most $`W`$ frames,
where audio_window_size sets $`W=625`$ by default. Once the local
position reaches $`W`$, a cache reset retains the $`P`$ acoustic prompt
frames and the $`\tau=\lfloor W/2\rfloor`$ recent generated frames while
discarding the rest. The frames are re-indexed from $`0`$ and their KV
states recomputed, so generation resumes at local position $`P+\tau`$.
The local position then climbs back to $`W`$, triggering the next reset
after $`W-P-\tau`$ frames and cycling between $`P+\tau`$ and $`W`$.

### 2.2 Analysis of Long-Form Generation Degradation

To examine long-form zero-shot TTS beyond the training length, we use
LibriSpeech-Long \[10\], whose recordings last up to four minutes. We
use the $`281`$ recordings of its dev-clean split, totaling $`15.89`$
hours. For each recording, the opening speech segment ending at a
sentence boundary serves as the acoustic prompt, with an average
duration of $`7.88`$s, and the remaining transcript serves as the target
text.

Figure 1: Segment-wise WER and SPK-SIM of VoXtream2, together with the
corresponding ground truth speech.

We measure pronunciation accuracy using word error rate (WER) over
$`10`$s windows, pooling reference words and errors across utterances
within each window. Generated speech is transcribed with
whisper-large-v3 \[11\], and its words are time-stamped with
Qwen3-ForcedAligner-0.6B \[12\]. After edit-distance alignment to the
target text, each error is assigned to the window containing its word,
with deletions placed between neighboring words. SPK-SIM is the cosine
similarity between speaker embeddings of generated speech and the
acoustic prompt, extracted with ECAPA-TDNN \[3\] over the same windows.
As shown in Figure 1, the cache reset does not prevent two distinct
degradation patterns. WER starts at $`2.74\%`$ in the first $`10`$s
window and rises more sharply near the training length, reaching
$`11.23\%`$ at $`40`$–$`50`$s and remaining high, while SPK-SIM
decreases steadily from $`0.760`$ to $`0.695`$ over the same interval
and continues to decline.

#### 2.2.1 Position, Not Cache Reset

Training crops are $`625`$ frames long, so TT never undergoes a cache
reset during training and observes local positions above roughly $`600`$
only near the end of a crop, when little of the transcript remains. With
the default $`W=625`$, however, the local position repeatedly enters
this region immediately before each cache reset during long-form
generation. The sharp WER increase in Figure 1 may therefore arise
either from position-dependent behavior near the end of the training
range or from the cache reset itself. To distinguish these effects, we
vary $`W`$ at inference, moving the reset position while leaving the end
of the training range at position $`625`$ unchanged.

Figure 2: Expected number of phonemes advanced per frame versus position
for $`W=400`$, $`625`$, and $`900`$. Shaded bands show standard errors
over $`8`$ randomly selected utterances.

Figure 2 shows that the phoneme advancement rate remains near $`0.8`$
below position $`600`$ but rises sharply toward position $`625`$,
reaching $`1.9\times`$ and $`2.6\times`$ this rate for $`W=625`$ and
$`900`$, respectively. For $`W=900`$, the increase still occurs near
position $`625`$, although the reset is delayed by $`275`$ frames,
showing that degradation follows local position rather than cache reset.
With $`W=900`$, generated audio is only $`70\%`$ as long as ground
truth, while deletion errors increase from $`0.7\%`$ at $`W=625`$ to
$`16.6\%`$, indicating that the model advances too quickly and skips
target words. WER rises from $`7.3\%`$ to $`49.8\%`$ across position
$`625`$, but changes only from $`47.8\%`$ to $`44.2\%`$ around the later
cache reset. This motivates our first intervention: restricting $`W`$
avoids problematic end-of-training positions while retaining recent
context.

#### 2.2.2 Attending to the Prompt

Unlike the sharp increase in pronunciation errors above, SPK-SIM
declines steadily throughout generation (Figure 1). Reducing $`W`$ does
not remove this trend but slows it, from $`-0.082`$ per $`100`$s with
$`W=625`$ to $`-0.054`$ with $`W=400`$. Nor does the decline track reset
frequency: with $`W=400`$, resets are more frequent than with $`W=625`$,
yet SPK-SIM degrades more slowly. We therefore examine whether the
acoustic prompt remains important throughout generation.

To test this hypothesis, after 30s, we prevent the model from attending
to the acoustic prompt keys while retaining the generated-history cache.
With $`W=400`$, the cache contains at least the most recent
$`\tau=\lfloor W/2\rfloor=200`$ generated frames, corresponding to
$`16`$s of speech. Despite this context, SPK-SIM drops from $`0.73`$ to
$`0.23`$ in the next $`10`$s window and remains below $`0.25`$
thereafter (Figure 3). This indicates that the acoustic prompt remains
essential throughout generation, but its $`P`$ frames remain fixed while
generated frames continue to accumulate in the cache.

We therefore strengthen attention to the acoustic prompt by adding a
constant bias $`b`$ to the pre-softmax attention logits of its
keys \[7\]. For an attention logit $`L_{ij}`$ from query $`i`$ to key
$`j`$, we use $`L^{\prime}_{ij}=L_{ij}+b`$ for prompt keys and leave it
unchanged otherwise. The bias is applied to the acoustic prompt keys
across all attention heads and layers of TT, requiring neither
retraining nor architectural changes. We also compare with
SpotLight \[14\], which adaptively adds
$`\log(\psi_{\mathrm{target}}/\psi_{\mathrm{current}})`$ to prompt-key
logits when the prompt attention mass $`\psi_{\mathrm{current}}`$ falls
below the target $`\psi_{\mathrm{target}}`$. Section 3.2.2 compares
constant and adaptive prompt biases.

attended and when it is masked after $`30`$s. Error bars show standard
errors over $`8`$ randomly selected utterances.

## 3 Experiments

### 3.1 Experimental Setups

All VoXtream2-based \[13\] experiments use the official implementation
and pretrained checkpoint. Our method requires no additional training.
We follow the same LibriSpeech-Long \[10\] evaluation protocol and
objective metrics as in Section 2.2. In addition to VoXtream2 and ours,
we compare against Qwen3-TTS-12Hz-1.7B-Base \[8\] and
MOSS-TTS-v1.5 \[6\], recent TTS models capable of long-form synthesis.
Both baselines use long-form training, whereas ours directly extends
pretrained VoXtream2 without additional training.

All models receive the same acoustic prompt and target text; when
required, the corresponding reference text is also provided. We further
evaluate Mimi codec reconstruction \[2\] to quantify degradation
introduced by encoding and decoding. All audio is resampled to 16kHz for
evaluation. In addition to WER and SPK-SIM, we evaluate naturalness and
overall speech quality using mean opinion score (MOS). For MOS
evaluation, 158 listeners rate overall naturalness and audio quality on
a five-point scale, yielding 325 ratings per model.

### 3.2 Results

#### 3.2.1 Model Comparison

Table 1 compares long-form synthesis performance. Mimi reconstruction
largely preserves WER (1.86 to 1.95) but noticeably reduces SPK-SIM from
0.832 to 0.754. Since VoXtream2 generates speech through the Mimi codec,
its reconstruction MOS of 4.10 also provides a practical upper bound on
perceptual quality under this codec. Our method substantially improves
VoXtream2, reducing overall WER from 8.63% to 3.58% and increasing
SPK-SIM from 0.659 to 0.759. The improvement is particularly evident
over long generation: while VoXtream2 progressively degrades with
generation length, our method maintains WER below 4.0% and nearly stable
SPK-SIM across 30s segments. Nevertheless, even within its stable
operating range, VoXtream2 already exhibits lower pronunciation accuracy
(3.60%), and our training-free extension therefore does not reach the
WER of Qwen3-TTS or MOSS-TTS. Our method achieves the same MOS of 4.03
as VoXtream2, close to the Mimi reconstruction upper bound of 4.10,
indicating that the improved long-form robustness does not compromise
perceptual quality.

Interestingly, speaker similarity degradation with generation length is
not unique to VoXtream2. Qwen3-TTS and MOSS-TTS, despite being trained
with long-form data, also exhibit decreasing SPK-SIM toward later
segments. Qwen3-TTS, for example, drops from 0.780 to 0.560, whereas our
method remains substantially more stable, from 0.770 to 0.754. This
suggests that maintaining speaker identity over extended generation
remains a broader challenge even for models explicitly trained for
long-form synthesis.

#### 3.2.2 Ablation Studies

Table 2 analyzes context window restriction and acoustic prompt
attention bias. Reducing the Temporal Transformer window from $`W=625`$
improves WER, with $`W=375`$ achieving the best value of 4.82, whereas
further reducing it to $`W=250`$ degrades performance. Despite more
frequent cache resets, $`W=375`$ remains real-time on an NVIDIA RTX
3090, with real-time factor (RTF) increasing only from 0.415 at
$`W=625`$ to 0.433. With $`W=375`$, increasing the constant bias
improves speaker similarity; $`b=2.0`$ achieves the highest SPK-SIM of
0.766, while $`b=1.5`$ yields the lowest WER of 3.58 with a comparable
SPK-SIM of 0.759. SpotLight-based adaptive steering \[14\] improves WER
over window restriction alone but remains less effective than constant
bias for speaker similarity. We therefore use $`W=375`$ and a constant
bias of $`b=1.5`$ for our experiments.

## 4 Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a training-free approach for long-form
generation with full-stream TTS, based on a systematic analysis of
VoXtream2. By shrinking the attention window to keep the model within
the positions encountered during training and applying a bias to the
attention logits of the acoustic prompt, we preserved both pronunciation
accuracy and speaker similarity throughout long-form generation while
retaining the naturalness of the original model.
