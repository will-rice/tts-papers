# Beyond the Benchmark: Conditioning, Domains and Failure Modes of Qwen3-TTS

Séverin Baroudi^(1,2)  Yanis Labrak³ Petr Motlicek^(3,4)  Hervé Bredin⁵
 Ricard Marxer^(1,6)

###### Abstract

Qwen3-TTS reports state-of-the-art zero-shot voice cloning, but its
technical report leaves open how the model should be conditioned, how it
behaves outside read speech, and how it fails. We characterise its two
cloning interfaces, in-context learning (ICL) and X-Vector, against
Chatterbox, XTTS-v2 and F5-TTS on read, meeting, emotional and
air-traffic-control (ATC) speech, using eighteen objective measures, an
LLM judge, a spoofing countermeasure and a listening test. Neither
interface dominates: ICL clones timbre more closely from correctly
transcribed references, X-Vector clones timing, and ICL degrades sharply
when a short reference transcript is wrong. Domain explains four times
more variance than system choice. Qwen3-TTS leads on intelligibility and
pitch register but trails on prosody, and the objective measures rank
systems differently from listeners. An off-the-shelf countermeasure
flags its clones no more often than genuine recordings, and on ATC radio
it fails for every system. Code and audio are released.

###### Index Terms: 

zero-shot voice cloning, qwen3-tts, text-to-speech evaluation,
anti-spoofing, perceptual evaluation

## 1 Introduction

Since neural codec language models made speaker-conditioned synthesis
practical \[1\], zero-shot voice cloning has become a crowded field of
open systems such as XTTS-v2 \[2\], F5-TTS \[3\], Chatterbox¹¹ 1
[github.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox](https://github.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox)
and, most recently, Qwen3-TTS \[4\], several reporting human parity on
read-speech benchmarks \[5, 6\]. Qwen3-TTS reports state-of-the-art
zero-shot cloning and, unusually, exposes two distinct cloning
interfaces. Its technical report, like most system papers, establishes
that the model is strong, but it does not tell a practitioner which
interface to use, how the model behaves away from clean read speech, or
how it fails.

Existing evaluations report ASR word error rate \[7\], speaker-embedding
similarity \[8, 9\] and predicted MOS \[10\], mostly on clean read
speech. These saturate as systems approach human quality, MOS tests are
sensitive to design \[11\], and none says which acoustic dimension
betrays a clone or how quality changes across domains. TTSDS2 \[12\]
broadens the objective battery, the human fooling rate \[13\] measures
deception directly, audio LLMs are emerging as judges \[14\] with only
partial human agreement \[15\], and deepfake detection \[16, 17\] asks a
different, machine-centred question. None covers Qwen3-TTS or its two
interfaces, and no single family of evidence suffices alone, so we
combine all four over four domains differing in recording conditions,
speaking style and vocabulary.

Listeners already miss a large share of speech deepfakes \[18\] and
often, LLM based judge TTS outputs to be real speakers in about $`80\%`$
of the time \[19, 20\], which fuels voice-phishing fraud that current
detectors struggle to stop \[21\] and has led the EU AI Act to mandate
disclosure of synthetic audio \[22\].

We therefore ask, how convincing the five systems are to listeners
(Section 3.1), whether an off-the-shelf spoofing detector catches what
listeners miss (Section 3.2), whether Qwen3-TTS should be conditioned on
reference audio and transcript or on a speaker embedding alone
(Section 3.3), which domains and inputs make it break down
(Section 3.4), which acoustic dimensions are strong or lacking
(Section 3.5), and whether automatic measures can stand in for human
judgment (Section 3.6). All code and the generated audio are released
publicly.²² 2
[beyond-the-benchmark.github.io](https://beyond-the-benchmark.github.io)

## 2 Experimental Setup

### 2.1 Domains, systems and cloning conditions

Four English corpora test sets, covering distinct domains: LibriSpeech
\[23\] (LS), clean (read speech); AMI \[24\] (far-field spontaneous
meetings); IEMOCAP \[25\] (acted emotional dialogue) and ATCO2 \[26\]
(noisy, jargon-dense air-traffic-control radio). Each is subsampled to
one hour in order to be manageable. All systems receive the same
reference clips and use public checkpoints without fine-tuning:
Qwen3-TTS (Qwen3-TTS-12Hz-1.7B-Base, 1.7 B parameters), Chatterbox
(500 M), XTTS-v2 ($`{\sim}518`$ M) and F5-TTS v1 Base (336 M). Qwen3-TTS
runs in bfloat16 with sampling (temperature $`0.9`$, top-$`k`$ $`50`$,
top-$`p`$ $`1.0`$, repetition penalty $`1.05`$); the baselines keep
their libraries defaults hyper-parameters.

Qwen3-TTS offers two cloning interfaces: ICL, the default, receives the
reference audio with its transcript and continues in that voice, while
X-Vector conditions only on a speaker embedding \[8\] and needs no
transcript. We also vary the reference–target pairing. In the aligned
condition the model receives the audio and text of utterance $`i`$ and
renders $`i`$; in the shifted condition, which matches deployment, the
voice comes from utterance $`i{+}1`$ and the text from $`i`$, so prosody
cannot be copied. All results below use the shifted condition unless
stated otherwise.

### 2.2 Perceptual test and LLM judge

We started by running a two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) test \[13\]
on a web platform. Each round presents the real recording and its
synthetic counterpart, same text, as clips A and B in random order,
listeners replay both freely and pick the one judged real. The fooling
rate is the fraction of rounds in which the synthetic clip is chosen.
Listeners are three entry-level annotators working at the same place, so
that familiarity with synthesis artefacts does not bias their judgments.
For each system, 100 sampled pairs of AMI are split over non-overlapping
lists and used to compute the human fooling rate (Table 1). A second
session, in which each listener judges 135 new pairs of AMI (new full
1 h subset) spread over the whole combinations of 5 models and 2
conditions (shifted and aligned), and supply the data points used as the
development set in Section 2.3, for measure selection and correlation
study. The full human annotation process took an estimated total of
12 hours to the annotators.

In order to extends these 2AFC study on all the others datasets, and at
a more reproductible and reasonable cost, we query a multimodal LLM
judge, capable of handle multiple audios in the same input, in our case,
Gemini 3.5 Flash \[27\]. It answers exactly the same 100 pairs per
system on AMI, and proceed to the same protocol on the 100 pairs from
the other three corpora. It is queried at temperature $`0`$ with the
answer constrained to the enum $`\{\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B}\}`$, and told
only that one clip is real and one synthetic and to reply with the
letter of the synthetic one. Because such judgments are sensitive to
presentation order, every pair is judged twice, once in each ordering.
The reported rate always uses the first ordering, with the real clip as
A, and every pair stays in the denominator. The second ordering is a
robustness diagnostic, and pairs whose verdict flips are counted as
order-dependent and reported in Section 3.6.

Afterwards, listeners described in free form the cues behind their
decisions, and their answers fall into four categories. All three
mentioned background and channel sounds, such as background speakers or
the radio beeps bracketing ATC transmissions, which occur only in real
recordings; two noted breathing and microphone noise, absent from
clones; all three reported differences in speaking rate and phrasing,
since real speakers vary their rate within an utterance and follow
punctuation whereas clones usually keep a steady rate; and two found
clones flatter in emotional intensity on IEMOCAP.

### 2.3 Objective measures

We compute 18 automatic measures comparing each synthetic clip with its
real counterpart, in four dimensions. Speaker: a ResNet34 embedding
cosine \[9\], vocal-burst rate and class match from a VoiceCLAP-based
detector of 82 non-verbal vocalisations \[28\], an emotion2vec cosine
\[29\] and SpeechBERTScore \[30\] on WavLM-Large layer-14 features.
Prosody: relative duration error, speech-ratio difference from an
energy-based VAD, gain-variation match ($`\sigma/\mu`$ of frame RMS) and
mean frame-RMS difference. $`F_{0}`$: per-utterance $`F_{0}`$ using
vocoder estimator from \[30\], then mean and standard-deviation
differences are applied and a DTW-aligned log-$`F_{0}`$ RMSE.
Intelligibility: WER difference between Whisper Large V3 \[7\]
transcripts of the synthetic and real clips, DNSMOS P.835 \[10\] BAK and
OVRL differences, and STOI, PESQ and reference-free SI-SDR by
TorchAudio-SQUIM \[31\].

We combine these automatic measures into four dimension scores and
metric filtering is based on the correlation between the metrics and
human annotations on the development set kept separate. We retain from
that 13 measures, whose for which agreement with human fooling validate
a Spearman correlation $`\rho\geq 0.45`$ on $`n=4`$. Other measures are
dropped as no strong signal is carried into the human perception. The
retained set is then applied to data it was not selected on, including
the 3 other systems and 3 other corpora with both conditions (shifted
and aligned).

Each retained measure is first mapped to a per-utterance agreement
$`A\in[0,1]`$, $`1`$ meaning that the synthetic clip matches its real
counterpart within a tolerance cap set per measure, so that
dimensionless and unbounded quantities become comparable. Let
$`\mathcal{C}=\{\mathrm{Spk},\mathrm{Pro},F_{0},\mathrm{Int}\}`$ and let
$`A_{c,i}`$ be the agreement of the $`i`$-th of the $`n_{c}`$ measures
retained in dimension $`c`$. Each measure is mapped to a reliability,
averaged within its dimension, and the same map is applied again to that
dimension mean, giving the four dimension scores $`S_{c}`$ and their
unweighted mean $`S`$ on a $`0`$–$`100`$ scale:

```math
\displaystyle r_{c,i} \displaystyle=1-(1-A_{c,i})^{2}, \displaystyle\bar{r}_{c} \displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{n_{c}}\textstyle\sum_{i}r_{c,i}, \tag{1}
```

```math
\displaystyle S_{c} \displaystyle=1-(1-\bar{r}_{c})^{2}, \displaystyle S \displaystyle=\tfrac{100}{|\mathcal{C}|}\textstyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}S_{c}.
```

The double mapping keeps the scale from saturating as systems approach
human quality, where raw agreements crowd towards $`1`$. Weighting the
four dimensions equally is a deliberate choice rather than a calibrated
one, and Section 3.6 tests it against human judgments rather than
assuming it.

### 2.4 Hallucination estimation

Hallucinated content, meaning repeated, skipped or invented words, is
common in language-model TTS \[32\]. We flag it from timing alone, which
keeps the test applicable at dataset scale and at controlled
computational cost. Writing $`d`$ for the duration of the synthetic
clip, $`n_{w}`$ for the number of words in its target text and
$`d_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ for the duration of the real counterpart, a clip is
flagged when

```math
h=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\;\frac{d}{n_{w}}>\tau_{w}\;\;\lor\;\;\frac{d}{d_{\mathrm{ref}}}>1+\tau_{d}\;\right],\qquad\tau_{w}=1\,\mathrm{s},\;\tau_{d}=0.5. \tag{2}
```

The first term catches clips that stall or loop on few words, the second
those that run past their reference, the two duration signatures of
repeated or invented content. Because the rule reads duration rather
than lexical content, two authors audited $`100`$ flagged and $`100`$
unflagged utterances across systems and corpora. It misses few
hallucinations, $`3`$ of $`57`$ (recall $`94.7\%`$, $`F_{1}`$
$`68.8\%`$), and unflagged clips are rarely hallucinated ($`3`$ of
$`100`$). Flagged rates therefore overestimate the true hallucination
rate, but the same rule applies to every system and corpus, so
comparisons between them remain valid.

### 2.5 Spoofing countermeasure

A clone that convinces a listener may still be trivially detectable by
machine, so we submit the same audios to AASIST \[17\], an end-to-end
raw-waveform anti-spoofing model. We take the released checkpoint³³ 3
[https://github.com/clovaai/aasist](https://github.com/clovaai/aasist)
($`0.30`$ M parameters) trained on the logical-access partition of
ASVspoof 2019 \[33\] and apply it exactly as distributed, with no
retraining or threshold recalibration, so that it stands for the
off-the-shelf countermeasure a practitioner would reach for rather than
the best achievable detector. Audio is converted to $`16`$ kHz mono and
truncated or zero-padded to $`64{,}600`$ samples, the original
protocol’s fixed length. A clip is labelled spoof whenever the spoof
logit exceeds the bona-fide logit. Both clips of a pair are preprocessed
identically. Each cell of Table 2 covers 100 shifted pairs. We report
the detection rate (Det.) which consist of the percentage of clones
identified properly as spoof, and the false-alarm rate (FA) which is the
percentage of their genuine counterparts miss-identified as spoof.

## 3 Results

### 3.1 Perceived realism and alternatives

Qwen3-TTS is the hardest of the five systems to tell from real speech,
but it remains detectable. On AMI, listeners pick its clones as real in
$`35.6\%`$ (X-Vector) and $`27.0\%`$ (ICL) of rounds, against at most
$`9.0\%`$ for the baselines (Table 1). This is consistent with the
difficulty listeners have with other modern voice cloning systems \[18,
19\]. Their cues (Section 2.2) show what gives clones away: they
reproduce the voice but not the sounds around it, nor the rate variation
and emotional intensity of natural speech.

On LibriSpeech, XTTS-v2 fools the judge most often ($`26\%`$) and
Chatterbox reaches the highest objective score of any system
($`S=51.9`$, Table 3). Outside read speech that advantage disappears.
XTTS-v2 collapses on ATCO2 and IEMOCAP ($`S=11.6`$ and $`12.9`$) and
hallucinates on $`18.5\%`$ of its samples, almost all on those two
short-utterance corpora, and F5-TTS is the weakest system overall
($`S=21.1`$). Chatterbox is the most robust alternative, close to
Qwen3-TTS on average ($`31.9`$ vs. $`33.4`$–$`34.3`$) and best on
prosody, yet it fools our listeners only $`9\%`$ of the time.

### 3.2 Machine detection

A standard detector does not fill the gap left by listeners, and on
Qwen3-TTS it barely works at all (Table 2). Since the false-alarm rate
varies by corpus, a system is detectable only where its detection rate
clearly exceeds it. By that criterion Qwen3-TTS is detectable in one of
the eight cells measured, ICL on LibriSpeech ($`39.0\%`$ against
$`19.0\%`$); in the other seven the detector flags the real recordings
at least as often as the clones. X-Vector on AMI is the extreme case,
caught on one clip in a hundred while a sixth of the genuine meeting
recordings are rejected.

Detectability is a property of the generator rather than of perceived
quality, and the two orderings do not match: XTTS-v2 is the only system
the detector separates convincingly ($`86`$–$`91\%`$ in all four
corpora) yet is among the least convincing for our listeners ($`6\%`$ on
AMI), while F5-TTS, the least convincing of all ($`1\%`$), is caught on
$`36.0\%`$ of AMI clips against $`14.0\%`$ false alarms. A clone a
detector recognises is therefore not a clone a listener recognises.

On ATC radio the failure is complete and system-independent, the
detector flags $`99\%`$ of the _genuine_ ATCO2 recordings as spoof, more
often than the clones of any system ($`75`$–$`95\%`$), including the
XTTS-v2 clones it identifies almost perfectly everywhere else. Its
decision is driven by the narrow-band, noisy, clipped VHF channel,
nothing like the studio-quality bona-fide speech of ASVspoof 2019
\[33\], and not by any property of the clone; this is the generalisation
gap reported for deployed anti-spoofing \[21\] in an extreme form, and
no operating point recovers a discrimination the scores do not contain.
Off-the-shelf anti-spoofing thus offers no protection for ATC, a
safety-critical domain in which a spoofed transmission is exactly the
attack that matters.

### 3.3 In-context learning or X-Vector?

Humans on AMI are fooled more by X-Vector ($`35.6\%`$ vs. $`27.0\%`$), a
gap too small for our panel to settle, whereas the LLM judge prefers ICL
on all four corpora (Table 1). Their objective profiles explain why. ICL
clones timbre better from clean references ($`S_{\mathrm{Spk}}`$
$`80.2`$ vs. $`61.5`$ on LibriSpeech, $`52.2`$ vs. $`43.6`$ on AMI),
while X-Vector produces closer prosody on every corpus
($`S_{\mathrm{Pro}}`$ higher by $`2.6`$ to $`11.3`$ points) and matches
or beats ICL on speaker similarity for noisy ATCO2 and short IEMOCAP
references. Overall scores are nearly tied ($`33.4`$ vs. $`34.3`$).
X-Vector is also safer, since ICL hallucinates $`1.6`$ times as often
($`4.1\%`$ vs. $`2.5\%`$) because it must reconcile the reference
transcript with the audio (Section 3.4).

### 3.4 Domains and failure modes

Figure 1: Hallucination rate of Qwen3-TTS ICL against reference
transcript length (shifted condition).

In a two-way decomposition of the scores of Table 3, corpus explains
$`63\%`$ of the variance and system only $`15\%`$. Showing that domains
matter more than the choice of system. LibriSpeech is easiest (mean
$`S=43.4`$) and ATCO2 hardest ($`18.5`$), where intelligibility
collapses for every system ($`S_{\mathrm{Int}}`$ $`14.7`$ vs. $`55.2`$)
and the judge is fooled in only $`3.8\%`$ of rounds. Emotional speech
like IEMOCAP has the lowest $`F_{0}`$ and prosody scores ($`10.5`$,
$`19.2`$), since expressive pitch and timing are hard to transfer from a
short reference. Listeners single out the same two domains.

We corrupt one word in the middle of it while keeping the audio the
same. Partial corruption (PC) slightly alters that word, word corruption
(WC) replaces it with a static distractor. The clearest failure mode of
Qwen3-TTS is an unreliable reference transcript under ICL. On the
short-utterance corpora the hallucination rate rises by an order of
magnitude (IEMOCAP $`0.8\%\!\to\!21.2\%`$ under WC, ATCO2
$`1.6\%\!\to\!15.2\%`$, means of $`11.2`$ and $`12.2`$ words per
utterance), while the long AMI and LS references ($`84.2`$ and $`33.8`$
words) stay below $`1.5\%`$; pooled over corpora the rate goes from
$`1.1\%`$ to $`9.0\%`$ under PC and $`14.4\%`$ under WC. Transcript
length, not corpus, is the driver: the WC rate falls from $`65\%`$ on
three-word transcripts to below $`20\%`$ once the transcript exceeds
eight words, and PC follows the same trend from $`33\%`$ (Fig. 1). With
few words to align, a single wrong word dominates the conditioning,
while diluted in longer context.

Transcribing the corrupted syntheses with Whisper Large V3 reveals a
mechanism more specific than a generic loss of alignment. The distractor
is audibly reproduced in $`7.0\%`$ of utterances, again concentrated on
the short-utterance corpora ($`12.2\%`$ on IEMOCAP, $`6.1\%`$ on ATCO2,
against $`1.8\%`$ and $`0.7\%`$ elsewhere), and where it leaks it almost
never appears where it was injected (mid-sentence) but rather, it
surfaces in the first third of the words of the synthesis in $`96.6\%`$
of cases and as the literal first word in $`57\%`$, never in the final
third. The model thus emits the unexpected word, due to a attention
leak, while still anchoring on the reference, consistent with a
continuation-style hallucinations before committing to the target.

### 3.5 Acoustic strengths and weaknesses

Table 3 shows where Qwen3-TTS stands out. One of its two modes reaches
the highest $`S_{\mathrm{Int}}`$ on three of the four corpora and the
highest $`S_{F_{0}}`$ on all four, and it reproduces each gender’s pitch
register, the per-utterance mean $`F_{0}`$ of ICL clones tracking the
real distribution with medians only a few Hz low. Its average speaker
similarity under ICL is the best of the five, though not in every
domain, since F5-TTS leads on AMI and Chatterbox on LibriSpeech and
IEMOCAP. Prosody is its main weakness, with Chatterbox and XTTS-v2
closer to the real timing on three corpora. In absolute terms $`F_{0}`$
is the weakest dimension for every system but F5-TTS ($`12.0`$ to
$`18.4`$), since pitch registers match at the population level while
each clone drifts from the specific speaker it should reproduce.

### 3.6 Can automatic measures replace listeners?

Over the development set, background and channel sounds are well
captured by DNSMOS background differences ($`\rho=0.77`$), breathing
only partly by the vocal-burst rate ($`0.52`$) and not by which bursts
occur ($`0.32`$), and speaking rate partly by duration error and VAD
ratio ($`0.71`$, $`0.54`$), which capture global timing but not rate
changes within an utterance or where pauses fall. Emotional intensity is
missed entirely: emotion2vec ($`0.11`$), gain variation ($`0.17`$) and
mean energy ($`0.37`$) barely track fooling, and SpeechBERTScore carries
no signal ($`0.01`$). The strongest correlate, SQUIM SI-SDR ($`0.81`$),
is reference-free, so it partly tracks how clean a corpus is rather than
how a clone differs from its reference.

Averaged over the four corpora, $`S`$ ranks the systems exactly as our
listeners do on AMI: Qwen3-TTS first ($`34.3`$, $`33.4`$), then
Chatterbox ($`31.9`$), XTTS-v2 ($`28.2`$) and F5-TTS ($`21.1`$). The
agreement does not survive a matched comparison. On AMI alone $`S`$ puts
XTTS-v2 first ($`40.9`$), yet it fools humans in only $`6\%`$ of rounds,
and over the five systems $`S`$ correlates weakly with fooling
($`\rho=0.20`$) while speaker similarity correlates negatively
($`-0.70`$), F5-TTS having the most similar voice
($`S_{\mathrm{Spk}}=64.7`$) and the least convincing clone; only
intelligibility and quality follows listeners ($`0.90`$). Averaging
reproduces the human ordering by mixing in domain difficulty, not by
tracking what listeners hear.

The LLM judge fails differently, on AMI it agrees with the human on at
least $`78\%`$ of Chatterbox, XTTS-v2 and F5-TTS clones, but on under
$`73\%`$ of Qwen3-TTS clones and on at most $`16.7\%`$ of the utterances
that fooled the human. It is also unstable, even with deterministic
decoding, the two presentation orders (Section 2.2) disagree on up to
$`43\%`$ of pairs (XTTS-v2, LibriSpeech).

## 4 Discussion and Conclusion

With Qwen3-TTS, the available reference should decide the interface,
since neither dominates. ICL clones timbre more closely and X-Vector
timing, so ICL suits clean references whose transcript is verified and
over ten words, X-Vector short, noisy or untranscribed ones and wherever
timing matters most. The domain decides the rest, explaining four times
more variance than the system, so a short in-domain validation is worth
more than switching models. Between systems, Qwen3-TTS leads on
intelligibility and pitch register and is hardest to tell from real
speech, yet lags on prosody and per-speaker pitch. Chatterbox is a
robust second, and XTTS-v2 should be restricted to read speech. No proxy
settles that choice, because neither our objective score nor an LLM
judge reproduces the listener ranking. No measure we tested tracks
perceived realism closely enough to replace a listener, so automatic
evaluation of cloned speech is an open problem and a small forced-choice
test in the target domain the only arbiter. Safeguards fare no better,
as an off-the-shelf countermeasure flags Qwen3-TTS clones no more often
than genuine recordings outside read speech and is unusable on ATC radio
at any threshold, making disclosure \[22\] more dependable than post-hoc
detection. Future work should target ATC, where a cloned controller
voice on a live frequency is a safety risk that no detector we tested
can flag, making a channel-matched countermeasure the priority.
