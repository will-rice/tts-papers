# Benchmarking Neural Defend ARCAS 290M: A Foundation Model for AI-Generated Audio Detection

 

 

Sivashankar Selvarajan¹  Piyush Verma¹  Sumit Kumar¹  Sharayu N.
Deshmukh¹

¹¹footnotetext: Neural Defend Inc. Correspondence to: Sivashankar
Selvarajan \<siva@neuraldefend.com\>.

###### Abstract

Voice cloning now needs only seconds of audio, and detectors tuned to
one dataset often fail when the generator, channel, or language changes.
We benchmark ARCAS 290M, Neural Defend’s foundation model for detecting
AI-generated audio. A single frozen production model, with no
benchmark-specific fine-tuning, generalizes across text-to-speech,
voice-conversion, and codec-language-model generators, telephone and
Bluetooth channels, recording conditions, and languages. We evaluate it
on the complete official test sets of ten public benchmarks, scoring
every recording exactly as released, and against commercial systems on
independent leaderboards. ARCAS 290M ranks first among all systems on
the Podonos leaderboard and has the lowest equal error rate among
commercial detectors on ASVspoof 5. It separates real from AI-generated
speech almost perfectly on ASVspoof 5, SpeechFake, and ShiftySpeech, and
detects nearly all AI-generated speech in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali, and
Telugu. On every benchmark with a published zero-shot comparison,
including in-the-wild audio, partial fakes, and the conditions hardest
for the field, it surpasses the strongest reported detector by a wide
margin. These results establish ARCAS 290M as a general-purpose
foundation for AI-generated audio detection in fraud prevention,
platform safety, and media verification.

## 1 Introduction

Generative speech models can now clone a voice from a few seconds of
reference audio, and human listeners identify current synthetic speech
as fake only about 69% of the time (Müller and Choong, 2026).
AI-generated audio is already used for voice phishing, impersonation of
executives and public figures, and fabricated evidence. Reliable
detection has therefore become core infrastructure for call centers,
banks, platforms, and newsrooms, and it must hold up on audio that no
laboratory dataset anticipated.

Generators change every few months; audio reaches the detector through
codecs, telephone networks, loudspeakers, and social platforms; and an
attacker may alter a single word rather than a whole recording.
Published detectors are typically trained and tested within one
benchmark and lose much of their accuracy when the generator, channel,
or language changes (Huang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Chandra et
al., 2025). A production system must instead behave as a foundation
model for the task: one model that transfers, without retraining, across
generators, channels, languages, and recording conditions.

In this report we benchmark ARCAS 290M, Neural Defend’s foundation model
for AI-generated audio detection. We score the complete official test
sets of ten public benchmarks, 5,644,079 recordings in total, exactly as
released: ASVspoof 5, SpeechFake, ShiftySpeech, CodecFake+, ML-ITW,
EchoFake, HQ-MPSD, LlamaPartialSpoof, IndicSynth, and PartialEdit. They
span current text-to-speech, voice-conversion, and codec-language-model
generators; codec, telephone, and Bluetooth channels (Wang et al.,
2024); partial manipulations (Luong et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Zhang
et al., 2025); physical replay (Zhang et al., 2026); social-media audio
(Li et al., 2026); and more than 15 languages, including four Indian
languages (Sharma et al., 2025). The model is evaluated as deployed,
through its production endpoint with no benchmark-specific fine-tuning,
following the protocol of the companion ARCAS 1B evaluation (Selvarajan
et al., 2026).

ARCAS 290M ranks first of 28 systems on the independent Podonos
leaderboard, with 99.93% accuracy (Podonos, 2026; Neural Defend, 2026),
and has the lowest ASVspoof 5 EER (0.14%) among the commercial detectors
reported on that set (Dowerah et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2024). Across
the five benchmarks of whole-file AI-generated speech it detects 99.60%
of 4.80 million fakes. On every benchmark with a published zero-shot
comparison, its EER is less than half that of the strongest reported
detector, and more than ten times lower on codec-language-model speech
and on HQ-MPSD partial fakes.

This evidence differs from how detection performance is usually
reported. Vendors typically cite accuracy on internal test sets whose
composition is not released, and academic papers often subsample or
re-encode test sets. Here every number comes from a complete, unmodified
public test set or an independent private-label leaderboard, with exact
denominators and confidence intervals, so each claim can be checked
against the released data.

Our contributions are:

- •
  A foundation model for AI-generated audio detection. A single
  production model that detects fully and partially AI-generated speech
  across generators, codecs, channels, and languages, with no
  task-specific fine-tuning.
- •
  A head-to-head comparison with commercial systems. Results on the same
  test data as commercial detectors, on the Podonos leaderboard and the
  Speech DF Arena, so buyers can compare vendors directly.
- •
  Full-scale, condition-level evaluation. Every recording of ten
  official test sets, broken down by generator, codec and channel,
  replay setup, language, platform, and fake-segment share, including
  the conditions hardest for all detectors and where human review adds
  most value.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Generalization in synthetic-speech detection

The ASVspoof challenges established the protocol of training on one set
of attacks and testing on unseen ones. ASVspoof 5 extends it to
crowdsourced Multilingual LibriSpeech speech, 16 unseen evaluation
attacks including adversarial filters, and 11 codec and telephone
conditions (Wang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026).
Its best open-condition system reached 2.59% EER on the Track 1
evaluation set, and its AASIST baseline 29.12% (Wang et al., 2024).

Later datasets show that generalization fails along several axes.
SpeechFake and MLAAD broaden generator and language coverage to tens of
text-to-speech (TTS) and voice-conversion (VC) systems (Huang et al.,
2025; Müller et al., 2024). CodecFake+ targets speech produced by
neural-codec language models and real speech re-synthesized through
neural codecs (Chen et al., 2025). ShiftySpeech holds the vocoder fixed
and shifts the source domain, language, and speaker (Garg et al., 2025).
Across these studies, detectors trained on ASVspoof 2019 reach EERs of
roughly 19–39% on the newer sets (Huang et al., 2025; Chen et al.,
2025). Generalization, robustness to channel effects, calibration, and
operating-point behavior are distinct properties, and we report them
separately.

Baseline detectors fall into a few families. Spectral and raw-waveform
models such as LFCC-based classifiers, RawNet2, and AASIST learn
artifacts directly from the training attacks. Self-supervised front-ends
such as wav2vec 2.0 and XLS-R transfer better because they are
pre-trained on large amounts of real speech (Huang et al., 2025; Chen et
al., 2025). Their gains are largest in-domain: a W2V+AASIST model
trained on SpeechFake’s bilingual split reaches 3.54% EER on its own
test set but 23.78% when trained on ASVspoof 2019 instead (Huang et al.,
2025). Training-data diversity matters as much as architecture. On
ML-ITW, the same XLSR+AASIST model ranges from 49.40% EER when trained
on ASVspoof 2019 to 27.65% when trained on SpoofCeleb (Li et al., 2026).

### 2.2 Benchmarks and performance interpretation

A benchmark number depends on the test population as much as on the
detector. Accuracy changes with the real-to-fake ratio, which in our
test sets ranges from about 1:1 (EchoFake) to about 1:12
(LlamaPartialSpoof). Subsampled or re-balanced test sets make
cross-paper comparison unreliable. We therefore report EER and
class-wise rates alongside accuracy and record exact denominators.

Recent benchmarks target conditions under which detectors fail.
LlamaPartialSpoof splices LLM-rewritten phrases into real recordings
(Luong et al., 2024); HQ-MPSD builds artifact-controlled partial fakes
in eight languages with added noise and echo (Li et al., 2025); and
PartialEdit changes a single word with neural speech editors (Zhang et
al., 2025). Detectors trained on PartialSpoof reach 24.49%, 51.38%, and
23.72% EER on these three sets, respectively (Luong et al., 2024; Li et
al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025).

EchoFake replays real and fake speech through loudspeakers and
re-records it; its zero-shot baselines score 37.81–56.23% EER on the
open set (Zhang et al., 2026). ML-ITW collects social-media deepfakes in
14 languages, on which eleven academic detectors score 40.49–50.03% EER
(Li et al., 2026). IndicSynth reports 8–93% EER for ASVspoof-trained
models across Indian languages (Sharma et al., 2025).

Deepfake-Eval-2024 is one of the few open evaluations of commercial
detectors: on audio circulated in 2024, open-source models lost about
48% of their AUROC, while the best of eight commercial audio detectors
reached 0.89 accuracy and 0.93 AUROC (Chandra et al., 2025). The Speech
DF Arena evaluates open and proprietary detectors on 14 datasets,
including ASVspoof 5 (Dowerah et al., 2026), and the Podonos leaderboard
scores commercial APIs and open models on a private-label set of modern
voice-cloning audio (Podonos, 2026). On both, proprietary systems far
outperform open models trained on ASVspoof 2019.

Metric choice is a second source of divergence. The ASVspoof 5 challenge
ranks systems by minimum detection cost (minDCF), reports actual cost
(actDCF) and $`C_{\mathrm{llr}}`$ to expose calibration, and keeps EER
as a secondary threshold-free measure (Wang et al., 2024). Most other
benchmarks report EER only, and partial-fake studies add segment-level
EER (Zhang et al., 2025; Luong et al., 2025). A detector can therefore
look strong under one metric and weak under another. We report each
benchmark in its native population and treat cross-paper numbers as
context rather than as a controlled ranking.

### 2.3 Audio-only evaluation

In deployment, a detector score is one signal within a wider
authenticity review that may include caller metadata, video, provenance
records, and human judgment. An audio-only benchmark measures how well
that signal separates real from synthetic speech under stated
conditions. It does not measure the accuracy of a full review process,
attribution of a fake to a specific generator, or resistance to an
attacker who adapts to the detector.

## 3 Methodology and Evaluation Design

The evaluation follows one principle: each benchmark is scored as its
authors released it. We download the complete official test set, send
every file unchanged to the production endpoint, and compute all metrics
from the returned verdicts and scores. No record is sampled, converted,
or added from another corpus, and no benchmark-specific adjustment is
made. This section describes the evaluated system, how records are
accounted for, how decisions and metrics are derived, and how
uncertainty is reported.

### 3.1 System Scope and Reproducibility

ARCAS 290M (version v3.1d) is Neural Defend’s production foundation
model for AI-generated audio detection. All records of all ten
benchmarks returned a valid response; no record is unscored.

The detector is accessed through its HTTP inference endpoint, which
accepts one audio file per request and returns a verdict and a
confidence $`c_{i}`$. For an audio record $`x_{i}`$, we derive a fake
score

```math
s_{i}=\begin{cases}c_{i}&\text{if the verdict is \emph{AI-Generated}},\\
1-c_{i}&\text{if the verdict is \emph{Genuine}}.\end{cases} \tag{1}
```

One frozen detector and decision policy were used throughout, with no
fine-tuning, calibration, or benchmark-specific update. Because the
endpoint reports no model version, 20 fixed canary records were
re-scored every 30 minutes throughout the run; all returned
bit-identical outputs. Re-scoring clips of an earlier 2,000-record pilot
reproduced every earlier output exactly.

### 3.2 Evaluation Design and Statistical Methods

#### 3.2.1 Study Design and Record Accounting

##### Population and labels.

The study includes ten public benchmarks (Table 7). For each, we use its
complete official test or evaluation release, with labels from the
authors’ protocol files. Where no test split is published and the
dataset is released for evaluation, we use the full release. Files are
uploaded exactly as distributed (FLAC, WAV, MP3, or audio bytes stored
in Parquet) at their original sample rate. Records use $`y_{i}=0`$ for
real speech and $`y_{i}=1`$ for fake speech. Denominators use scored
records.

PartialEdit and IndicSynth are released without real speech. For these
we report recall on fakes only. We do not pair them with real audio from
other corpora, because recording and format differences would confound
the comparison.

Coverage accounting is kept separate from detector error accounting. A
record is scored if the endpoint returns a valid verdict. HTTP errors,
undecodable files, and files larger than 50 MiB are recorded as unscored
and reported as coverage, never as correct or incorrect. Every file sent
to the endpoint is byte-identical to the released file; this was
verified by SHA-256 on a sample of files.

##### Decision rule and confusion counts.

The predicted label $`\hat{y}_{i}`$ is the endpoint’s own verdict; we
apply no threshold of our own. This is equivalent to
$`\hat{y}_{i}=\mathbb{1}[s_{i}>0.5]`$ except at $`s_{i}=0.5`$ (a
confidence of exactly 0.5), where the endpoint returns either verdict;
in that case the returned verdict is used. Scores are rounded to four
decimals, so threshold-free curves step through each distinct score
value. With fake speech as the positive class,

```math
\displaystyle N \displaystyle=TP+TN+FP+FN, \tag{2}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathrm{Acc} \displaystyle=\frac{TP+TN}{N}, \tag{3}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathrm{TPR} \displaystyle=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad\mathrm{TNR}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP}, \tag{4}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathrm{Precision} \displaystyle=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \tag{5}
```

```math
\displaystyle F_{1} \displaystyle=\frac{2TP}{2TP+FP+FN}. \tag{6}
```

Where presentation-attack terminology is useful, fake speech is the
attack class, so $`\mathrm{APCER}=1-\mathrm{TPR}`$ and
$`\mathrm{BPCER}=1-\mathrm{TNR}`$.

##### Partial fakes and utterance-level labels.

The endpoint returns one verdict per file, so every benchmark is
evaluated at utterance level. A record that contains any synthetic or
edited segment is labeled fake, following each dataset’s protocol.
Segment-level localization metrics are therefore not computed.
Fake-segment length, where released, is used only to group records.

##### Failure slices and baseline comparison.

Failure slices are defined only from metadata released with each
benchmark, such as generator, codec or channel, replay device, language,
and platform; we do not infer or add labels. Published baselines are
quoted from each dataset’s own paper and placed next to our results only
when they use the same test population. Each baseline is marked as
in-domain or zero-shot.

#### 3.2.2 Metrics and Aggregation

##### Ranking metrics and native metrics.

The EER and AUROC are computed from $`s_{i}`$ and are independent of the
decision rule. For ASVspoof 5, we run the official Track 1 evaluation
package (minDCF, actDCF, $`C_{\mathrm{llr}}`$) (Wang et al., 2024). The
endpoint returns a capped probability rather than a calibrated
log-likelihood ratio, so actDCF and $`C_{\mathrm{llr}}`$ describe the
deployed system rather than its best operating point.

##### Aggregation and uncertainty.

All metrics are pooled at record level within each benchmark.
Proportions carry 95% Wilson score intervals,

```math
\frac{\hat{p}+\frac{z^{2}}{2n}\pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}+\frac{z^{2}}{4n^{2}}}}{1+\frac{z^{2}}{n}},\qquad z=1.96, \tag{7}
```

and EER and AUROC intervals use 1,000 bootstrap resamples drawn
separately within the real and fake classes, so each resample keeps the
original class counts (fixed seed 12345). Slices with fewer than 30 real
or 30 fake records are reported but marked as too small to judge.

### 4.1 ASVspoof 5 Evaluation

ASVspoof 5 is the fifth edition of the automatic speaker verification
spoofing challenge (Wang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). Its Track 1
evaluation set contains 680,774 records (138,688 bona fide, 542,086
spoofed) built from Multilingual LibriSpeech. It covers 16 unseen
attacks, including TTS, voice conversion, and the adversarial filters
Malafide and Malacopula, and every attack appears under 12 conditions:
uncoded, Opus, AMR, Speex, EnCodec, MP3, M4A, MP3 followed by EnCodec,
three narrowband codecs, and real telephone and Bluetooth devices. All
680,774 records were scored.

Figure 2 compares the 0.14% EER of ARCAS 290M with the best open- and
closed-condition challenge submissions and the AASIST baseline. With the
official evaluation package, ARCAS reaches minDCF 0.00361, against
0.0750 for the best open-condition submission. The endpoint returns an
uncalibrated score, mapped to $`\log((1-s_{i})/s_{i})`$ for the package,
so actDCF (0.00644) and $`C_{\mathrm{llr}}`$ (0.01486 bits) describe the
system as deployed. Challenge systems were trained under the closed
rules (ASVspoof 5 training data only) or the open rules (external data
allowed), so the figure is contextual. ARCAS 290M is below the lowest
reported challenge EER, by a factor of more than 18.

Commercial detectors have also been evaluated on ASVspoof 5. The Speech
DF Arena leaderboard, run by its academic maintainers, reports EERs on
the ASVspoof 5 evaluation data of 0.38% for Modulate VELMA-2, 0.45% for
Resemble DETECT-3B Omni, 0.71% for Whispeak V2, and 0.79% for Hiya
(Dowerah et al., 2026), and Reality Defender’s challenge system reached
5.5% EER on the Track 1 evaluation set (Zhu et al., 2024). ARCAS 290M,
at 0.14%, has the lowest EER of these systems (Figures 1 and 2). The
arena does not state whether it uses all 680,774 Track 1 evaluation
records, so this comparison is indicative.

The residual errors are concentrated rather than random. The pre-trained
YourTTS attack accounts for 146 of the 302 misses (99.58% recall on
34,414 records), and by condition 111 misses follow MP3 plus EnCodec, 72
narrowband Opus, and 58 EnCodec. False alarms are spread across
conditions without a dominant cause. The result should be read as robust
to transmission, with double compression and one TTS attack as the
measurable weak points. In practice, a voice clone delivered over
WhatsApp, a mobile call, or a Bluetooth headset is caught as reliably as
a clean file, with about one error in every 1,300 recordings.

### 4.2 SpeechFake Evaluation

SpeechFake, published at ACL 2025, is a large-scale multilingual
speech-deepfake dataset built with about 40 generation tools (Huang et
al., 2025). Its release defines test sets as experiment lists rather
than as a single fixed evaluation split; we score the union of all
official test lists, 906,068 records (117,841 real, 788,227 fake) from
31 open-source generators in 13 languages. The ten commercial-API
subsets are unreleased and not evaluated, and no official test list
contains Hindi. Released counts differ slightly from the paper; we use
the released files.

Figure 3 compares ARCAS 290M with AASIST and W2V+AASIST trained on
SpeechFake’s bilingual training split and with W2V+AASIST trained on
ASVspoof 2019, all on the bilingual (BD) test list. On that list alone
(346,307 records: 309,065 fake, 37,242 real), ARCAS reaches 0.556% EER
(95% CI 0.523–0.588), 99.70% accuracy, and AUROC 0.9998; English and
Chinese sub-lists give 0.576% and 0.485%. Pooled over all lists, EER is
0.23%.

Figure 3: Neural Defend ARCAS 290M and selected published SpeechFake
results (EER, %). All values are on the bilingual (BD) test list.
Source: SpeechFake, Table 2 (Huang et al., 2025).

ARCAS 290M reaches 99.92% recall and 99.58% specificity. TTS is detected
at 100.00% (2 of 434,878 missed) and voice conversion at 100.00% (10 of
273,076 missed). EER is 0.44% for English and 0.35% for Chinese, and the
other 11 languages are at 0.00–0.10%, except Icelandic, which has too
few real records.

Misses concentrate in one generator family. Of the 665 misses, 635 are
BigVGAN vocoder re-syntheses (94.96% recall on 12,601 records) and 18
are StyleMelGAN. Vocoder re-synthesis keeps the speaker and words and
leaves few artifacts, making it the one measurable gap. False alarms
(0.42% of real speech) are mostly English (275) and Chinese (205). For a
platform handling voice notes in these 13 languages, about one fake in
1,200 would pass unflagged.

### 4.3 ShiftySpeech Evaluation

ShiftySpeech tests whether detectors hold up under distribution shift
(Garg et al., 2025). Real speech from audiobooks, podcasts, YouTube,
AISHELL Mandarin read speech, and JSUT Japanese read speech is
re-synthesized by 12 neural vocoders (APNet2, BigVGAN, BigVSAN,
HiFi-GAN, iSTFTNet, MB-MelGAN, Parallel WaveGAN, Style-MelGAN, UnivNet
in two sizes, Vocos, and WaveGrad). The same vocoders are applied to
Common Voice, LJSpeech, VoxCeleb, LibriTTS train-clean-360, and VCTK,
and the release adds TTS fakes from XTTS, VITS, FastPitch, Glow-TTS,
Grad-TTS, and YourTTS. We score all 106 archives of the released test
split: 2,781,458 records (58,806 real, 2,722,652 fake, of which
2,466,712 are vocoder re-syntheses and 255,940 TTS). Only the five
domains listed first contain real speech.

Figure 4 compares the 0.94% pooled EER of ARCAS 290M with the authors’
SSL-AASIST detector trained on WaveFake vocoders. The authors report
average EER over test vocoders and domains, from 9.14% (trained on
HiFi-GAN) to 32.37% (trained on WaveGlow), and 13.57–14.79% in a
leave-one-vocoder-out setting. The metrics differ, so the comparison is
contextual.

Figure 4: Neural Defend ARCAS 290M pooled EER and author-reported
SSL-AASIST average EER on ShiftySpeech (%). SSL-AASIST values average
over test vocoders and domains and depend on the training vocoder.
Source: ShiftySpeech, Tables 5–6 (Garg et al., 2025).

ARCAS 290M reaches 99.33% recall and 98.83% specificity (AUROC 0.9995).
All six TTS systems are detected at 100.00% (11 of 255,940 missed).
Among vocoders, recall ranges from 95.05% for APNet2 and 97.57% for
BigVGAN to 99.56% for UnivNet, MB-MelGAN, Style-MelGAN, and Parallel
WaveGAN. By source domain, EER is 0.02% for JSUT, 0.28% for podcasts,
0.38% for audiobooks, and 0.59% for AISHELL, but 5.42% for YouTube,
where recall falls to 93.64% and specificity to 95.88%. By language, EER
is 1.05% for English, 0.25% for Chinese, and 0.02% for Japanese.

The shift that matters is the source audio, not the language or the
vocoder. Of the 18,197 misses, 11,975 come from YouTube-sourced audio,
and 646 of the 690 false alarms are real YouTube clips. By generator,
misses are led by BigVGAN (4,883), APNet2 (3,149), and Vocos (2,441).
Already-compressed, noisy web audio is the weak condition, consistent
with the in-the-wild results below. For content platforms, clips
re-uploaded from video sites deserve a second check, while podcast,
audiobook, and read speech is screened almost perfectly.

### 4.4 CodecFake+ Evaluation

CodecFake+ studies speech produced by neural-codec language models (Chen
et al., 2025). It has two evaluation sets. CoSG contains speech from 17
codec-LM TTS systems, including VALL-E, MaskGCT, and NaturalSpeech 2
and 3; its released label file lists 1,797 records (866 real, 931 fake),
16 more real records than the paper. The CoRS evaluation set
re-synthesizes real VCTK speech of speakers p227 and p228 through 31
neural codecs: 24,160 records (755 real, 23,405 fake). Four codec
versions absent from the official label file were not scored.

Figure 5 compares the 1.62% CoSG EER of ARCAS 290M with W2V2-AASIST and
AASIST trained on ASVspoof data, with or without CoRS. The strongest
baseline, trained with CoRS and ASVspoof 5, reaches 8.07%. CoRS EERs are
reported only graphically and are not compared; Figure 6 instead shows

### 4.6 EchoFake Evaluation

EchoFake contains AI-generated fakes from 11 recent zero-shot
voice-cloning systems released 2023–2025, including F5-TTS, LLaSA,
MaskGCT, and StyleTTS2 (Zhang et al., 2026). Real and fake speech are
also replayed through loudspeakers and re-recorded on phones, laptops,
and earbuds in varied rooms and at varied distances. We score the
closed-set (5,991) and open-set (25,600) evaluation splits, 31,591
records: 7,900 real, 7,900 fake, 7,898 replayed real, and 7,893 replayed
fake. Replayed records keep their original label; the public open set
has 400 fewer records than the paper’s 32,000.

Figure 8 compares the 7.74% open-set EER of ARCAS 290M with in-domain
and zero-shot baselines. The open set adds unseen speakers, TTS systems,
and replay conditions. On the closed set, the in-domain wav2vec 2.0
baseline is stronger (0.27% against 10.83%).

ARCAS 290M reaches 78.74% recall with 99.65% specificity. On original

Protection is strongest on digitally captured audio, such as call
recordings and uploads, rather than loudspeaker re-recordings.

### 4.8 LlamaPartialSpoof Evaluation

### 4.10 PartialEdit Evaluation

PartialEdit changes one word in real VCTK sentences with a neural speech
editor, mimicking the subtle edits used to alter recorded statements or
interviews (Zhang et al., 2025). Two editors are released, VoiceCraft
and SSR-Speech; only the edited word is generated and pasted back into
the original recording, so the file keeps the speaker, prosody, and
recording conditions of the original. Each edited set also has a codec
variant in which the whole file has passed through the editor’s neural
codec. We score the official 70-speaker evaluation split of release
v1.1: 107,454 fakes. With no real speech, only recall is reported.

Figure 12 reports recall by subset. Author-reported baselines use EER
with real VCTK speech and are not comparable to fake-only recall; for
context, XLSR-SLS trained on PartialSpoof reaches 23.72% EER on
PartialEdit and 12.95% on PartialEdit-Codec (Zhang et al., 2025).

Figure 12: Neural Defend ARCAS 290M recall on PartialEdit v1.1
evaluation fakes by subset (26,841 VoiceCraft and 26,886 SSR-Speech
records per variant) (Zhang et al., 2025).

ARCAS 290M reaches 66.91% recall (95% Wilson interval 66.62–67.19).
Single-word edits without the codec pass are detected at 31.0%
(VoiceCraft) and 36.6% (SSR-Speech), but the same edits are detected at
100% when the whole file has passed through the codec. By edited share
of the clip, recall is 2.4% below 10%, 24.6% at 10–25%, 62.6% at 25–50%,
and 92.8% at 50% or more (471 records).

The plain-versus-codec contrast indicates that the detector responds to
file-wide codec artifacts, not to the edit itself. Misses are split
evenly between editors (VoiceCraft 18,509; SSR-Speech 17,052) and are
confident (median fake score 0.004; 77% below 0.1). Where a single
altered word matters, as in recorded statements, ARCAS 290M should be
paired with segment-level review.

### 5.1 What the results mean in practice

Results separate into two regimes. When the whole file is synthesized by
TTS, voice-conversion, or codec-LM systems, ARCAS 290M separates real
from fake almost perfectly: EER is 0.14–2.19% on ASVspoof 5, SpeechFake,
ShiftySpeech, and CodecFake+, and recall is 99.998% on IndicSynth,
holding across 12 transmission conditions and over 15 languages. When
only part of the file is fake, the audio is replayed, or it comes from
web platforms, detection depends on how much synthetic signal reaches
the detector. There, fake share, acoustics, and devices matter more than
the generator.

Figure 15 shows the two regimes directly. On whole-file synthetic
benchmarks the real and fake score distributions sit at opposite ends of
the scale with little overlap, so the default threshold separates them
cleanly. In the partial-fake, replay, and in-the-wild benchmarks, many
fakes score near zero rather than near the 0.5 boundary, so the detector
is confident, not uncertain, about these misses. The DET curves in
Figure 14 show the same pattern across all nine benchmarks with real
speech. For deployment, this means the default threshold needs no
adjustment on whole-file synthetic audio. For partial, replayed, or
web-sourced audio, lowering the threshold would raise false alarms
faster than it recovers missed fakes, so these cases are better routed
to segment-level analysis or human review, where an analyst can examine
the suspect segment and the recording conditions. Routing rules can
therefore be set by audio type rather than by generator, which is rarely
known in practice.

Two observations point to the same mechanism. On PartialEdit, identical
single-word edits are detected 31–37% of the time, but 100% after the
whole file passes through a neural codec. On EchoFake, the same fakes
are detected 99.84% of the time before replay and 57.62% after, while
real speech passes more than 99% of the time in both cases. The detector
responds to signal-level artifacts spread across the file; when those
artifacts are confined to a short span, or washed out by a loudspeaker
and microphone, the file-level verdict follows the real portion. The
same mechanism explains the weaker ML-ITW and ShiftySpeech YouTube
results, where platform compression and background audio dilute the
artifacts left by whole-file synthesis.

### 5.2 Where to rely on ARCAS 290M and where to add review

Under the stated conditions, the benchmark supports three operational
statements. First, whole-file synthetic speech, including
codec-compressed and telephone audio, is reliably detected; this covers
voice-cloned calls and messages produced directly by TTS or
voice-conversion tools. Second, partial manipulations are the main
residual risk: a single replaced word or short phrase in genuine speech
is usually not flagged, so use cases where such edits matter, such as
evidence review or recorded statements, need segment-level analysis or
human review. Third, over-the-air capture reduces detection: audio
recorded through a microphone from a loudspeaker loses about 40 points
of recall, especially with low-cost recording devices. Source and
language effects add to these: already-compressed web audio (YouTube in
ShiftySpeech, several platforms in ML-ITW), Russian, Portuguese, and
Turkish social-media audio, and false alarms on Polish read speech are
concrete targets for data collection and validation. IndicSynth shows
high recall on four Indian languages, but without a real class it does
not measure false alarms on Indian-language speech.

### 5.3 How ARCAS 290M compares

On every benchmark where the authors report a zero-shot baseline on an
aligned population, ARCAS 290M has a lower EER, between 2.5 and more
than 11 times below the strongest zero-shot baseline: 11.05% against
27.65–50.03% on ML-ITW, 7.74% against 37.81–56.23% on the EchoFake open
set, 5.61% against 24.49–57.18% on LlamaPartialSpoof, and 1.62% against
18.92–36.95% on CodecFake+ CoSG. Against commercial detectors the
picture is the same: ARCAS 290M leads the Podonos leaderboard and has
the lowest ASVspoof 5 EER among the commercial systems on the Speech DF
Arena. These comparisons are context rather than rank. The baselines are
research models trained on one public corpus, whereas ARCAS is a
production system; some populations differ slightly; and in-domain
baselines trained on a benchmark’s own data can outperform ARCAS on that
benchmark, as on the EchoFake closed set (0.27% against 10.83%) and
HQ-MPSD English (0.29% against 1.7%).

The failure regions are shared. PartialSpoof-trained detectors reach
23.72–51.38% EER on the partial-fake sets, and EchoFake’s in-domain
baselines pass only 65.9–67.6% of replayed real speech on the open set
(Zhang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). The
difficulties identified here are properties of the conditions, not of a
single detector. They also define where future benchmarks are most
needed: short partial edits, replay through consumer devices, and real
speech from more languages, platforms, and accents.

### 5.4 Deploying ARCAS 290M

The benchmark does not replace validation on a deployment’s own audio.
Recordings from the actual capture chain (devices, codecs, platforms,
and languages) should be used to set operating points, and real-speech
scores should be monitored for false-alarm drift. The endpoint’s
confidence is not a calibrated likelihood ratio, so thresholds should
come from validation data. Each decision score should be logged with the
integration version and date. The detector output should be one signal
in a review process, with human review for high-impact decisions.

## 6 Conclusion

We benchmarked ARCAS 290M, Neural Defend’s foundation model for
AI-generated audio detection, at full scale on the complete official
test sets of ten public benchmarks. On the independent Podonos
leaderboard it ranks first of 28 systems, ahead of every other vendor,
and on ASVspoof 5 its EER is below that of every commercial system on
the Speech DF Arena. On whole-file AI-generated speech, accuracy is
99.32–99.92% on ASVspoof 5, SpeechFake, and ShiftySpeech, and across
five such benchmarks 99.60% of 4.80 million fakes are detected,
including 99.998% of IndicSynth’s Indian-language fakes. Performance
holds across codec and telephone conditions, and EER is lower than every
zero-shot baseline reported on aligned populations. Condition-level
diagnostics place the remaining difficulty in short partial edits,
physically replayed audio, already-compressed web audio, and in-the-wild
speech in some languages. These conditions remain the hardest for every
published detector, so they mark where detection research, benchmark
design, and human review should focus next. We recommend that detectors
be reported on complete test sets with per-condition breakdowns, not one
score per dataset. Comparisons rely on author-reported baselines,
PartialEdit and IndicSynth measure recall only, and calibration and
adaptive attacks are not assessed.
