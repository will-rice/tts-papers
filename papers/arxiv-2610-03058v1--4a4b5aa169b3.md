# Unsupervised Instantaneous Phase and Frequency Tracking by Inverse Voice Synthesis

Chin-Yun Yu    György Fazekas

###### Abstract

Knowledge-driven neural vocoders struggle to learn reliable fundamental
frequency end-to-end, because spectral objectives provide weak
supervision of periodic structure and lack phase information. We address
this with a source-filter model whose alias-free additive source makes
the instantaneous phase of the glottal cycle explicit; differentiating
it yields the instantaneous frequency, and thus $`F_{0}`$, without an
external tracker. Waveform error supervises only the deterministic
harmonic path, while a spectral loss covers the full signal. On M4Singer
and LM-SSD, the reconstruction is phase-aligned, reaching a
signal-to-reconstruction-error ratio of $`8.1\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}`$,
while neural baselines remain negative. However, GOLF, given an external
$`F_{0}`$, still reaches lower spectral distortion. On LM-SSD, the
recovered $`F_{0}`$ attains the highest overall accuracy of any method
tested, including supervised neural pitch trackers applied off the
shelf, and the glottal closure instants come within 0.53 points of
REAPER’s identification rate, without any $`F_{0}`$ label.

###### Index Terms: 

singing voice analysis, pitch detection, glottal closure instant
detection, source-filter model, instantaneous phase

## 1 Introduction

The fundamental frequency ($`F_{0}`$) is the rate of vocal fold
vibration. It shapes intonation in speech and singing and is a key
control in parametric voice synthesis \[1, 2\]. These systems work by
analysis-by-synthesis: parameters are estimated from the input, then
used to rebuild it with a source-filter model \[3\].

Recent neural vocoders integrate parameter extraction with neural
networks \[4, 5, 6\], but learning $`F_{0}`$ end-to-end is hard because
magnitude-spectral losses drop phase and give noisy gradients for
periodicity \[7\]. Prior systems therefore use synthetic
self-supervision \[8, 9\] or pitch-tracker labels \[6, 10\]; other
losses change STFT settings \[11\] or use optimal transport \[12\], but
their effect on synthesis is unclear. Consequently, many systems rely on
external $`F_{0}`$ estimators \[13\], which are not differentiable,
block joint training, and pass their errors into synthesis.

Most neural vocoders ignore phase because waveform misalignment is
rarely audible \[14\]. GOLF \[10, 13\] is an exception: it synthesises
with an all-pole filter and a glottal model, so its phase response
follows the physics of the vocal tract. This lets GOLF capture glottal
pulses by waveform matching \[10\] to fine-tune the predicted
parameters, enabling recovery of instantaneous phase and frequency. GOLF
itself cannot follow that path: its oscillator is still driven by an
external $`F_{0}`$.

We introduce GOLF-inv with four contributions: (1) an additive glottal
source that avoids wavetable resampling aliasing, with a closed-form
pulse whose derivatives are analytic; (2) a phase correction from the
Quasi-Harmonic Model (QHM) \[15\] that integrates frequency across
frames without error accumulation; (3) a numerically stable state-space
lattice filter evaluated by a generalised parallel prefix scan; and (4)
unsupervised joint learning of $`F_{0}`$, phase, and envelopes from
complementary spectral and harmonic-only waveform losses. Throughout,
reconstruction quality matters only as evidence that the inferred
parameters explain the signal. Audio examples are available online ¹¹ 1
Available at
[yoyolicoris.github.io/golf-inv-demo/](https://yoyolicoris.github.io/golf-inv-demo/).

## 2 Methodology

### 2.1 Model formulation

Building on GOLF \[13\], the proposed synthesiser represents the voice
$`S(z)`$ using a glottal source $`G(z)`$, a shaped noise component
$`Q(z)`$, and a vocal-tract filter $`H(z)`$:

```math
\displaystyle S(z) \displaystyle=H(z)\left(G(z)+Q(z)\right). \tag{1}
```

Here, $`Q(z)`$ is modelled as white-noise excitation passed through
learned spectral shaping (Section 2.4). A neural encoder (Section 2.6)
estimates all source and filter parameters from the input audio, trained
by minimising reconstruction losses through this synthesiser. Unlike
GOLF, which needs external $`F_{0}`$ and a resampled wavetable, GOLF-inv
builds the source additively, predicts frequency and phase per frame and
reads $`F_{0}`$ off the corrected phase (Section 2.3), and replaces the
direct-form filter with a state-space lattice (Section 2.5). Because the
harmonic branch $`H(z)G(z)`$ has a deterministic phase response, it can
be supervised by waveform mean squared error (MSE), unlike the
stochastic $`Q(z)`$ branch.

### 2.2 Training criterion

The sum of the deterministic harmonic output $`\hat{S}_{h}(z)=H(z)G(z)`$
and a stochastic output $`\hat{S}_{n}(z)=H(z)Q(z)`$ forms the full
reconstruction $`\hat{S}(z)`$. These outputs define the two
complementary loss paths in Fig. 1.

![](arxiv-2610-03058v1--4a4b5aa169b3.figures/inline-1.svg)

Figure 1: Loss routing in GOLF-inv. MSE compares only $`\hat{S}_{h}(t)`$
with $`S(z)`$, whereas MSS compares the spectrum of the full
reconstruction $`\hat{S}(z)`$ with the target $`S(z)`$.

Multi-scale spectral loss (MSS) \[11\] is applied to the full
reconstruction $`\hat{S}(z)`$, so both harmonic and stochastic
magnitudes are learned. Waveform MSE constrains phase, but the random
excitation in $`\hat{S}_{n}(z)`$ makes its waveform unpredictable. We
therefore exclude $`\hat{S}_{n}(z)`$ from MSE and apply it only to the
deterministic harmonic output $`\hat{S}_{h}(z)`$. This works well in
practice, likely because voiced regions dominate our training data. The
total objective sums harmonic-only waveform MSE and $`\lambda`$-weighted
full-signal MSS.

### 2.3 The glottal source $`G(z)`$

GOLF resamples a non-band-limited LF glottal-pulse wavetable \[16, 10\],
which can introduce aliasing artefacts. Later versions oversample \[13\]
to mitigate it, but cannot remove it. In this work, we generate the
glottal source using additive synthesis:

```math
\displaystyle g(t) \displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\cos\left(\phi_{k}(t)\right). \tag{2}
```

We assume one canonical pulse shape, so the amplitudes $`a_{k}`$ and
phase offsets $`\psi_{k}`$ are global and time-invariant.
$`\phi_{k}(t)`$ is computed from the instantaneous phase $`\phi(t)`$ and
the offset:

```math
\displaystyle\phi_{k}(t) \displaystyle=k\phi(t)+\psi_{k}. \tag{3}
```

We set $`\psi_{1}=0`$ so the fundamental harmonic is in phase with
$`\phi(t)`$. $`K`$ is set to 100 in this work, and we band-limit the
glottal source by setting $`a_{k}=0`$ when
$`\frac{d\phi_{k}(t)}{dt}\geq\pi`$, so no partial crosses Nyquist. We
constrain $`\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}^{2}=1`$ and
$`a_{1}>a_{2}>\dots>a_{K}>0`$ so the first harmonic stays strongest and
$`\phi(t)`$ tracks the fundamental rather than a sub-harmonic.

To obtain $`\phi(t)`$ without accumulating integration errors across
frames, we adopt the phase correction mechanism from QHM \[15, 17\]. The
encoder predicts frame-wise frequency $`\hat{f}(t_{l})`$ and phase
$`\phi(t_{l})`$ at frame centres $`t_{l}`$. $`\hat{f}(t_{l})`$ is then
upsampled to $`\hat{f}(t)`$ using cubic spline interpolation. For
$`t\in[t_{l},t_{l+1})`$, we integrate the instantaneous frequency from
the predicted phase at the current frame centre to obtain the
uncorrected phase

```math
\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}(t) \displaystyle=\phi(t_{l})+\int_{t_{l}}^{t}2\pi\hat{f}(\tau)\,d\tau. \tag{4}
```

The final instantaneous phase $`\phi(t)`$ is obtained by smoothly
distributing the boundary phase mismatch $`z_{l}`$:

```math
\displaystyle\phi(t)=\tilde{\phi}(t)+\frac{z_{l}}{2}\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{\pi(t-t_{l})}{t_{l+1}-t_{l}}\right)\right) \tag{5a}
```

```math
\displaystyle z_{l}=\left[\phi(t_{l+1})-\tilde{\phi}(t_{l+1})\right]_{2\pi}, \tag{5b}
```

where $`[x]_{2\pi}=(x+\pi)\bmod 2\pi-\pi`$ wraps phase differences to
$`[-\pi,\pi)`$.

However, the instantaneous frequency
$`\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\phi(t)}{dt}`$ can deviate from
$`\hat{f}(t_{l})`$. The maximum absolute frequency correction in (5)
occurs at $`t=\frac{t_{l}+t_{l+1}}{2}`$ and equals
$`\frac{|z_{l}|}{4(t_{l+1}-t_{l})}`$. With a $`100\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}`$
frame rate, that roughly means $`\pm`$$`79\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}`$ maximum
correction, significant for low-pitched voices. The encoder can thus
leverage both $`\hat{f}(t_{l})`$ and $`\phi(t_{l})`$ to encode
$`F_{0}`$. To decouple the two, we randomly adjust the integration
window to be $`[t_{l},t_{l+m})`$, where $`m\sim\mathcal{U}\{1,2,3,4\}`$
during training as a regulariser. In this way, the maximum correction is
divided by $`m`$, and the randomness forces the encoder to rely more on
$`\hat{f}(t_{l})`$ for phase conversion. We bound $`\hat{f}(t_{l})`$
within $`32960\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}`$.

### 2.4 The noise source $`Q(z)`$

Following previous design \[13\], $`Q(z)`$ denotes white noise after
learned spectral shaping. We parametrise the shaping response using
cepstra, similar to Liu et al. \[5\], but with symmetric real
coefficients, yielding zero phase. We use 30 cepstral coefficients,
sufficient for the broad shape of the noise spectrum, leaving fine
detail to $`H(z)`$. The frame-wise frequency response is converted to a
time-domain FIR via the windowing method, using an FFT size of 512 and a
149-point Hamming window.

### 2.5 The vocal tract filter $`H(z)`$

The all-pole vocal tract filter models the voice’s time-varying
dynamics. Direct-form implementations \[13\] accumulate numerical error
at high-dynamic-range summation nodes \[18\]. The vocal tract can be
discretised as a cascade of cylindrical acoustic tubes, with each area
discontinuity scattering waves into forward and backward
components \[3\]. A lattice section models the scattering at one
junction, so its reflection coefficient follows from the adjacent areas,
$`\kappa_{n}(t)=\frac{A_{n-1}(t)-A_{n}(t)}{A_{n-1}(t)+A_{n}(t)}`$. Since
$`A_{n}(t)>0`$, $`\kappa_{n}(t)`$ stays in $`(-1,1)`$, tying the
parametrisation to vocal-tract geometry and bounding the section
weights, so the summation nodes avoid the dynamic range that troubles
the direct form.

#### 2.5.1 State-space realisation of the lattice filter

We use a state-space lattice realisation \[19\], which enables parallel
evaluation. With
$`\mathbf{x}(t)=[x_{N}(t),x_{N-1}(t),\dots,x_{1}(t)]^{\top}`$ denoting
the state vector, an $`N^{\textit{th}}`$ order lattice filter can be
expressed as follows:

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t+1) \displaystyle=\mathbf{A}(t)\mathbf{x}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)e_{0}(t) \tag{6a}
```

```math
\displaystyle y(t) \displaystyle=\mathbf{C}^{\top}(t)\mathbf{x}(t)+e_{0}(t) \tag{6b}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\kappa_{N}&\kappa_{N-1}&\cdots&\kappa_{2}&\kappa_{1}\\
\kappa_{N}^{c}&-\kappa_{N-1}\kappa_{N}&\cdots&-\kappa_{2}\kappa_{N}&-\kappa_{1}\kappa_{N}\\
0&\kappa_{N-1}^{c}&\cdots&-\kappa_{2}\kappa_{N-1}&-\kappa_{1}\kappa_{N-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\kappa_{2}^{c}&-\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\end{bmatrix} \tag{6c}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-\kappa_{N}&-\kappa_{N-1}&\cdots&-\kappa_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\top} \tag{6d}
```

where $`\kappa_{n}^{c}=1-\kappa_{n}^{2}`$ and we have omitted $`t`$ for
brevity. $`N=14`$ roughly corresponds to the length of a male vocal
tract at $`16\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}`$ \[20\]. We learn
$`\mathbf{C}(t)\in I\!\!R^{N}`$ to incorporate zeros and apply a
time-varying pre-filter gain $`\gamma(t)`$ before the filter; the
direct-path gain is fixed to 1.

#### 2.5.2 Parallel implementation

Evaluating (6a) directly takes $`T`$ sequential steps. Composing $`L`$
consecutive steps into a single affine transform advances the state
every $`L`$ steps:

```math
\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i}(t) \displaystyle=\prod_{j=i}^{L-1}\mathbf{A}(t+j),\quad\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{L}(t)=\mathbf{I} \tag{7a}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(t) \displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i+1}(t)\mathbf{B}(t+i)e_{0}(t+i) \tag{7b}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t+L) \displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{0}(t)\mathbf{x}(t)+\mathbf{u}(t). \tag{7c}
```

Products are time-ordered, with factors of larger $`j`$ placed to the
left. The block coefficients in (7a) and (7b) depend only on their own
block, so all blocks are computed in parallel, leaving in (7c) a
sequential chain $`L`$ times shorter. Once the block-boundary states
$`\mathbf{x}(nL)\mid_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}`$ are known, (6a) recovers the
remaining states in parallel across blocks, with $`L-1`$ sequential
steps per block. The reduced recursion has the same affine form as (6a),
so the same reduction applies to itself; recursing until one step
remains lowers the sequential cost from $`O(T)`$ to $`O(L\log_{L}T)`$.
For $`L=2`$, this is the standard parallel prefix scan \[21\]; we use
$`L=32`$, selected empirically for runtime. Our pure-PyTorch
implementation is sufficiently fast for this work ²² 2 Available at
[github.com/yoyolicoris/philtorch](https://github.com/yoyolicoris/philtorch)..

### 2.6 The analysis encoder

The encoder follows the same architecture as in \[13\], but with
$`16\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}`$ audio in this work. The input is a 4-channel
spectrogram (FFT size 512, $`100\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}`$ frame rate)
holding the log-magnitude, angle, real and imaginary parts. This STFT
feature is used only by the analysis encoder to extract synthesis
parameters; the decoder remains the physical source-filter synthesiser
in (1). The four channels are redundant, which is acceptable for an
analysis front end, unlike DDSP, which uses a mel-spectrogram to
condition synthesis \[14, 6\]. The feature passes through four 2D
convolutional layers, each followed by batch normalisation, ReLU
activation, and max pooling along the frequency axis. Then, the
frequency axis and channels are flattened and fed into three Bi-LSTM
layers with 256 hidden units and a dropout rate of 0.1. After layer
normalisation, fully connected layers project to the parameters, with
non-linearities keeping them in range. The parameters are linearly
upsampled to the audio rate for time-varying synthesis.

## 3 Experiments

### 3.1 Datasets and training details

We evaluate the proposed system on two singing voice datasets,
M4Singer \[22\] and LM-SSD \[23\], both resampled to
$`16\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}`$. M4Singer contains $`30\text{\,}\mathrm{h}`$
of Chinese singing voice from 20 singers, ranging from Bass to Soprano.
For each singer, $`10\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}`$ of the data is randomly
assigned to the test set, and the remainder is used for training; the
split is therefore within-singer. LM-SSD contains singing performances
recorded simultaneously with a larynx microphone and a studio
microphone. We select only solo recordings made with a studio microphone
and without cross-talk, yielding three singers and roughly two hours of
data. The larynx microphone captures throat vibration directly with
little cross-talk and provides the reference labels (Section 3.4,
Section 3.5); all methods process the studio signal. For each song, we
hold out the sixth take for testing, and the remaining takes form the
training set; thus, the test split contains unseen performances, but not
unseen songs or singers.

Audio is peak-normalised and cut into 32768-sample segments; we train
with AdamW, learning rate 0.0002, batch size 8. Following \[11\], the
spectral loss uses co-prime window and hop sizes, {251, 509, 1021, 2053}
and {67, 127, 257, 521}, which smooths the loss surface and helps
$`F_{0}`$ learning. We compute it with auraloss and set $`\lambda=0.01`$
from early experiments. The random integration window (Section 2.3)
hurts early convergence, so we train 10 epochs without it, then switch
it on until validation loss stops improving.

### 3.2 Model configurations

GOLF-inv is the proposed unsupervised system. GOLF uses $`F_{0}`$ from
FCNF0++ \[24\] for the oscillator, as in the original vocoder \[13\].
DDSP+$`\bf F_{0}`$ follows the DDSP-inv setting of \[9\]: FCNF0++
provides $`F_{0}`$ targets for the prediction head to follow, and
time-varying additive synthesis replaces $`H(z)`$. QHM is our
conventional analysis and resynthesis reference and we use FCNF0++’s
$`F_{0}`$ as initialisation for its analysis process \[15\]. Additional
ablations replace the lattice with the direct-form filter of \[13\],
omit $`H(z)`$ to use a QHM-only source, or disable integration-window
randomisation.

### 3.3 Copy-synthesis evaluation

Copy-synthesis reconstructs each recording from parameters inferred from
that same recording through the analysis encoder and physical
synthesiser. We report MSS, mel-cepstral distortion (MCD), perceptual
evaluation of speech quality (PESQ), and
$`\text{SRER}=10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_{t}s^{2}(t)}{\sum_{t}(s(t)-\hat{s}(t))^{2}}`$.
The first three measure spectral fidelity; SRER measures sample-level
waveform match, and thus phase. The two need not move together. A
positive SRER requires the reconstruction to follow the target sample by
sample, which phase-agnostic systems cannot do; Fig. 2 shows
$`\hat{s}(t)`$ matches $`s(t)`$ closely on a low- and a high-register
LM-SSD excerpt.

cycle.

We compute each metric per clip and average over the split, weighted by
clip duration. In Table 1, GOLF gives the lowest spectral distortion
using external $`F_{0}`$, and QHM the highest SRER, but QHM fits each
frame by least squares from an external $`F_{0}`$ and has no vocal

sinusoids to unvoiced parts too, while our noise branch $`Q(z)`$ has
random phase and leaves an irreducible residual there. GOLF-inv

$`-2.67\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}`$ to $`8.10\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}`$ despite

### 3.4 Fundamental frequency estimation

We evaluate $`F_{0}`$ estimation on LM-SSD against the larynx reference,
where we use a majority vote of REAPER \[25\], pYIN \[26\], and
SWIPE \[27\] estimates. For GOLF-inv, $`F_{0}`$ is the frame average of
$`\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\phi(t)}{dt}`$ under a local-stationarity
assumption. A frame is marked as voiced if
$`\|\hat{S}_{h}(z)\|^{2}>\|\hat{S}_{n}(z)\|^{2}`$ and the energy is
above $`-40\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}`$. We report raw pitch accuracy (RPA),
the share of frames within 50 cents of the target, and overall accuracy
(OA), which also counts unvoiced frames. Frame counts are pooled over
the split before each percentage is formed, rather than averaging
per-clip scores. Baselines are SWIPE, pYIN, REAPER, and two neural pitch
trackers, FCNF0++ \[24\] and PESTO \[28\], using their published model
weights. We report the training split as an in-sample diagnostic:
GOLF-inv trains on LM-SSD while the baselines run off the shelf, so part
of its lead comes from adapting to this data, which needs no $`F_{0}`$
labels and could therefore be repeated on any corpus.

In Table 2, GOLF-inv outperforms all neural baselines (DDSP+$`F_{0}`$,
FCNF0++, and PESTO) and achieves the highest overall accuracy on both
splits, with RPA exceeding REAPER and SWIPE. pYIN attains the highest
RPA but the lowest OA, so its accuracy on voiced frames comes with
weaker voicing decisions. Without the random window, test RPA from
$`\hat{f}(t_{l})`$ drops 5.39 points to $`86.89\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}`$,
against $`92.28\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}`$ with correction; with the random
window, the same gap is only 0.50 points, demonstrating that the
randomisation prevents the phase correction from absorbing frequency
error.

### 3.5 Glottal closure instant detection

Finally, we evaluate glottal closure instant (GCI) detection on the
LM-SSD test set against REAPER pitch marks computed on the larynx
signal. Because (2) is a closed-form sum of sinusoids, its first and
second derivatives are analytic, so the Newton-Raphson method locates
the minimum of the learned canonical pulse, which we take as its closure
phase; the corresponding instants in $`\phi(t)`$ are marked as GCIs.
Fig. 2 also plots the larynx signal and the learned source $`g(t)`$,
with the estimated and reference GCIs overlaid. Following \[29\], the
identification rate (IDR), false alarm rate (FAR), missed rate (MR), and
the standard deviation of the timing error ($`\sigma`$) are reported in
Table 3. A mark counts as correctly identified when it falls inside a
one-period window centred on the reference GCI. Hits, false alarms, and
misses are likewise pooled over the split, and $`\sigma`$ is taken over
the pooled timing errors. Different modelling assumptions can displace
estimates from the reference by a near-constant offset, as REAPER’s
marks show in Fig. 2. As a standard deviation, $`\sigma`$ ignores such
an offset and measures the spread that remains. GOLF-inv tracks the
reference in both registers, trailing REAPER by 0.53 points in IDR and
$`0.01\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}`$ in $`\sigma`$.

### 3.6 Discussion

The experiments show that a strong inductive bias in the synthesiser,
plus the waveform loss, is enough to learn instantaneous phase and
frequency without labels. Convergence depends on initialising the
predicted frequency range to overlap the data and on harmonic amplitudes
decaying with frequency. The lattice gives a higher SRER than the direct
form, which also needs gradient clipping to converge. The QHM-only
source is less stable to train, supporting the source-filter
decomposition. Each output sample of GOLF-inv sums up to 100 sinusoids
where a wavetable lookup would read a single value; in exchange, the
source avoids aliasing, and its closed form allows analysis, such as the
Newton search of Section 3.5. The main limitation is the biased
criterion: applying MSE only to the deterministic path encourages
harmonic energy where noise should dominate, audible as residual buzzing
in unvoiced regions, which voicing-dependent masking or spectral
whitening before the MSE may reduce. We only test singing voice; many
instruments share a similar source-filter structure, and speech is left
for future work.

## 4 Conclusion

Fitting a physically constrained synthesiser and neural encoder to a
waveform is enough to recover the parameters of voice production: the
$`F_{0}`$ and closure instants here are read off the encoder, and on
LM-SSD they match REAPER with no $`F_{0}`$ label. The proposed method
works because it matches the waveform, including phase, rather than the
magnitude spectrum alone. Within the limits discussed above, inverse
synthesis offers a practical route to label-free voice analysis.
