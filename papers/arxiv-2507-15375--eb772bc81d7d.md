###### Abstract

Spoken Language Models (SLMs) are designed to take speech inputs and
produce spoken responses. However, current SLMs lack the ability to
perform an internal, unspoken thinking process before responding. In
contrast, humans typically engage in complex mental reasoning
internally, enabling them to communicate ideas clearly and concisely.
Thus, integrating an unspoken thought process into SLMs is highly
desirable. While naively generating a complete chain-of-thought (CoT)
reasoning before starting to talk can enable thinking for SLMs, this
induces additional latency for the speech response, as the CoT reasoning
can be arbitrarily long. To solve this issue, we propose Stitch, a novel
generation method that alternates between the generation of unspoken
reasoning chunks and spoken response chunks. Since the audio duration of
a chunk of spoken response is much longer than the time to generate the
tokens in a chunk of spoken response, we use the remaining free time to
generate the unspoken reasoning tokens. When a chunk of audio is played
to the user, the model continues to generate the next unspoken reasoning
chunk, achieving simultaneous thinking and talking. Remarkably, Stitch
matches the latency of baselines that cannot generate unspoken CoT by
design while outperforming those baselines by 15% on math reasoning
datasets; Stitch also performs equally well on non-reasoning datasets as
those baseline models. Some animations and demonstrations are on the
project page:
[https://d223302.github.io/STITCH](https://d223302.github.io/STITCH).

## 1 Introduction

Spoken language models (SLMs) can take speech inputs and generate speech
outputs ([OpenAI, 2024a](#bib.bib17); [Défossez et al.,
2024](#bib.bib7); [Arora et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)). SLMs generate speech
by emitting some speech tokens, discrete tokens that encode the
information in speech ([Défossez et al., 2023](#bib.bib52); [Zhang et
al., 2024](#bib.bib51)), which will be converted into an audio waveform
by a speech decoder. Most SLMs are trained from a backbone text-based
LLM ([Défossez et al., 2024](#bib.bib7); [Ding et al.,
2025](#bib.bib9)); we can obtain an SLM by teaching the backbone LLM to
understand speech input and generate the speech tokens as the
output ([Xiezhifei, 2024](#bib.bib8); [Fang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib21)).

Teaching the backbone LLM to directly generate speech tokens is
challenging, as the LLM is originally trained to predict text tokens, so
the speech tokens are completely new for the LLM backbone. To make SLMs
better predict the speech tokens, current mainstream methods first
generate the text tokens before generating the speech tokens ([Chen et
al., 2024](#bib.bib11); [Zeng et al., 2024](#bib.bib12); [Nguyen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib10)). By design, those text tokens and the speech tokens
are aligned, in the sense that the text tokens correspond to the
semantic content of the speech tokens.

While the aligned text-speech generation pipeline makes it easier to
generate the speech response, these SLMs directly generate the output
text-speech tokens without any inner thinking process, though explicitly
modeling such reasoning steps has been shown helpful in text generation
tasks ([OpenAI, 2024c](#bib.bib16); [Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)).
Humans can perform complex internal reasoning before we speak: we
disambiguate questions, recall facts, manipulate variables, and then
verbalize only the polished answer. Intuitively, this unspoken internal
thinking helps improve the accuracy and coherence of our spoken content.
However, verbalizing these internal thoughts can make communication
confusing and unnecessarily lengthy. Thus, it is desirable to enable
SLMs a similar ability to think longer and silently before speaking out.

and $`N_{\text{speech}}`$ speech tokens and synthesize the speech
output. The duration $`t_{\text{chunk}}`$ is much longer than the time
for generating the text tokens and speech tokens corresponding to
$`S_{i}`$, and we use the remaining time to generate the reasoning
tokens.

A naive way to do so is to teach SLMs to generate an unspoken
chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning ([Wei et al., 2022](#bib.bib19)) in
text, and then generate the spoken responses based on the CoT reasoning.
In this way, we can benefit from the CoT reasoning without making the
spoken response overly long. While this does improve the speech output
quality, as validated by our experiments in
Section [5](#S5 "5 Main Results ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models"),
the speech responses need to wait until the full text CoT is generated,
creating an uncontrollable latency for the speech output.

To reduce and remove this latency, we propose Stitch: Simultaneous
Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning. Stitch alternately
generates unspoken reasoning chunks and spoken response chunks, allowing
the spoken responses to condition on some thinking process. Since the
audio duration of a chunk of spoken response is much longer than the
time to generate the tokens in a chunk of spoken response, we use the
remaining free time to generate the unspoken reasoning tokens. When a
chunk of audio is played to the user, Stitch keeps generating the next
unspoken reasoning chunk, achieving simultaneous thinking and talking.
Stitch-S, a variant of Stitch, has the same latency as the models that
cannot generate unspoken reasoning, while significantly outperforming
those baselines on math reasoning datasets.

We summarize our contributions as follows:

1. We propose Stitch, an SLM generation pipeline that outputs unspoken
   reasoning chunks and spoken response chunks alternately. To the best of
   our knowledge, we are the first to introduce the unspoken thinking
   process to SLMs.
2. Stitch-S, while improving the generation with unspoken reasoning, has
   a latency the same as an SLM that does not generate text reasoning by
   design.
3. On five math QA datasets, Stitch consistently outperforms the
   baseline models that cannot reason with text CoT.

## 2 Related Work: Spoken Language Models

Our paper improves SLMs by generating text reasoning before generating
the speech output. Current SLMs first generate some speech tokens, and
the speech tokens will be synthesized into a waveform by the speech
decoder. In this section, we recap how existing SLMs generate speech
tokens. The mainstream SLMs nowadays adopt two types of speech
generation methods: (1) Interleaved text-speech tokens ([Zeng et al.,
2024](#bib.bib12); [Li et al., 2025b](#bib.bib23)) and the (2)
Thinker-Talker architecture ([Xu et al., 2025](#bib.bib20); [Fang et
al., 2025b](#bib.bib26)).

In the interleaved decoding paradigm, the SLM backbone model generates a
chunk of text tokens and a chunk of speech tokens alternately. The text
tokens serve as guidance for future speech tokens by transcribing what
the speech token will say. For example, GLM-4-Voice ([Zeng et al.,
2024](#bib.bib12)) interleaves between generating $`N_{\text{text}}=13`$
text tokens and $`N_{\text{speech}}=26`$ speech tokens. After a chunk of
speech tokens is generated, it is immediately synthesized into audio by
the speech decoder and streamed to the user, enabling low latency and
real-time interaction. A figurative illustration of this output format
is shown in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 A Trivial Method: Think before Speaking in Speech (TBS) ‣ 3 Method: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models")(a).
When concatenating the chunk of text tokens, they should correspond to
the transcription of the speech tokens. The ratio of the text tokens and
speech tokens is carefully selected such that the text tokens are always
faster than the speech tokens to ensure that the content of the speech
tokens has already appeared in previous text tokens. Once all the text
tokens are generated, the model will continue to generate the remaining
speech tokens.

The thinker-talker SLM uses a thinker model to take speech input and
generate text tokens, and uses a talker model to convert the text tokens
and/or their continuous representations into speech tokens ([Xu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20); [Fang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib26)). The talker model
functions like a Text-to-Speech (TTS) model that converts the textual
output from the thinker into speech tokens, and the output by the
thinker and talker will be highly aligned in their semantics, while
there may be slight differences.

The text-speech interleaved SLMs and thinker-talker SLMs differ in their
architecture, but they share a similarity: they generate text and then
the speech, and the text is highly aligned with the speech. In this
paper, we focus on enabling SLMs to generate an unspoken thinking
process while speaking.

While reasoning, also sometimes referred to as thinking, is widely
explored in text-only LLMs ([OpenAI, 2024c](#bib.bib16); [Wu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib49); [Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib13); [Hong et al.,
2025](#bib.bib25)), reasoning is only included in audio LLMs
recently ([Wen et al., 2025](#bib.bib60); [Li et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib59); [Xie et al., 2025](#bib.bib50)). Audio LLMs are
multimodal LLMs that can take audio and speech as input, but they cannot
generate speech outputs ([Changli et al., 2023](#bib.bib3); [Chu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib4); [Xu et al., 2025](#bib.bib20); [Lu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib2)); this marks a significant difference from SLMs studied
in our paper. Currently, no prior work introduces unspoken reasoning
ability to SLMs.

## 3 Method: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning

While current SLMs also include some textual inner monologue ([Défossez
et al., 2024](#bib.bib7)) or thinking ([Xu et al., 2025](#bib.bib20))
(as in thinker-talker) before generating the speech tokens, these text
tokens simply transcribe what the model will say next. In other words,
they do not generate unspoken reasoning before speaking. In this paper,
we aim to improve the SLMs by generating some unspoken text reasoning
before generating the speech responses (including the text and speech
tokens).

Our proposed concept can be applied to both the interleaved and
thinker-talker SLMs, but we adopt the interleaved SLMs in this paper for
the following three reasons: (1) The interleaved SLM only contains a
single backbone model to generate the text and speech tokens,
simplifying the model architecture. (2) [Wu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib27)
show that the thinker-talker SLM requires careful tuning to make it
properly generate well-aligned text and speech output, making
thinker-talker harder to fine-tune. (3) Qwen-2.5-Omni, the most widely
used thinker-talker SLM, does not publicly release the speech tokenizer,
adding difficulties to fine-tune the talker model ([Liu,
2025](#bib.bib14); [Shin, 2025](#bib.bib15)).

To unify the wording, we will use reasoning tokens to refer to the
unspoken reasoning (in text), text tokens to refer to the original text
tokens of the interleaved SLMs, and speech tokens to refer to the speech
tokens. Text tokens and speech tokens are the output of interleaved SLMs
from prior works ([Zeng et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)), and the reasoning
tokens are introduced in this paper. Both text tokens and reasoning
tokens are texts, while they differ in their function: reasoning tokens
are for the unspoken thinking process, and text tokens are the
transcriptions of the spoken output.

### 3.1 A Trivial Method: Think before Speaking in Speech (TBS)

Before introducing Stitch, we start from a trivial baseline method that
introduces the thinking process to SLMs: Thinking in text Before
Speaking in speech (TBS). Introducing TBS can help us understand the
motivation and design of Stitch later. In TBS, given a user query in
speech, the model first generates a complete trace of reasoning span. A
complete reasoning span is enclosed by two special tokens \[SOR\] and
\[EOR\], denoting the start and end of the reasoning span. The \[EOR\]
token lets the model know that the reasoning has finished and it needs
to start generating the tokens for the spoken response, including the
text tokens and the speech tokens. The text tokens and the speech tokens
are generated in an interleaved manner, following the original design of
interleaved SLMs, with $`N_{\text{text}}`$ and $`N_{\text{speech}}`$
alternating with each other.

To teach SLMs to operate in TBS, we construct the training data
$`\mathcal{D}_{\text{TBS}}`$ where each training instance has the form
$`(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})`$, $`\mathbf{x}`$ is the speech
token sequence of the user input, $`\mathbf{z}`$ is the reasoning token
sequence, and
$`\mathbf{y}=[\mathbf{t}_{1}\circ\mathbf{s}_{1}\circ\mathbf{t}_{2}\circ\mathbf{s}_{2},\cdots]`$
is the token sequence for the speech output that interleaves between
$`N_{\text{text}}`$ text tokens ($`\mathbf{t}_{j}`$) and
$`N_{\text{speech}}`$ tokens ($`\mathbf{s}_{j}`$) (The last text token
chunk may be less than $`N_{\text{text}}`$ while the last speech token
span can have more than $`N_{\text{speech}}`$ tokens); $`\circ`$ denotes
concatenating two token sequences. We defer how we construct
$`\mathcal{D}_{\text{TBS}}`$ from existing datasets until
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Training ‣ 4 Experiment Setup ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models").
A figurative illustration of the target output for TBS is in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 A Trivial Method: Think before Speaking in Speech (TBS) ‣ 3 Method: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models")(b).

During training, given an input $`\mathbf{x}`$, we train the SLM to
predict the reasoning tokens $`\mathbf{z}`$ and then the interleaved
text-speech tokens $`\mathbf{y}`$ using the standard cross-entropy loss.

### 3.2 Stitch-R: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Reasoning First

While inserting text reasoning before the speech response in TBS
improves the quality of the speech response, the speech response can
only be generated after the full reasoning has been generated. As later
shown in
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5 Main Results ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models"),
the reasoning by TBS can be long, creating significant latency before
generating the speech response. To resolve the latency issue, we draw
inspiration from the fact that humans can think while speaking and
propose Stitch-R.

Stitch-R realizes this ”thinking when speaking” by alternating
fixed-length ($`N_{\text{reason}}`$) partial reasoning spans,
fixed-length ($`N_{\text{text}}`$) text token spans, and fixed-length
($`N_{\text{speech}}`$) speech token spans. The partial reasoning spans
are for inner thinking, while the text and speech token spans are for
the spoken response. Stitching the partial reasoning spans together will
form a complete CoT reasoning. A figurative illustration of the output
of Stitch-R is shown in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 A Trivial Method: Think before Speaking in Speech (TBS) ‣ 3 Method: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models")(c),
and some samples generated by Stitch-R are shown in
Table [5](#A2.T5 "Table 5 ‣ B.2 Testing Dataset ‣ Appendix B Dataset ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models")
in the Appendix. The ”R” in Stitch-R stands for ”reasoning first” since
it generates a partial reasoning chunk before speaking; this is used to
distinguish the ”speaking first” Stitch-S that will be introduced in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Stitch-S: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Speaking First ‣ 3 Method: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models").

We explain how simultaneous thinking and speaking is achieved by
Stitch-R, which can be most easily understood with
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models").
After the SLM receives the speech input, it first generates
$`N_{\text{reason}}`$ reasoning tokens, $`N_{\text{text}}`$ text tokens,
and $`N_{\text{speech}}`$ speech tokens for the first audio output chunk
$`S_{1}`$. Those speech tokens will be passed through the speech decoder
to synthesize the audio with duration $`t_{\text{chunk}}`$ seconds and
played to the user. When synthesizing the speech waveform and playing
the audio for $`S_{1}`$, the SLM continues to generate the next
$`N_{\text{reason}}`$ reasoning tokens; this is where simultaneous
thinking and speaking happens. After generating the second reasoning
chunk, the model continues to generate $`N_{\text{text}}`$ text tokens,
and $`N_{\text{speech}}`$ speech tokens for the next audio chunk
$`S_{2}`$. As long as the time to generate
$`N_{\text{reason}}+N_{\text{text}}+N_{\text{speech}}`$ tokens, denoted
as $`t_{token}`$, is less than $`t_{\text{chunk}}`$, the next audio
chunk $`S_{2}`$ can be played seamlessly after the previous audio chunk
$`S_{1}`$.

Let’s understand with some concrete numbers. On an A100-80G GPU, our
model generate around 80 tokens per second using vLLM ([Kwon et al.,
2023](#bib.bib44)), and the duration of the audio for
$`N_{\text{speech}}=26`$ speech tokens is around $`t_{\text{chunk}}=2`$
seconds, so we can generate around 160 tokens in $`t_{\text{chunk}}=2`$
seconds. The number of text tokens and speech tokens sums to
$`N_{\text{text}}+N_{\text{speech}}=13+26=39`$, so we have time to
generate at most $`80\times 2-39=121`$ reasoning tokens.¹¹ 1 Since the
token generation and audio synthesis can be run in parallel, we do not
need to consider $`t_{\text{syn}}`$, the time to synthesize the audio,
as long as $`t_{\text{syn}}<t_{\text{chunk}}`$.

Each partial reasoning chunk is enclosed in two special tokens: \[SOPR\]
(start of partial reasoning) and \[EOPR\] (end of partial reasoning). If
the reasoning chunk is fully generated, an additional \[EOR\] is used to
denote the end of the full reasoning CoT.

To construct the training data for Stitch-R, we simply split the full
reasoning CoT $`\mathbf{z}`$ in $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{TBS}}`$ into chunks
with $`N_{\text{reason}}`$ tokens
$`\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2},\cdots\}`$, where each
$`\mathbf{z}_{i}`$ except the last chunk has $`N_{\text{reason}}`$
tokens. Next, we interleave those chunks with the interleaved
text-speech token sequence
$`\mathbf{y}=[\mathbf{t}_{1}\circ\mathbf{s}_{1}\circ\mathbf{t}_{2}\circ\mathbf{s}_{2},\cdots]`$
to create the interleaved data of the form
$`[\mathbf{z}_{1}\circ\mathbf{t}_{1}\circ\mathbf{s}_{1}\circ\mathbf{z}_{2}\circ\mathbf{t}_{2}\circ\mathbf{s}_{2}\circ\cdots]`$,
as shown in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 A Trivial Method: Think before Speaking in Speech (TBS) ‣ 3 Method: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models")(c).
If the number of the reasoning span is more than the number of text
spans, this indicates that the reasoning token spans think slower than
the text token spans, so we remove the sample from the training data.²²
2 The number of text spans is always less than or equal to the number of
speech spans by design. This can be done by properly selecting the ratio
between $`N_{\text{text}}`$ and $`N_{\text{speech}}`$. The model is
fine-tuned to auto-regressively predict the interleaved
reasoning-text-speech token spans using standard language modeling
cross-entropy loss.

### 3.3 Stitch-S: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Speaking First

Stitch-R can have a latency shorter than that of TBS, since Stitch-R
only needs to wait at most $`N_{\text{reason}}`$ tokens before
generating the text and speech tokens. However, compared with the
original text-speech interleaved SLMs that directly generate the text
and speech tokens for the spoken response, Stitch-R still needs to wait
for $`N_{\text{reason}}`$ reasoning tokens.

To fully remove the latency of waiting for the first partial reasoning
span, we propose an alternative generative pipeline that directly starts
to generate the text and speech token chunks and then generates the
first reasoning chunk; the model continues to interleave this generation
pattern. We call this Stitch-S since it generates a speech response
first, and an illustrative figure is shown in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 A Trivial Method: Think before Speaking in Speech (TBS) ‣ 3 Method: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models")(d).

To construct the training data for Stitch-S, we similarly split the full
reasoning z in $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{TBS}}`$ into chunks with
$`N_{\text{reason}}`$ tokens, and insert a text token chunk of length
$`N_{\text{text}}`$ and a speech token chunk of length
$`N_{\text{speech}}`$ before each reasoning span. Following previous
notation, the target output for Stitch-S looks like
$`[\mathbf{t}_{1}\circ\mathbf{s}_{1}\circ\mathbf{z}_{1}\circ\mathbf{t}_{2}\circ\mathbf{s}_{2}\circ\mathbf{z}_{2}\circ\mathbf{t}_{3}\circ\mathbf{s}_{3},\cdots]`$.
If a training instance’s number of reasoning chunks is more than the
number of text chunks, the above data construction pipeline will result
in an instance that has a reasoning span after the text chunks are
finished, which does not make sense since the thinking process hasn’t
ended while the content to be spoken is finished. We remove this kind of
sample from the training dataset.

## 4 Experiment Setup

### 4.1 Training

We fine-tune a separate model for TBS, Stitch-R, and Stitch-S from
GLM-4-Voice-9B ([Zeng et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)). During fine-tuning, we
fully fine-tune the backbone transformer model ([Vaswani et al.,
2017](#bib.bib45)) of GLM-4-Voice and freeze the speech encoder ([Zeng
et al., 2025](#bib.bib46)) and speech decoder ([Du et al.,
2024](#bib.bib47); [Kong et al., 2020](#bib.bib48)). The detailed
hyperparameters for fine-tuning are listed in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Training Details ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models").

We include three types of tasks in the training data: general dialogue
dataset (VoiceAssistant400K ([Xiezhifei, 2024](#bib.bib8))),
mathematical reasoning (Tulu-3-Persona Math, Tulu-3-Persona-Math-Grade,
and Tulu-3-Persona-Algebra ([Lambert et al., 2024](#bib.bib5))), and
knowledge-intensive question answering (Natural Question ([Kwiatkowski
et al., 2019](#bib.bib28)) and TriviaQA ([Joshi et al.,
2017](#bib.bib30))).

We introduce how to construct $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{TBS}}`$, the training
data for TBS, while the training data for Stitch-R and Stitch-S can be
constructed from $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{TBS}}`$ following the procedures
in
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Stitch-R: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Reasoning First ‣ 3 Method: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models")
and
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Stitch-S: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Speaking First ‣ 3 Method: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models").

To train TBS, each sample in the dataset needs to have the spoken query
$`\mathbf{x}`$, text reasoning $`\mathbf{z}`$, and the spoken responses
$`\mathbf{y}`$. For VoiceAssistant400K, which is already a
speech-to-speech dialogue dataset, we generate the text reasoning using
GPT-4o. For mathematical reasoning datasets, which only contain a
question and its long answer in text, we synthesize the question audio
$`\mathbf{x}`$ using GPT-4o-mini-TTS ([OpenAI, 2024b](#bib.bib18)), use
the ground truth long answer as the text CoT $`\mathbf{z}`$, and rewrite
the long answer and synthesize its audio to form the spoken response
$`\mathbf{y}`$. For knowledge-intensive QA datasets, which are also in
text form, we synthesize the question audio $`\mathbf{x}`$ using
GPT-4o-mini-TTS, generate the text CoT $`\mathbf{z}`$ with GPT-4o, and
rewrite the answer and synthesize its audio to form the spoken response
$`\mathbf{y}`$. All the prompts used for constructing the datasets are
in
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Training Dataset ‣ Appendix B Dataset ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models").
The full training dataset contains around 400K instances.

### 4.2 Evaluation

We include reasoning and non-reasoning datasets for evaluation. We use
the former to evaluate whether reasoning before responding in speech can
improve the spoken answer quality for reasoning tasks. We include five
math reasoning datasets: AddSub ([Hosseini et al., 2014](#bib.bib31)),
SinglEq ([Koncel-Kedziorski et al., 2015](#bib.bib34)), MultiArith ([Roy
and Roth, 2015](#bib.bib35)), SVAMP ([Patel et al., 2021](#bib.bib33)),
and GSM8K ([Cobbe et al., 2021](#bib.bib32)), which are commonly used to
evaluate the math reasoning ability of LLMs ([Wei et al.,
2022](#bib.bib19); [Kojima et al., 2022](#bib.bib36); [Wang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib37)). The questions and answers in these datasets are not
too difficult and can be described clearly in speech.Since these
datasets are originally in textual form, we synthesize the question
audio using GPT-4o-mini-TTS.

For non-reasoning datasets, we include three knowledge-intensive QA
datasets (Llama Questions ([Nachmani et al., 2024](#bib.bib29)),
TriviaQA ([Joshi et al., 2017](#bib.bib30)), and WebQuestions ([Berant
et al., 2013](#bib.bib22))) and AlpacaEval ([Li et al.,
2023](#bib.bib24)), a dialogue dataset, from OpenAudioBench ([Li et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib23)). We use these datasets to understand whether
generating unspoken reasoning before responding in speech has any impact
on non-reasoning datasets. The dataset statistics are in Appendix
Table [4](#A2.T4 "Table 4 ‣ B.2 Testing Dataset ‣ Appendix B Dataset ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models").

For all reasoning datasets and knowledge-intensive QA datasets, we
evaluate the SLM’s performance using the accuracy. To calculate the
accuracy, we use GPT-4o to judge whether the SLM’s prediction matches
the ground truth answer. For AlpacaEval, we follow the setting in
OpenAudioBench to use LLM-as-a-judge ([Chiang and Lee,
2023](#bib.bib38); [Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib39); [Zheng et al.,
2023](#bib.bib40)) to rate the response from 1 to 10, and report the
GPT-4o-score scaled to 10 and 100, where higher is better.

When evaluating the responses from SLMs, we use the text tokens for
evaluation. Alternatively, we can synthesize the audio from the speech
tokens, transcribe the audio, and evaluate those transcriptions.
However, since the SLM’s text tokens are designed to align with the
speech output, we only report the performance of the text tokens here.
The general trend observed in the following experiment results holds
when we evaluate the transcription, while the latter has a slight
performance drop of around 3% to 6%, as shown in
Table [7](#A6.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Customized Attention Mask ‣ Appendix F Unsuccessful Attempts ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models")
in the Appendix.

### 4.3 Compared Models

We compare six models, which are based on the same base model:
GLM-4-Voice, an interleaved SLM. GLM-4-Voice has an
$`N_{\text{text}}=13`$ and $`N_{\text{speech}}=26`$. All the models we
use follow the $`N_{\text{text}}`$ and $`N_{\text{speech}}`$ in
GLM-4-Voice.

\(1\) GLM-4-Voice: The model takes speech input and generates
interleaved text-speech tokens. This model cannot generate unspoken
reasoning by design.  (2) No reasoning: We fine-tune GLM-4-Voice on a
dataset without reasoning, $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{no-reason}}`$. Each
instance in $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{no-reason}}`$ has the form
$`(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})`$, which is constructed from
$`\mathcal{D}_{\text{TBS}}`$ by removing the text reasoning
$`\mathbf{z}`$.  The model is trained to predict interleaved text-speech
tokens $`\mathbf{y}`$ based on the input $`\mathbf{x}`$.  (3) TBS: see
Section
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 A Trivial Method: Think before Speaking in Speech (TBS) ‣ 3 Method: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models"). (4)
Mix reasoning: We mix half of $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{no-reason}}`$ and
half of $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{TBS}}`$ to fine-tune GLM-4-Voice. We use a
system prompt to control whether the model should generate reasoning
before generating the interleaved text-speech tokens. This allows us to
control the model’s behavior during inference.  By comparing the
performance of this model when inference with and without reasoning, we
can understand if unspoken reasoning really helps SLM’s speech
output. (5) Stitch-R: see Section
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Stitch-R: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Reasoning First ‣ 3 Method: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models"). (6)
Stitch-S: see Section

We summarize several key observations as follows:

TBS significantly outperforms the baselines without reasoning on math
QA. In row 4, TBS achieves an average accuracy of 79.12%, outperforming
GLM-4-Voice (row 1) by 26.04% and the ”no reasoning” model (row 2) by
16.14%. Most significantly, the accuracy of TBS almost doubles compared

we found that the TBS outperforms GLM-4-Voice and the no-reasoning
baselines in a statistically significant way, with $`p`$-value less than

respond better to questions that require reasoning.

Reasoning before speaking is better than no reasoning before speaking,
even if the training data is the same. The strong result of TBS,
compared with the ”no reasoning” baseline, may raise a question on
whether TBS is winning because the training data includes additional
reasoning $`\mathbf{z}`$, which are not included in the training data
for the ”no reasoning” baseline. This question can be answered by
comparing the results of the ”mix reasoning” model with and without text
reasoning before generating the speech response (row 3 vs. row 5). Since
the two different inference modes use the same mix reasoning model, the
performance difference can be attributed to the reasoning before the
spoken response during inference. On average, reasoning before speaking
(row 5) obtains an accuracy of 77.47%, outperforming not using reasoning
by more than 10% (row 3). For the most difficult GSM8K, the improvement
of using reasoning is 24.9%. The results again confirm the importance of
generating reasoning before the speech response for reasoning tasks.

Stitch-R (row 6) shows almost no performance drop on average. Stitch-R
alternates between partial text reasoning and text-speech tokens. While
the reasoning is split by text-speech tokens, the average performance
only drops by 0.42% compared to TBS. In
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5 Main Results ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models"),
we report the average number of reasoning tokens on three testing
datasets. Both TBS and Stitch-R generate 150 to 300 reasoning tokens for
math reasoning datasets on average, but Stitch-R only needs to wait for
the first $`N_{token}=100`$ reasoning tokens before starting to generate
the first chunk of the speech output, yielding a smaller latency for
Stitch-R. Importantly, this speedup comes with almost no loss in
accuracy. Still, on a more difficult dataset (GSM8K), the performance
drop is larger, but it still significantly outperforms the non-reasoning
baselines (row 1 and row 2). Again, using McNemar’s test, we find that
the STITCH-R outperforms the no-reasoning baselines in a statistically
significant way. We discuss two samples from STITCH-R in
Section [6](#S6 "6 Qualitative Results ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models").

Stitch-S shows almost no performance drop on average without adding any
inference latency. Remarkably, Stitch-S shows an average accuracy of
78.04% (row 7), slightly lower than TBS by 1.08% (row 4), but is still
much better than the ”no reasoning” baseline by 15.06% (row 2). We see a
larger performance drop in GSM8K around 8% compared with TBS, but again,
still significantly outperforms no reasoning baseline by 19.99%, again
in a statistically significant way based on McNemar’s test. Recall that
the first packet latency of Stitch-S is exactly the same as the
baselines that do not generate text reasoning; both of them only need to
wait for $`N_{\text{text}}+N_{\text{speech}}`$ tokens before
synthesizing the speech waveform. This exciting result indicates that we
can improve the answer quality without sacrificing any inference
latency. By scrutinizing the responses from Stitch-S, we find that the
first text token chunk is often a rephrase of the question, which can be
generated without any reasoning. This explains why Stitch-S does not
show a severe accuracy drop compared with Stitch-R.

On non-reasoning datasets, reasoning models perform comparably well or
slightly outperform non-reasoning
baselines. Table [1(b)](#S4.T1.st2 "In Table 1 ‣ 4.3 Compared Models ‣ 4 Experiment Setup ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models")
shows the performance on non-reasoning datasets. We find that TBS (row
4), Stitch-R (row 6), and Stitch-S (row 7) all have performance
comparable to or better than the GLM-4-Voice (row 1) and the no
reasoning baseline (row 2). This shows that fine-tuning SLMs to think in
text before responding does not harm the performance on non-reasoning
datasets.

Generating text reasoning before generating the speech responses does
not harm the perceptual quality and the fluency of the speech
response. In the rightmost block in
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5 Main Results ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models"),
we report some quality assessment of the responses generated by the
SLMs. We report (1) the perceptual quality of the speech response
evaluated by UTMOSv2 ([Baba et al., 2024](#bib.bib41)), an automatic MOS
evaluation model, and (2) the fluency score of the text tokens judged by
GPT-4o ([Chiang and Lee, 2023](#bib.bib38)). The fluency score focuses
on whether the text tokens form a fluent narrative. Both metrics range
from 1 to 5, with 5 being the best. We observe that all fine-tuned
models have a similar UTMOSv2 score over 3, similar to the original
GLM-4-Voice, indicating that our fine-tuning does not harm the
perceptual quality of the speech output. We also observe that the
fluency scores for Stitch-R and Stitch-S are high, indicating that
alternating between the text reasoning token spans and the text-speech
token spans does not harm the fluency of the text tokens.

Human evaluation shows that STITCH-S is more responsive than STITCH-R,
followed by TBS. We conduct a human evaluation on the responsiveness of
different models. We present the human evaluator with a question from
the user and two audio responses from different models, asking them to
compare the responsiveness of the two models on a seven-point scale,
where +3 indicates that model A is more responsive than model B and -3
indicates that model B is more responsive than model A. To simulate the
first packet latency that the user may experience, each audio includes a
silence in the beginning, accounting for the prefilling and decoding
time of the SLM and the speech synthesis latency. We compare the
following pairs: TBS / STITCH-R, TBS / STITCH-S, STITCH-R / STITCH-S,
and STITCH / no-reasoning. For each pair, we use the same 100 questions
from GSM8K, and we recruit 3 human evaluators to judge the
responsiveness of a pair of models. The results are presented in
Table [6](#A4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Appendix D Human Evaluation ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models")
in the Appendix, and we summarize the results as follows: 1. STICH-R and
STITCH-S are both more responsive than TBS. 2. STITCH-S is more
responsive than STITCH-R. 3. STITCH-S and no-reasoning are equally
responsive.

The reasoning segments finish much earlier than the text segments. Based
on the statistics in
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5 Main Results ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models"),
we can see that the average number of reasoning chunks are less than the
number of text chunks. For example, on GSM8K, STITCH-R has on average
$`322.40/100=3.22`$ reasoning chunks, while the average number of text
chunks is $`74.36/13=5.72`$. During inference time, we do not find the
interleaved text-speech chunks to end before the reasoning chunks
finish, i.e., before the EOR is emitted. This is mainly because when we
construct the training data for STITCH-R and STITCH, we remove the
samples whose reasoning chunks are more than the text chunks, i.e.,
those samples that have reasoning slower than the response. As a result,
after training, the model will learn that it must finish the reasoning
before concluding its spoken response.

## 7 Adjusting the Length of the Reasoning Chunk During Inference

During training, we fix $`N_{token}`$, the number of tokens in each
partial reasoning chunk, to 100 tokens. In this section, we ask whether
we can adjust $`N^{\prime}_{token}`$ at test time without retraining the
model. One might want to adjust $`N^{\prime}_{token}`$ at test time if
the hardware is slower and the time to generate
$`N_{token}+N_{\text{text}}+N_{\text{speech}}`$ tokens may exceed the
duration $`t_{\text{chunk}}`$ of the audio for $`N_{\text{speech}}`$
tokens. In this case, the model deployer may want to reduce
$`N^{\prime}_{token}`$ during inference time to ensure that generating
the $`N^{\prime}_{token}+N_{\text{text}}+N_{\text{speech}}`$ does not
exceed the duration $`t_{\text{chunk}}`$. Thus, it would be interesting
to see if we can train a single model with a fixed $`N_{token}`$ and
vary $`N^{\prime}_{token}`$ during inference.

Recall that each reasoning chunk ends with \[EOPR\]. To adjust the
length of the reasoning chunk to $`N^{\prime}_{token}\leq N_{token}`$
during the inference time, we can append \[EOPR\] after
$`N^{\prime}_{token}`$ reasoning tokens are generated. Once the model
sees \[EOPR\], it will cease generating the reasoning tokens and switch
to generating text-speech interleaved tokens. We intervene in the
model’s generation by the above process whenever the model generates a
reasoning chunk until the \[EOR\] token is generated.

We vary the number of $`N^{\prime}_{token}`$ from 60 to 100 with a 10
increment on the math reasoning datasets for Stitch-R and Stitch-S. The
results are shown in Appendix
Figure [3(a)](#A1.F3.sf1 "In Figure 3 ‣ Nov. 19th ‣ Appendix A Change Log ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models")
and
[3(b)](#A1.F3.sf2 "In Figure 3 ‣ Nov. 19th ‣ Appendix A Change Log ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models");
we summarize the main findings below. We see that the accuracy on all
tasks mostly rises when increasing $`N^{\prime}_{token}`$ from 60 to 100. While the performance of $`N^{\prime}_{token}<100`$ is mostly worse
than the performance of $`N_{token}=100`$, using
$`N^{\prime}_{token}<100`$ is still better than the ”no reasoning”
baseline when $`N^{\prime}_{token}\geq 70`$. We also observe that when
$`N^{\prime}_{token}\geq 80`$ tokens, the accuracy recovers to 90% of
the accuracy of $`N_{token}=100`$. The results indicate that the model
deployer can control the number of $`N^{\prime}_{token}`$ in a
reasonable range during inference without retraining the model with some
accuracy trade-off.

## 8 Using Reasoning from Other Models

Previously, we showed that Stitch-R and Stitch-S can generate reasoning
before responding in speech. Here, we discuss whether we can improve the
speech response by using the reasoning from other models. The motivation
is that sometimes the reasoning generated by the SLM itself is not good
enough or takes too long to generate, and we may use other stronger or
smaller models to generate the reasoning. We call the model used to
generate the reasoning the ”reasoning augmentation model”.

We discuss how to improve Stitch-R by using a reasoning augmentation
model. Given a user input speech, we first use an ASR model to
transcribe the audio and feed the transcription into the reasoning
augmentation model to generate a response, which will be used as the
reasoning for Stitch-R. Next, we crop the reasoning into chunks of
$`N^{\prime}_{token}`$ enclosed by \[SOPR\] and \[EOPR\], and then feed
the speech input and the first reasoning chunk to Stitch-R and generate
the first text-speech interleaved token span. After the first
text-speech interleaved tokens are generated, we append the second
reasoning chunk to the input and generate the second text-speech
interleaved token spans. We continue the above process to generate the
full response.

In the following experiment, we replace the text reasoning of Stitch-R
using four models: GLM-4-9B-Chat ([GLM et al., 2024](#bib.bib42)),
Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.2-3B, and Llama-3.2-1B ([Grattafiori et al.,
2024](#bib.bib43)). The results are shown in
Figure [3(c)](#A1.F3.sf3 "In Figure 3 ‣ Nov. 19th ‣ Appendix A Change Log ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models")
in the Appendix, and we summarize the main findings below. In general,
stronger reasoning augmentation models (GLM-4-9B-Chat and larger Llama
models) can improve the accuracy of the spoken response, while weaker
reasoning augmentation model like Llama-3.2-1B worsens the performance
of Stitch-R. This shows that Stitch-R can incorporate the reasoning
ability of stronger models to improve the spoken answer. The experiment
also shows that Stitch-R really considers the contents in the reasoning
to form the spoken response, instead of independently generating the
reasoning and not using that reasoning in the spoken responses.

## 9 Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce Stitch, a generation framework for SLM that
enables simultaneous thinking and speaking. We achieve simultaneous
thinking and speaking by using the long duration of an audio chunk to
generate additional reasoning tokens before generating the speech tokens
for the next audio chunk. By design, Stitch-S induces no additional
latency compared to the models that cannot generate reasoning before
they speak. On five math reasoning QA datasets, we show that Stitch-R
and Stitch-S significantly improve the accuracy over non-reasoning
baselines by over 15%. On non-reasoning datasets, Stitch-R and Stitch-S
perform comparably well with non-reasoning baselines. Overall, the low
latency and high performance of Stitch show a promising future direction
for spoken language models.

## Appendix B Dataset

### B.1 Training Dataset

We use three types of training data: dialogue datasets, math reasoning
datasets, and knowledge-intensive QA datasets. The sizes of the datasets
are in
Table [3](#A2.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Knowledge-Intensive Datasets ‣ B.1 Training Dataset ‣ Appendix B Dataset ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models").
We explain how we construct our training datasets from those existing
datasets as follows.

#### Dialogue Datasets

VoiceAssistant400K ([Xiezhifei, 2024](#bib.bib8)) is a spoken dialogue
dataset built from a text dialogue dataset, including
OpenAssistant ([Köpf et al., 2023](#bib.bib53)) and HH-RLHF ([Bai et
al., 2022](#bib.bib55)). We subsample the dataset by filtering out (1)
the instances whose ‘round‘ attribute is None or 0, (2) the instances
from the subset identity, and (3) the instances whose response audio is
less than 2 seconds. Given a user query and the response or answer from
the dataset, we use GPT-4o to generate a text reasoning given some
in-context samples. GPT-4o is instructed to generate CoTs that
understand the user’s intention, recall relevant information, and
provide the final answer. The prompt we use is shown in
Table [13](#A6.T13 "Table 13 ‣ Customized Attention Mask ‣ Appendix F Unsuccessful Attempts ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models").

#### Math Reasoning Datasets

We include three math reasoning datasets for fine-tuning: Tulu-3-Persona
Math, Tulu-3-Persona-Math-Grade, and Tulu-3-Persona-Algebra. The
datasets include the question and the CoT answer in text. We directly
use the ground truth CoT answer as the text reasoning. To obtain the
spoken answer, we prompt GPT-4o to rewrite the answer such that the
answer is suitable for speech communication and then synthesize it with
GPT-4o-mini-TTS. The voice is sampled from the following list: alloy,
ash, ballad, coral, echo. The prompt used to rewrite the spoken answer
is shown in
Table [10](#A6.T10 "Table 10 ‣ Customized Attention Mask ‣ Appendix F Unsuccessful Attempts ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models").
The question audio is also synthesized with GPT-4o-mini-TTS, while the
list of voices we sampled from is the same as the previous. The voice of
the spoken question and response is selected randomly and independently.
The total size of the math reasoning datasets used for fine-tuning is
220K.

#### Knowledge-Intensive Datasets

We include two knowledge-intensive QA datasets, Natural Question
(NQ) ([Kwiatkowski et al., 2019](#bib.bib28)) and TriviaQA (TQA) ([Joshi
et al., 2017](#bib.bib30)), to preserve the SLM’s ability to respond to
knowledge-seeking questions. These two datasets are text-based datasets.
To obtain the dataset for fine-tuning, we rewrite the ground truth
answer from the dataset to make it more suitable for spoken
communication, and then synthesize the question and answer audio using
GPT-4o-mini-TTS. We sample from the same subset of voices randomly. The
text reasoning is also generated by GPT-4o by prompting it with the
question and ground truth answer, and asking it to generate an inner
monologue that understands the user’s intention, recalls relevant
information, and provides the final answer. The prompts used for
rewriting the answer and generating the reasoning are shown in
Table [12](#A6.T12 "Table 12 ‣ Customized Attention Mask ‣ Appendix F Unsuccessful Attempts ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models")
and
Table [11](#A6.T11 "Table 11 ‣ Customized Attention Mask ‣ Appendix F Unsuccessful Attempts ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models"),
respectively. When subsampling from NQ and TQA, we simply take the first
50K instances from NQ and the first 20K instances from TQA without
shuffling the dataset before subsampling. The size of
knowledge-intensive datasets is around 70K instances.

STITCH-R vs TBS, (2) STITCH-S vs TBS, (3) STITCH-R vs STITCH-S, and (4)
STITCH-S vs no-reasoning. We select the above model pairs because we
believe they can form a partial order of the responsiveness of the four
models.

We generate the responses of each model from the same 100 questions from
GSM8K; the reason for selecting GSM8K is that the number of reasoning
tokens required for this dataset is longer, as shown in
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5 Main Results ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models"),
which may make the difference of responsiveness among the models more
observable. For each audio, we insert a silence to represent the first
packet latency. The first packet latency considers the prefilling and
decoding time for the SLM, and the speech synthesis latency of the
speech decoder. The prefilling and decoding latency is profiled using an
A100 GPU with vLLM, while the speech synthesis latency is calculated
based on the reported latency from Cosyvoice.

The human evaluators are given a question in GSM8K and two audios from a
pair of models, and they are asked to judge the responsiveness of the
two models on a 7-point comparison category rating, following the
P.808 ([Rec, 2018](#bib.bib1)), using the following rubrics:

- +3
  : A is more responsive than B
- +2
  : A is somewhat responsive than B
- +1
  : A is slightly responsive than B
- 0
  : A and B are equally responsive
- -1
  : B is slightly responsive than A
- -2
  : B is somewhat responsive than A
- -3
  : B is more responsive than A

We use Amazon MTurk (AMT) to recruit the human evaluators, and each pair
of comparisons is rated by three annotators. We require the annotators
to have a HIT approval rate $`\geq`$ 98 and to hold a master
qualification on AMT. We also only include participants from the US, UK,
Australia, and New Zealand in this study. The interface for the human
evaluation is shown in
Figure [4](#A4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Appendix D Human Evaluation ‣ Stitch: Simultaneous Thinking and Talking with Chunked Reasoning for Spoken Language Models").
Human evaluators are paid \$1.70 for rating 8 pairs of responses, and we
estimate that the hourly rate is roughly \$12.75.

## Appendix E Detailed Latency Calculation

In the main content, we assume that the duration of each audio chunk
$`t_{\text{chunk}}`$ synthesized from $`N_{\text{speech}}`$ tokens is
the same, and we use $`t_{\text{chunk}}=2`$ seconds in the paper. This
is a simplification to make the discussion easier. In fact, for the
first chunk of 26 speech tokens, the corresponding audio is about 1.6
seconds, while the later audio chunks all have a duration of about 2
seconds. As a result, a safer $`N_{\text{reason}}`$ on an A100-80G GPU,
with token per second around 86, should be
$`86\times 1.6-(13+26)=98.6`$, which is still around 100, the number we
use in our paper. It is also possible to make only the first reasoning
chunk shorter while all the later reasoning chunks have
$`N_{\text{reason}}=100`$. If the model is deployed on H100-80G GPU, the
token per second can be as high as 123, the maximum number for
$`N_{\text{token}}`$ can be $`123\times 1.6-(13+26)=157.8`$. Using
speculative decoding ([Leviathan et al., 2023](#bib.bib58)) to speed up
and increase the number of reasoning tokens for each chunk is also a
valid option.

## Appendix F Unsuccessful Attempts

We detail the unsuccessful attempts and negative findings observed in
our experiments. Our aim is to guide future research by highlighting
these pitfalls so that others can avoid them.

#### LoRA Fine-tuning

Initially, we tried to use LoRA ([Hu et al., 2022](#bib.bib54)) for
fine-tuning. However, when fine-tuning TBS using LoRA, we found that the
resulting model cannot generate useful reasoning. After fine-tuning, the
performance on GSM8K is still around 35%, which is almost the same as
the accuracy of GLM-4-Voice. By observing the outputs from the
fine-tuned model, we observed that the model can generate outputs that
satisfy the output format in the training data, i.e., reasoning and then
interleaved text-speech tokens. However, the reasoning is seldom correct
and can make very trivial mistakes. We suspect that this is because
teaching GLM-4-Voice mathematical reasoning requires significant model
parameter updates, making LoRA fine-tuning a bad choice.

#### Fine-tuning only on Math Datasets

We have tried to fine-tune only on the math datasets to teach the model
reasoning. However, we found that the resulting model will treat every
input query as a math question and list some equations to solve the
question, even if the question simply asks ”Which city is located at the
intersection of the Tigris and Euphrates rivers?” This is possibly
because the model overfits to the pattern in the training data and
forgets how to solve other non-math questions. However, this can be
easily solved by including diverse types of tasks in the training data,
which is what we do in the end.

#### Customized Attention Mask

When training the Stitch-R model, we tried with a variant with
customized attention masks. Precisely, we make the reasoning tokens
unable to attend to the text tokens and speech tokens, while the text
and speech tokens can attend to all types of tokens. The attention mask
still remains causal. The rationale for this customized attention mask
is to make the reasoning tokens feel like there is no gap between two
chunks of reasoning tokens, since they cannot see the text and speech
tokens that separate two chunks of reasoning tokens. We use the
customized attention mask during training and inference. However, we
found that the results on the math reasoning datasets are similar to the
results of not using a customized attention mask. Considering that the
customized attention mask requires some customization during training,
complicates the inference procedure, and does not improve the
performance, we do not recommend this configuration.
