# From Speech Tokens to Stolen Voices: Attacker-Chosen-Text Speaker Impersonation via Representation Inversion

Ye Lu, Shen Wang, Runze Liu, Zhitao Ou, Haonan Huang, Yihan Yan, Fanghui
Sun, and Zhaoyang Zhang Affiliation: School of Cyberspace Science,
Harbin Institute of Technology Affiliation: Corresponding author:
Zhaoyang Zhang (zhaoyang_zhang@hit.edu.cn)

###### Abstract

Speech interaction systems have gradually evolved from traditional
cascaded systems that use text as intermediate information to end-to-end
speech language models that directly process speech representations. In
some applications, the speech frontend runs on the user’s device and
sends speech representations to a remote server for inference, while the
original audio remains local. However, these representations may still
contain speaker identity information. If an attacker obtains them, it
may use this information to imitate the user’s voice, threatening their
voice privacy. To investigate this risk, we propose STIA, a Speech Token
Inversion Attack that generates speech with attacker-specified text in a
victim’s voice using a single short leaked representation, without
access to the original audio or its transcript. Specifically, STIA
converts discrete, continuous, or mixed speech representations into
conditioning sequences and trains a conditional flow-matching speech
generation model using pairs of different utterances from the same
speaker in public speech datasets. The model combines speaker
characteristics contained in the leaked representation with the
specified text to generate speech in the victim’s voice. Experiments
show that, with a Moshi representation leak corresponding to 3 seconds
of speech, STIA achieves an average CAM++ speaker cosine similarity of
0.78 and a target-text word error rate of 3.80%. These results show that
speech frontend representations reveal reusable speaker identity
information and highlight the need to consider privacy risks when
designing speech frontend representation methods.

###### Index Terms: 

speech privacy, model inversion attacks, speaker impersonation, privacy
and security of speech language models

## I Introduction

End-to-end speech language models increasingly use speech-native
representations during inference, rather than relying on a cascaded
pipeline that uses text as the intermediate modality between automatic
speech recognition (ASR), a large language model (LLM), and
text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis. These models typically use a speech
frontend to convert audio into acoustic tokens, continuous features, or
both, preserving emotional cues, intonation, and other prosodic
information that text alone cannot fully convey \[1, 2, 3\]. In a split
deployment, user audio can be converted locally into these
representations, which are then sent to a remote server for processing,
while raw waveforms remain on the user’s device. This deployment may
create the misleading impression that transmitting frontend
representations rather than raw waveforms is sufficient to protect
speaker privacy.

However, prior work has shown that frontend encoding does not remove all
speaker information. SpInv recovers speaker embeddings, which are vector
representations used to compare speaker identities, from
speech-language-model frontend representations \[4\]. Its results
establish that identity information can remain exposed through frontend
representations even when the original waveform is unavailable. Keeping
raw audio local is therefore insufficient, by itself, to protect speaker
privacy.

Speaker-embedding recovery leaves an unresolved security question: can
an attacker reuse the identity information retained in frontend
representations to generate new content in the victim’s voice? Embedding
recovery alone does not establish whether a leaked representation
supports speech generation that both preserves the speaker’s vocal
characteristics and accurately conveys chosen text. Existing
voice-cloning methods can synthesize speech for new text from short
reference recordings, but typically rely on access to the speaker’s
audio \[5, 6, 7\]. We therefore investigate whether an attacker with
only fragmented frontend representations of the victim’s speech, and no
original audio, can generate speech that preserves the victim’s vocal
characteristics and conveys attacker-chosen text unrelated to the source
utterance.

Fig. 1: Split-deployment setting and STIA attack overview. The attacker
combines leaked frontend representations with chosen text to generate
speech; the boxes on the right state the attack objectives.

To investigate speaker impersonation from frontend representations, we
propose STIA, a Speech Token Inversion Attack. Figure 1 places the
attack in the split deployment we consider: the victim’s device converts
speech into frontend representations for a remote server, while
retaining the raw waveform locally. We assume that the attacker can read
only the frontend representation of a segment of one victim utterance,
rather than the full-utterance representation. Neither the victim’s
waveform nor the source transcript is available. STIA combines the
leaked fragmented representation with independently chosen text to
generate a new speech waveform. The objectives on the right describe the
intended impersonation capability: the generated speech should preserve
the victim’s vocal identity, convey the chosen text, and sound natural.

The central idea behind STIA is to learn to reuse vocal cues retained in
fragmented frontend representations to generate speech with new textual
content. During training, we use a public auxiliary speech dataset to
learn a conditional flow-matching model in a frozen audio codec’s
continuous latent space. Each training example pairs a source
representation fragment with target text and speech from a different
utterance by the same speaker. Specifically, a leak conditioning encoder
maps discrete, continuous, or mixed representation fragments obtained
through black-box frontend queries into conditioning sequences. A
trainable text projection maps the outputs of a frozen multilingual text
encoder to text-condition sequences of the same width. Both conditions
feed a Transformer shared between denoising and decoding modes. The
network learns a transformation from noise to target speech latents
through conditional flow matching, while codec-aware decoding
supervision aligns predicted latents with the target speech’s discrete
codes \[8, 9\]. At attack time, the trained model conditions on the
victim’s leaked representation fragment and attacker-chosen text to
iteratively generate speech latents from Gaussian noise. A final latent
decoding step is followed by quantization and waveform synthesis through
the frozen codec to produce the generated audio. No victim-specific
training or adaptation is required.

For evaluation, we select the frontends of three representative speech
language models (Moshi, Kimi-Audio, and Qwen3-Omni) covering discrete,
mixed, and continuous representations, respectively. We use speakers
unseen during attack training and assess vocal similarity against
genuine recordings distinct from the leakage source. We construct
SpInv+TTS as a two-stage baseline that recovers speaker embeddings
before synthesizing the chosen text with SpeechT5 \[10\]. Across all
three frontends, STIA achieves higher mean speaker similarity under both
CAM++ and ECAPA and lower mean target-text word error rate (WER), while
mean UTMOS scores for predicted naturalness remain similar to those of
the baseline. For a Moshi representation leak corresponding to 3 seconds
of speech, STIA obtains a mean CAM++ cosine similarity of 0.78 and a
mean WER of 3.80%. These results show that, under the evaluated
conditions, fragmented frontend representations can be used to generate
speech that retains a victim’s vocal characteristics and conveys new,
attacker-chosen content, creating risks of voice impersonation and
leakage of private speaker information.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

- •
  To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to study arbitrary-text
  speaker impersonation from leaked frontend representations of
  end-to-end speech language models. The only victim-specific input
  required is a short leaked representation fragment. The attacker can
  choose arbitrary target text independently of the source utterance,
  without access to the victim’s waveform or source transcript.
- •
  We propose STIA, which generates speech directly from leaked frontend
  representation fragments and attacker-chosen text. It accommodates
  discrete, continuous, and mixed representations, learns reusable vocal
  cues through same-speaker, cross-utterance training on public
  auxiliary speech, and combines conditional flow matching with
  codec-aware decoding supervision for synthesis, without
  victim-specific training data or a separate speaker-embedding recovery
  stage.
- •
  We conduct extensive and detailed experimental evaluations on the
  frontends of three representative target models. The results show that
  STIA effectively exploits representation fragments to generate speech
  that preserves victims’ vocal characteristics and conveys chosen text,
  outperforming the SpInv+TTS baseline method on both speaker-similarity
  metrics and mean target-text WER. We further examine the effects of
  leakage duration, auxiliary-data volume, model scale, and leakage and
  text conditioning, and test McAdams anonymization against a fixed
  attacker.

## II Related Work

### II-A Speech Representations and Speech–Language Interfaces

Speech interfaces have progressed from task-specific acoustic features
to self-supervised representations and, subsequently, to discrete or
continuous interfaces consumed by speech language models. The
wav2vec 2.0 contrastive objective uses quantized latent targets, whereas
HuBERT learns by masked prediction of clustered hidden units; both
provide reusable frontends \[11, 12\]. Recent systems connect such
representations to generative language models: SpeechGPT uses discrete
speech units for cross-modal conversation, Spectron directly models
spectrograms for spoken question answering and continuation, and Moshi
supports streaming full-duplex speech–text interaction \[13, 14, 1\].
Such systems may expose token sequences or hidden states at module
boundaries, caches, or logs. We study the security consequence of this
exposure: whether a finite representation fragment retains speaker
information reusable for generation without access to the source
waveform.

### II-B Model Inversion and Privacy of Intermediate Representations

Early model inversion examined how an adversary could recover training
inputs or sensitive features through a model interface. Work by
Fredrikson et al. exploited confidence outputs, while Zhang et al. later
introduced generative priors for inversion of deep networks \[15, 16\].
Recent studies show that intermediate representations are also at risk:
text embeddings can be inverted to recover source text, including in
multilingual settings \[17, 18\], while VoxGuard and HearSay broaden
systematic privacy evaluation for speech and audio language models \[19,
20\].

For vocal identity, Pizzi et al. reconstruct audio and intermediate
voice features from automatic speaker-recognition models and investigate
impersonation \[21\]. SpInv targets speech-language-model frontend
representations, inverting them into embeddings in an attacker-specified
speaker-encoder space \[4\]. This objective recovers identity encodings
rather than a generative representation that retains all timbral and
prosodic detail, potentially limiting voice fidelity when combined with
TTS. We adapt SpInv to this task by retraining it in the embedding space
required by SpeechT5 to construct a two-stage baseline; STIA instead
generates speech directly from leaked representations and target text.

### II-C Speaker Recognition and Voice Anonymization

Speaker recognition evolved from generative identity models to compact
embeddings and deep discriminative frontends. Classic GMM–UBM systems
used adapted speaker models, i-vectors compressed speaker and channel
variability into a low-dimensional total-variability space, and
x-vectors subsequently learned utterance-level embeddings with deep
networks \[22, 23, 24\]. Recent architectures such as ECAPA-TDNN and
CAM++ strengthen temporal aggregation and channel or context modeling
for speaker verification \[25, 26\]. In parallel, voice anonymization
seeks to suppress identity while retaining linguistic content:
VoicePrivacy established common threat models and evaluation protocols,
with approaches including learned pseudo-speaker mappings and signal
transformations based on the McAdams coefficient \[27, 28\]. These
defenses primarily target identification or linkability; we test whether
representations exposed after attacker-unknown McAdams anonymization
still condition cross-content synthesis by the fixed STIA model.

### II-D Zero-Shot Voice Cloning and Audio Deepfakes

Few-shot multispeaker synthesis moved voice cloning from per-speaker
training to inferring a speaker condition from a short reference
recording. Classic work by Arik et al. compared few-shot speaker
adaptation and speaker encoding, while Jia et al. transferred
speaker-verification embeddings to multispeaker text-to-speech
synthesis \[29, 5\]. More recently, YourTTS enabled zero-shot
multispeaker and cross-lingual synthesis, and VALL-E cast zero-shot TTS
as neural audio-codec language modeling \[6, 7\].

Recent flow-matching methods further advance zero-shot speech
generation. Voicebox supports multilingual generation through
text-guided speech infilling; E2 TTS simplifies non-autoregressive
synthesis, and F5-TTS improves flow-matching TTS robustness through
refined text modeling and sampling \[30, 31, 32\]. These methods
typically derive voice conditions from reference audio and do not
natively accept arbitrary leaked representations from different
black-box frontends. STIA studies how to use such representations
directly, without the victim’s waveform or source transcript. ASVspoof
addresses the complementary detection problem of distinguishing genuine
speech from synthetic, converted, or replayed speech \[33\].

## III Threat Model and Motivation

### III-A Threat Model

#### III-A1 Target model

We consider a hypothetical split deployment of an open-source speech
language model: the user device runs the speech frontend $`T`$, and the
remote service runs the speech backend $`G`$. This setting keeps raw
waveforms local instead of uploading audio directly, allowing us to
study the privacy risk of exposing frontend representations. Given user
speech $`x`$, the frontend produces $`R=T(x)`$. The backend combines
this representation with textual, system, or session context $`p`$
available to the service. Denoting the complete model by $`M`$, we have

```math
M(x,p)=G(R,p),\qquad R=T(x). \tag{1}
```

Thus, the client must make $`R`$ available to the service, while the
source waveform $`x`$ need not leave the device.

We write the frontend representation as
$`R=(R^{\mathrm{tok}},R^{\mathrm{feat}})`$. The component
$`R^{\mathrm{tok}}`$ contains one or more ordered token streams whose
elements index tokenizer vocabularies or codebooks, while
$`R^{\mathrm{feat}}`$ contains one or more ordered streams of feature
frames. Either component may be empty, but not both, and each stream
retains its own temporal order and native frame rate. Besides linguistic
content needed by the backend, these representations may retain prosody,
speaker characteristics, and channel conditions. The input segment
$`x_{I}`$ spans a finite source-time interval
$`I=[t_{0},t_{0}+\Delta)`$, where $`t_{0}`$ is the start time and
$`\Delta`$ is the segment duration. The adversary intercepts only the
token and feature streams produced when the frontend processes this
segment. We denote this fragmented frontend representation by

```math
R_{I}^{\mathrm{frag}}(x)=T(x_{I})=\bigl(R_{I}^{\mathrm{tok}}(x),R_{I}^{\mathrm{feat}}(x)\bigr). \tag{2}
```

Here, $`R_{I}^{\mathrm{tok}}(x)`$ and $`R_{I}^{\mathrm{feat}}(x)`$ are
the token and feature streams produced from $`x_{I}`$, respectively. The
fragment retains the visible streams and their temporal order but is not
the full-utterance representation $`R`$.

#### III-A2 Adversary knowledge and capabilities

During one victim interaction, the adversary can read the fragmented
representation sent by the frontend, but cannot obtain its source
waveform or transcript, arbitrary user-device memory, or service-side
backend states. Read access may arise from interception of readable
representations at the client–service interface, a compromised endpoint,
an honest-but-curious service that legitimately receives the same
representation, or a third-party large-model gateway or relay that
terminates the user connection and forwards requests to a model
provider. The adversary also has black-box access to the same frontend
$`T`$: it may submit chosen audio and observe the corresponding frontend
representation, but cannot read or modify the parameters of $`T`$. It
additionally possesses a public auxiliary speech corpus with transcripts
and may use its speaker metadata to train an attack model offline on
different utterances from the same auxiliary speaker. Victim speech,
identity labels, and victim-specific training data are unavailable.

Formally, let $`v`$ denote the victim and let $`x_{v}^{r}`$ denote the
victim utterance that produced the observed representation, where
superscript $`r`$ marks the representation source. The adversary
observes $`R_{I}^{\mathrm{frag}}(x_{v}^{r})`$ during that interaction,
but not $`x_{v}^{r}`$ itself or its transcript. It has no
victim-specific training data or parameters of $`G`$ and does not use
the evaluation models for training or output selection. Its resources
comprise frontend queries, public auxiliary data, a finite leakage
duration, and attack-model capacity and training compute.

#### III-A3 Adversary objective

An attack trial for victim $`v`$ additionally contains an enrollment
utterance $`x_{v}^{e}`$ disjoint from $`x_{v}^{r}`$ and available only
to the experimenter, where superscript $`e`$ denotes enrollment, and
attacker-chosen target text $`y`$ independent of the source content.
Given the fragmented representation and target text, an attack algorithm
$`A`$ generates $`\hat{x}=A(R_{I}^{\mathrm{frag}}(x_{v}^{r}),y)`$. The
attack aims to preserve the victim’s vocal identity, convey the chosen
text, and produce natural speech. We describe these goals through
real-valued scores.

Let $`S`$ be an independent speaker-similarity scorer, $`C`$ a fixed ASR
transcription map, $`d`$ the normalized edit distance relative to the
target text, and $`Q`$ a quality scorer that predicts speech
naturalness. For the same generated waveform, we define identity
similarity $`s_{\mathrm{id}}`$, content error $`e_{\mathrm{text}}`$, and
predicted naturalness $`s_{\mathrm{qual}}`$:

```math
\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{id}}=S(\hat{x},x_{v}^{e}),e_{\mathrm{text}}=d(C(\hat{x}),y),s_{\mathrm{qual}}=Q(\hat{x}). \tag{3}
```

Subject to the stated knowledge, capability, and resource constraints,
the adversary seeks higher identity similarity and predicted naturalness
together with lower content error. The corresponding multi-objective
formulation is given below, with expectations over the prespecified
trial distribution of victims, source and enrollment utterances,
interception intervals, target texts, and attack randomness. The three
components specify preferred directions and allow tradeoffs rather than
being combined into a weighted aggregate:

```math
\underset{A}{\operatorname{maximize}}\quad\bigl(\mathbb{E}[s_{\mathrm{id}}],\,-\mathbb{E}[e_{\mathrm{text}}],\,\mathbb{E}[s_{\mathrm{qual}}]\bigr). \tag{4}
```

Equation (4) characterizes three system-level attack goals rather than
prescribing a direct training loss. The formulation emphasizes that
impersonation involves preserving vocal identity together with conveying
attacker-chosen content and retaining natural-speech quality, with
possible tradeoffs among these goals.

### III-B Motivation

Direct voice cloning from reference audio is well studied. Few- and
zero-shot systems can extract a speaker condition from a short reference
recording and synthesize new textual content \[29, 5, 6, 7\].
Consistently, speaker-verification research shows that learned speech
representations encode identity information sufficient for speaker
matching \[24, 25, 26\]. More recent evidence extends this privacy
concern to general-purpose speech representations and speech language
models: self-supervised representations support age estimation \[34\];
HearSay demonstrates private-attribute inference from acoustic
voiceprints by audio language models \[20\]; and SpInv reveals
recoverable voiceprint information in end-to-end speech-language-model
frontend outputs \[4\]. These studies indicate that speech
representations may retain acoustic cues associated with speaker timbre
and identity in addition to linguistic content.

Together, this evidence motivates an unresolved security question:
without access to a victim reference waveform, can an adversary turn a
single intercepted short frontend representation into reusable speaker
conditioning for attacker-chosen speech independent of the source
content? Reference-audio cloning establishes that speaker conditioning
can support synthesis of new content, while embedding recovery
establishes that identity information persists in frontend
representations; neither determines whether leaked representations
retain the acoustic detail required for direct speech generation. This
motivates learning to reuse such information across utterances and
testing whether short leaks support impersonation of unseen speakers.

## IV Speech Token Inversion Attack

### IV-A Overview

STIA converts fragmented frontend representations into generation
conditions for attacker-chosen-text speech. As shown in Fig. 2, a leak
encoder maps the representation to a speaker-related condition sequence,
while a frozen mT5 encoder \[35\] and trainable text projection supply
the target-content condition. The two sequences are concatenated as
inputs to a Transformer whose parameters are shared between denoise and
decode modes. It learns conditional flow matching \[8\] in the
continuous latent space of the Higgs encoder \[36\]. Its denoise mode
learns to move from noise toward speech latents, its decode mode
provides codec-aware endpoint supervision, and a stop head predicts the
valid output boundary.

Figure 2(a) shows training on same-speaker, cross-utterance pairs from
public auxiliary speech: constructing leakage and text conditions and
supervising the generator with target speech latents. The target
frontend only supplies black-box query outputs and is excluded from
gradient computation; mT5 and the Higgs codec components remain frozen.
The leak encoder, text projection, shared Transformer, and its output
heads are trained. Figure 2(b) shows the fixed-model attack: encoding
the intercepted representation and new text, sampling a latent from
Gaussian noise, applying final decode, and synthesizing speech through
frozen RVQ and the Higgs waveform decoder. For readability, the figure
omits latent-standardization bars and abbreviates frame- and
channel-averaged losses.

### IV-B STIA Training

Training pairs and conditioning. Each training example pairs target
utterance $`x`$ with a distinct source utterance $`x^{r}`$ from the same
speaker, where superscript $`r`$ denotes the representation source. A
source crop $`x_{I}^{r}`$ over interval $`I`$ passes through frozen
frontend $`T`$ to produce $`R_{I}^{\mathrm{frag}}(x^{r})`$; the target
utterance supplies transcript $`y_{x}`$ and acoustic supervision.
Identity is thus shared across the two utterances, whereas target text
specifies the generated content.

The trainable leak encoder $`E_{R}`$ maps heterogeneous frontend outputs
to a condition sequence $`H_{R}`$ of a common width. Discrete tokens use
codebook-specific embeddings aggregated across codebooks; continuous
features use a linear projection and normalization. For mixed
representations, the aligned branches are concatenated along the feature
dimension and projected to the shared width. A validity mask preserves
the temporal extent of the observed sequence:

```math
H_{R}=E_{R}(R_{I}^{\mathrm{frag}}(x^{r})). \tag{5}
```

The frozen multilingual mT5 encoder $`M_{\mathrm{T5}}`$ encodes the
target transcript, and trainable projection $`E_{Y}`$ maps it to a
text-condition sequence $`H_{Y}`$ of the same width. This permits a
leaked condition to be combined with different target texts, without
requiring the target text to match the source transcript:

```math
H_{Y}=E_{Y}(M_{\mathrm{T5}}(y_{x})). \tag{6}
```

The shared Transformer assembles $`H_{R}`$, $`H_{Y}`$, speech latents,
and time and mode embeddings into one sequence. Condition positions
cannot attend to speech positions, whereas speech positions can attend
to all valid condition and speech positions. This noncausal speech
attention combines identity cues, target text, and full latent context.
Cross-utterance supervision encourages speaker information that can be
reused in generating the target content, but does not enforce complete
disentanglement of identity and content.

Latent flow matching. We use conditional flow matching \[8\], adapting
ELF’s scheme of denoising in a continuous embedding space followed by
decoding \[9\] to speech-latent generation. The frozen Higgs encoder
$`E_{Z}`$ extracts the pre-quantization target latent
$`z_{1}=E_{Z}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{Z}}`$, where $`L`$ and
$`d_{Z}`$ are the valid frame count and channel dimension. We
standardize it using auxiliary-training-set channelwise mean $`\mu_{Z}`$
and standard deviation $`\sigma_{Z}`$. The continuous pre-quantization
space admits interpolation paths between noise and the target:

```math
\bar{z}_{1}=\frac{z_{1}-\mu_{Z}}{\sigma_{Z}}. \tag{7}
```

Conditioned on $`H_{R}`$ and $`H_{Y}`$, the flow model learns the
distribution of standardized target latents. Denoise training samples
noise $`z_{0}`$ from $`\mathcal{N}(0,4\mathbf{I})`$, where
$`\mathbf{I}`$ is the identity matrix, and a time $`t`$ from a time
distribution $`p_{t}`$ on $`(0,1)`$. The state $`z_{t}`$ on the linear
path is

```math
z_{t}=(1-t)z_{0}+t\bar{z}_{1}. \tag{8}
```

As in ELF \[9\], we use an _x-prediction, v-loss_ strategy: the network
predicts the clean endpoint rather than velocity directly. Let
$`F_{\theta}`$ denote the shared Transformer mapping with its latent and
stop heads, where $`\theta`$ collects trainable parameters, $`u`$ is the
noisy latent input, $`\tau`$ is the network’s time input, and $`b`$
selects denoise or decode mode. The auxiliary self-conditioning endpoint
$`q`$ is zero when unused. Its outputs are clean-endpoint estimate
$`\hat{\bar{z}}_{1}`$ and framewise stop probabilities $`\hat{p}`$.
Denoise mode uses $`u=z_{t}`$ and $`\tau=t`$:

```math
(\hat{\bar{z}}_{1},\hat{p})=F_{\theta}(H_{R},H_{Y},u,\tau,b;q). \tag{9}
```

The predicted endpoint is converted to velocity through the remaining
path length. Let $`d_{t}=\max(1-t,\epsilon)`$, where $`\epsilon`$ is a
numerical-stability floor. The predicted velocity $`\hat{v}_{t}`$ is

```math
\hat{v}_{t}=\frac{\hat{\bar{z}}_{1}-z_{t}}{d_{t}}. \tag{10}
```

The target velocity parameterization $`v_{t}^{*}`$ is computed
identically from the true endpoint. When the floor is inactive, it
equals the exact linear-path velocity $`\bar{z}_{1}-z_{0}`$; near the
endpoint, the floor limits error amplification:

```math
v_{t}^{*}=\frac{\bar{z}_{1}-z_{t}}{d_{t}}. \tag{11}
```

The denoise objective minimizes squared velocity error. Let $`j`$ index
valid latent frames and $`\mathbb{E}`$ denote expectation over auxiliary
pairs, noise, time, and valid frames. Averaging over latent channels
gives a clean-endpoint error weighted by $`d_{t}^{-2}`$. Thus, endpoint
prediction is linked to iterative denoising along the path through
velocity supervision:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|\hat{v}_{t,j}-v^{*}_{t,j}\|_{2}^{2}}{d_{Z}}\right]. \tag{12}
```

Codec-aware decoding. Flow matching supervises denoising in continuous
space, whereas the resulting speech also depends on discrete code
selection by the frozen RVQ. Following ELF’s shared denoising/decoding
design \[9\], decode mode adds target-code supervision to connect the
predicted endpoint to the Higgs quantization structure. It shares the
Transformer and output heads with denoise mode and is not a separate
waveform decoder.

Decode training constructs input $`z_{a}`$ using independent Gaussian
noise $`\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})`$ and corruption coefficient
$`a`$ sampled from a distribution $`p_{a}`$ on $`(0,1)`$. The network
receives $`u=z_{a}`$, but its time is fixed to $`\tau=1`$ and its mode
to $`b=\mathrm{decode}`$:

```math
z_{a}=a\bar{z}_{1}+(1-a)\xi. \tag{13}
```

The predicted endpoint is denormalized before RVQ supervision:

```math
\hat{z}_{1}=\sigma_{Z}\cdot\hat{\bar{z}}_{1}+\mu_{Z}. \tag{14}
```

Let $`k\in\{1,\ldots,8\}`$ index RVQ stages, $`e_{k,c}`$ denote codeword
$`c`$ at stage $`k`$, and $`P_{k}`$ its frozen input projection. Target
indices $`c^{*}_{k,j}`$ are obtained by quantizing $`z_{1}`$. The
predicted residual $`\hat{r}_{k,j}`$ is initialized with
$`\hat{z}_{1,j}`$ and updated by subtracting the output projections of
preceding ground-truth codewords. This teacher forcing supervises each
stage under the same target-code prefix. Classification logits
$`\ell_{k,j,c}`$ use projected residual-to-codeword distances, with
temperature $`\tau_{Q}`$:

```math
\ell_{k,j,c}=-\frac{\|P_{k}(\hat{r}_{k,j})-e_{k,c}\|_{2}^{2}}{\tau_{Q}}. \tag{15}
```

Let $`\ell_{k,j}`$ collect the logits over all codewords at a frame,
$`\operatorname{CE}`$ denote cross-entropy between predicted
probabilities and the target code index, and $`w_{k}`$ be normalized
stage weights. The RVQ loss weights stages and averages over decode
examples and their valid frames. Codebooks and projections remain
frozen, but the distance logits propagate gradients to the predicted
latent:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RVQ}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{8}w_{k}\operatorname{CE}(\operatorname{softmax}(\ell_{k,j}),c^{*}_{k,j})\right]. \tag{16}
```

Stopping and joint training. The stop head predicts stopping probability
$`\hat{p}_{j}`$ at each frame. Let $`s_{j}`$ be a binary label equal to
$`1`$ only at the final valid frame, and $`\beta_{\mathrm{stop}}`$ a
positive-class balancing weight. Both modes use weighted binary
cross-entropy to learn output boundaries alongside acoustic generation:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stop}}=-\mathbb{E}\big[\beta_{\mathrm{stop}}s_{j}\log\hat{p}_{j}+(1-s_{j})\log(1-\hat{p}_{j})\big]. \tag{17}
```

Each example selects denoise mode with probability $`0.8`$ and decode
mode with probability $`0.2`$. The former learns continuous denoising,
the latter learns target-code consistency, and stopping supervision
applies to both. The expected training objective is therefore given
below, with the stopping loss also averaged over mode sampling:

```math
\mathcal{L}=0.8\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}+0.2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RVQ}}+0.1\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stop}}. \tag{18}
```

As in ELF \[9\], self-conditioning supplies a preliminary endpoint
prediction as $`q`$ without gradient propagation, providing an existing
clean-latent estimate for iterative prediction. It is enabled for a
minibatch with probability $`0.5`$. Each example jointly drops leakage
and text conditions with probability $`0.1`$, denoting empty conditions
by $`\varnothing`$, to learn unconditional predictions for
classifier-free guidance at attack time \[37\].

### IV-C STIA Attacking

At attack time, fixed encoders transform
$`R_{I}^{\mathrm{frag}}(x_{v}^{r})`$ intercepted from victim $`v`$ and
chosen text $`y`$ into conditions $`H_{R},H_{Y}`$. No victim waveform,
source transcript, or enrollment speech is required. Let $`L_{\max}`$ be
the latent-length limit determined from the chosen text. Initial state
$`z^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{\max}\times d_{Z}}`$ is sampled from the same
Gaussian base distribution as denoise training:

```math
z^{(0)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,4\mathbf{I}). \tag{19}
```

At sampling step $`n`$, the model predicts clean endpoints with
$`(H_{R},H_{Y})`$ and with empty conditions, denoted
$`\hat{\bar{z}}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{c}}`$ and
$`\hat{\bar{z}}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{u}}`$; superscripts $`\mathrm{c}`$ and
$`\mathrm{u}`$ mean conditional and unconditional. Classifier-free
guidance (CFG) \[37\] combines the predictions with scale $`s`$ to
strengthen adherence to leakage and text conditions. Each path uses its
own preceding endpoint as self-conditioning, initialized to zero:

```math
\hat{\bar{z}}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{cfg}}=\hat{\bar{z}}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{u}}+s\big(\hat{\bar{z}}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{c}}-\hat{\bar{z}}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{u}}\big). \tag{20}
```

The guided endpoint defines a velocity from the current noisy state
toward conditional speech. Let $`t_{n}`$ denote the current time,
$`\epsilon_{\mathrm{sam}}`$ the sampling denominator floor, and
$`v_{n}`$ the velocity evaluated at the current state with its
self-conditioning:

```math
v_{n}=\frac{\hat{\bar{z}}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{cfg}}-z^{(n)}}{\max(1-t_{n},\epsilon_{\mathrm{sam}})}. \tag{21}
```

We integrate the learned flow with an explicit Euler ordinary
differential equation (ODE) sampler, as in ELF’s ODE sampling
scheme \[9\]. We use $`N=50`$ steps in all experiments. Integration ends
at $`t_{\star}=0.99`$ on the uniform grid $`t_{n}=nt_{\star}/N`$, where
$`n=0,\ldots,N`$, with step size $`\Delta t=t_{\star}/N`$. Each update
advances the latent along the predicted velocity:

```math
z^{(n+1)}=z^{(n)}+\Delta t\,v_{n}. \tag{22}
```

After sampling, the model performs one conditional decode at time $`1`$
on $`z^{(N)}`$, without CFG and retaining the last conditional endpoint
as self-conditioning. This step produces the final latent and stop
probabilities. Valid length $`L`$ is the first frame after
$`L_{\max}/2`$ whose stop probability reaches threshold
$`\tau_{\mathrm{stop}}`$, or $`L_{\max}`$ if none does. The prediction
is then truncated to $`L`$ frames and denormalized.

Frozen RVQ converts the final continuous latent into discrete codes. Let
$`r_{k,j}`$ denote the inference residual, initialized with
$`r_{1,j}=\hat{z}_{1,j}`$, $`\hat{c}_{k,j}`$ the selected code index,
and $`O_{k}`$ the frozen output projection at stage $`k`$. Each stage
selects the nearest codeword to its projected residual, then updates the
residual by subtracting that codeword’s output projection. Unlike
training teacher forcing, these updates use only predicted codewords:

```math
\hat{c}_{k,j}=\arg\min_{c}\|P_{k}(r_{k,j})-e_{k,c}\|_{2}^{2}. \tag{23}
```

Let $`\hat{C}\in\mathbb{N}^{8\times L}`$ be the array of predicted code
indices $`\hat{c}_{k,j}`$ ordered by stage and frame, $`Q_{Z}`$ denote
frozen RVQ reconstruction from indices to quantized latents, and
$`D_{Z}`$ be the Higgs waveform decoder. Decoding the reconstructed
latent yields the attack waveform:

```math
\hat{x}=D_{Z}(Q_{Z}(\hat{C})). \tag{24}
```

Algorithm 2 summarizes the complete attack in four stages. First, it
encodes the leaked representation and target text and initializes
latents with Gaussian noise. Second, it iteratively performs Euler ODE
sampling using CFG to combine conditional and unconditional predictions.
Third, a final decode determines the valid output length and
denormalizes the latent. Fourth, frozen RVQ and the waveform decoder
synthesize speech. These stages convert a single short representation
leak into speech conveying attacker-chosen text.

$`H_{R}\leftarrow E_{R}(R_{I}^{\mathrm{frag}}(x_{v}^{r}))`$

7: Encode text as $`H_{Y}\leftarrow E_{Y}(M_{\mathrm{T5}}(y))`$;

9: Set $`t_{n}\leftarrow 0.99n/N`$ and $`\Delta t\leftarrow 0.99/N`$

10: Initialize separate self-conditions
$`q^{\mathrm{c}},q^{\mathrm{u}}\leftarrow 0,0`$

12: for $`n=0,\ldots,N-1`$ do

$`(H_{R},H_{Y},q^{\mathrm{c}})`$ via Eq. (9)

$`\hat{z}_{1}\leftarrow(\sigma_{Z}\cdot\hat{\bar{z}}_{1}+\mu_{Z})_{1:L}`$

29: Quantize $`\hat{z}_{1}`$ to $`\hat{C}`$ with 8-stage RVQ, Eq. (23)

32: return $`\hat{x}`$

## V Experiments

### V-A Experimental Setup

Datasets. Our training and validation sets are two disjoint subsets of
the public Emilia dataset partitioned by speaker ID, with no shared
speakers \[38\]. Each experiment uses data from one specific language
within Emilia for both training and validation. Main experiments use
Emilia-English, while separate Chinese experiments use Emilia-Chinese.
We train an attack model separately for each language and evaluate it on
the validation subset of the same language; we do not perform
cross-language validation.

Target models. We select Moshi, Kimi-Audio, and Qwen3-Omni as three
representative target models and use only their input-side audio
frontends \[1, 2, 3\]. Access to all three targets follows a black-box
input–output interface: submitting audio and receiving frontend
representations, without access to model parameters or gradients.
Representations of auxiliary speech are obtained through frontend
queries. The target models remain fixed and are excluded from
attack-model parameter optimization and backpropagation; their backends
are not involved in attack training.

Table I summarizes the audio frontend architectures, processing
pipelines, and output formats of the three target models. The selected
representations are taken after Mimi RVQ, at Kimi-Audio’s two branch
outputs, and at the AuT output, respectively, to instantiate the assumed
split-deployment interfaces. The attacker receives only victim
representations at these interfaces, not raw waveforms. Qwen3-Omni’s
Talker output codec is not part of the selected input-side audio
frontend.

Comparison baseline. We combine SpInv \[4\], the closest
representation-inversion method, with the SpeechT5 synthesizer \[10\] to
construct a SpInv+TTS baseline for this task. SpInv supports
attacker-specified embedding spaces; we retrain it specifically in the
512-dimensional x-vector space used by SpeechT5 so that the recovered
vectors are compatible with the synthesizer. SpeechT5 then generates
speech conditioned on the predicted embedding and attacker-chosen text;
neither stage receives genuine victim audio. Both attacks use the same
leakage segments, target texts, victim trials, and evaluators. The
comparison evaluates two-stage embedding recovery and synthesis against
STIA’s direct representation-conditioned generation. Evaluation models
participate in neither training nor output selection.

Metrics. _CAM++ similarity_ (CAM in tables) is computed by extracting
speaker embeddings from generated speech and the victim’s separate
genuine enrollment speech using CAM++, then taking their cosine
similarity: the embedding dot product divided by the product of their
Euclidean norms \[26\]. It measures preservation of the victim’s vocal
identity; higher is better.

_ECAPA-TDNN similarity_ (ECAPA in tables) is computed from ECAPA-TDNN
embeddings of generated speech and the same genuine enrollment speech,
taking their cosine similarity as the dot product divided by the product
of their norms \[25\]. It measures identity preservation with a second
speaker encoder; higher is better.

_Word error rate_ (WER) aligns the ASR transcript of generated English
speech with the attacker-chosen text, divides the minimum total number
of word substitutions, deletions, and insertions by the target word
count, and reports the result as a percentage. It measures how
accurately the generated English content matches the chosen text; lower
is better.

_Character error rate_ (CER) aligns the ASR transcript of generated
Chinese speech with the attacker-chosen text, divides the minimum total
number of character substitutions, deletions, and insertions by the
target character count, and reports the result as a percentage. It
measures how accurately the generated Chinese content matches the chosen
text; lower is better.

_UTMOS_ passes each generated waveform through a pretrained scoring
model to predict its mean opinion score without reference audio \[39\].
It estimates the perceptual naturalness of generated speech; higher is
better.

Implementation details. M, L, and XL use 8, 12, and 16 layers,
respectively; the default is XL (0.60B). The main Moshi setting uses
3-second leaks and the largest Emilia-English budget. Tables without a
target-model column use Moshi and reuse its main-configuration results.
Training settings and cost appear in Sec. V-I.

Leakage and enrollment utterances are disjoint; frozen target prompts
are independent of leaked content. Each configuration generates four
samples per recording for 1,000 recordings using 50-step Euler sampling
and CFG scale 2. All 4,000 utterance-level scores are aggregated
separately by language. Table II reports mean $`\pm`$ standard
deviation; Tables III–VIII report means. Reported statistics are rounded
to two decimals.

Result presentation. Subscripts indicate the direction and magnitude of

table in Tables III–VIII. Error-rate differences are expressed in

determined before rounding, within each frontend in Table II and within

Evaluators use 3D-Speaker’s CAM++ and ECAPA-TDNN checkpoints \[40\],

### V-G Speaker Anonymization

We use the VoicePrivacy 2022 B2 McAdams anonymization method and default
parameter settings as an input-side defense \[28\]. Each 3-second source
crop is converted to 16-kHz mono audio and processed using 20-ms
windows, a 10-ms hop, and order-20 linear predictive coding (LPC). The
coefficient $`\alpha\sim\mathcal{U}(0.5,0.9)`$ is drawn once per speaker
and held fixed across that speaker’s crops and repeated generation
trials. Boundary zero-padding covers the full crop; frontend
representations are then re-extracted from the anonymized waveform to
condition STIA.

The attacker is trained on unanonymized auxiliary speech without
retraining or tuning; its checkpoint, sampling settings, and output
selection rule remain fixed. The anonymization transform and
coefficients are unknown to the attacker. Both input conditions use the
same Moshi frontend, victims, and English prompts; identity evaluation
uses disjoint, genuine enrollment speech of the victim’s original
identity.

Table VII shows lower mean similarity under both speaker encoders and
lower UTMOS after anonymization, with a small increase in mean WER.
These results support attenuation of this attacker, but distribution
shift and reduced naturalness preclude interpreting them as evidence
that identity information has been removed; adaptive attackers remain
unevaluated.

### V-H Conditioning and Text Controls

We examine leakage and text conditioning through three input
interventions: No-leak removes leakage, No-text removes text, and
No-leak-and-text removes both. These interventions use the fixed
main-configuration model without retraining. Let $`v`$ denote the victim
speaker and define $`R_{v}=R_{I}^{\mathrm{frag}}(x_{v}^{r})`$.
Generation seeds and other settings are fixed for these three
interventions; Standard in Table VIII reuses the main-experiment
results.

No-leak removes all leakage-conditioning streams, retaining text in the
conditional branch while preserving normal behavior of the CFG
unconditional branch; the final decode also receives no leakage.
Training jointly drops text and leakage, so the text-only no-leak
condition may be out of distribution; degradation alone cannot establish
the necessity of leakage conditioning.

No-text retains leakage conditioning but supplies no target text,
whereas No-leak-and-text (Neither in Table VIII) supplies neither
leakage conditioning nor target text. WER is not reported for these two
conditions because no target text is supplied.

Mismatched text (Wrong text in Table VIII) is a separate scoring
negative control: the fixed model generates speech conditioned on
attacker-chosen text $`y_{A}`$, but the ASR transcript is scored against
a different, unpaired text $`y_{B}`$, with $`y_{A}\neq y_{B}`$. This row
uses a newly generated set of utterances rather than directly rescoring
the Standard audio; the model is not retrained. Its identity and
naturalness scores describe that output set, not an effect of changing
the scoring reference. Its WER measures mismatch with $`y_{B}`$, not
adherence error relative to the generation condition $`y_{A}`$.

Removing leakage reduces CAM similarity from 0.78 to 0.25, whereas
retaining leakage without text yields 0.57. Mismatched text yields CAM
and ECAPA similarities of 0.75 and 0.71 and UTMOS of 3.08, but WER
against the unpaired text is 99.17%, compared with 3.80% under the
standard protocol. This high error rate reflects mismatch with the
scoring text and cannot be interpreted as failure to follow the
generation condition.

## VI Discussion

### VI-A Security Implications

SpInv established that speaker identity information can be recovered
from speech-language-model frontend representations \[4\]. Our results
show a further consequence: vocal cues retained in fragmented
representations can be reused to generate new, attacker-chosen content.
Evaluation on three representative frontends shows that a representation
leak can expose identity information and also provide a condition for
generating speech with the victim’s vocal characteristics. A speaker may
therefore face voice impersonation risks even when the attacker cannot
obtain the original recording.

Assessing voice impersonation risks requires examining both whose voice
the generated speech resembles and what it says. Table II shows higher
mean speaker similarity and lower mean target-text WER for STIA than for
the SpInv+TTS baseline method, while mean UTMOS scores remain similar.
The principal observed gains are therefore in vocal-identity
preservation and content accuracy, with no large improvement in
predicted naturalness. Privacy evaluation should consider all three
aspects together: high identity similarity alone does not establish
accurate delivery of the chosen text, and accurate text does not
establish resemblance to the victim’s voice.

### VI-B Defensive Directions

Because the attack assumes read access to frontend representations,
limiting that access is a direct defensive measure. Authenticated
encryption can protect representations in transit, while process
isolation, restricted read permissions, and short retention can reduce
exposure at endpoints. Protection must also account for services or
relays that legitimately receive the representations, since transport
encryption alone cannot prevent their reuse by a recipient. Systems
should therefore identify which components can read or store
representations and restrict access accordingly. Shorter exposure can
reduce the information available, but Sec. V-E shows that even the
shortest tested fragments retain useful speaker cues.

A complementary direction is to alter speaker identity cues before
frontend encoding. In the Moshi experiment, McAdams anonymization lowers
both mean speaker-similarity scores for the fixed attacker, along with
the UTMOS of the generated speech (Table VII). This shows that the
transformation can weaken the evaluated attacker, but does not establish
that identity information has been removed. Because the attacker was
trained only on unanonymized speech, the decrease may also partly
reflect differences between its inputs and training data. Further
defense evaluation should therefore include attackers retrained on
anonymized inputs and measure the impact on legitimate backend tasks;
neither aspect is evaluated here.

In applications that use voice for authentication, an additional
authentication factor or independent confirmation tied to a specific
transaction could help limit the consequences of forged speech. Such
measures address the application-level impact of voice impersonation and
complement protection of the frontend representations themselves.

### VI-C Limitations

Evaluation coverage. The experiments use speaker-disjoint subsets of
Emilia and attack models trained for each target frontend. Three
representative frontends are evaluated in English, with a separate
Chinese evaluation on Moshi. The results therefore establish
generalization to unseen speakers within the tested settings;
generalization across datasets or languages, and transfer across
frontends without retraining, remain untested.

Output-codec dependence. Although STIA accommodates multiple frontend
representation formats, speech generation uses the frozen Higgs codec’s
continuous pre-quantization latent space. The vocal characteristics and
quality of the generated speech therefore also depend on what the Higgs
codec can represent and reconstruct. Alternative output codecs have not
been evaluated.

Measurement limitations. Speaker similarity, ASR error rates, and UTMOS
provide automatic measurements of vocal identity, text accuracy, and
naturalness, but may not fully agree with listener judgments. The
reported similarities are not authentication success rates, and UTMOS
predicts naturalness rather than collecting listener ratings. Listener
studies and evaluation against operational authentication systems are
needed to determine practical impersonation effectiveness.

## VII Ethics Considerations

This study uses public Emilia data for offline privacy measurement; the
dataset is distributed under CC BY-NC 4.0. We synthesize speech
resembling dataset speakers without recruiting participants, collecting
private recordings, or constructing real-world identity profiles.
Institutional consultation confirmed that formal ethics approval was not
required for this study. Experiments remain within controlled,
dataset-based settings and do not deploy attacks against external
services.

Public availability does not imply speaker consent to voice cloning.
Representation inversion could enable unauthorized impersonation or
fraud. To limit individual exposure through experimental results, this
paper reports aggregate metrics rather than identifiable voice
demonstrations. Future releases should respect dataset licenses and
restrict distribution of identifiable audio and reusable attack models.
The study aims to expose these risks and inform more privacy-preserving
speech interfaces.

## VIII Conclusion

We introduced STIA for arbitrary-text speaker impersonation from leaked
frontend representation fragments of end-to-end speech language models.
The method combines cross-utterance training with conditional flow
matching to reuse vocal cues in representations without the victim’s
waveform or source transcript. Experiments on three representative
frontends show higher mean speaker similarity and lower mean target-text
WER than the SpInv+TTS baseline method, exposing voice impersonation
risks from representation leakage.

Future work should pursue three directions. First, extend evaluation to
additional datasets, languages, and realistic deployment conditions.
Second, assess practical impersonation effectiveness through listener
studies and tests against operational authentication systems. Third,
investigate stronger voice anonymization and frontend privacy
protections, jointly evaluating their resistance to adaptive attackers
and their impact on legitimate speech tasks.
