# Nedimo: An Evidence-Grounded Multilingual Pipeline for AI-Generated Sleep Stories

Georgios Makridis and Stefanos Kampouris Affiliation: Department of
Digital Systems  
University of Piraeus  
Piraeus, Greece  
Web application and project website:[nedimo.com](https://nedimo.com)  
Mobile apps:[Google

###### Abstract

Generative audio systems can produce large libraries of personalized
bedtime content, but ordinary media objectives—retention, completion,
and novelty—are poorly aligned with the goal of supporting a transition
toward sleep. Direct evidence for narrated bedtime audio in adults is
mixed, and a recent active-controlled trial did not support such audio
as a standalone treatment for sleep disturbance. We translate this
uncertain evidence into conservative engineering requirements and
present a multilingual pipeline for generating and narrating low-arousal
sleep stories. The implemented prototype separates deterministic
planning from large language model generation, represents content and
delivery parameters explicitly, shapes each script through a four-phase
attention taper, validates structural and lexical constraints, performs
a bounded error-aware retry, and quarantines unresolved outputs.
Provider-agnostic text-to-speech synthesis is followed by
paragraph-aware joining, two-pass loudness normalization, and a
publication gate that re-measures the saved, encoded file against fixed
loudness, loudness-range, and true-peak limits. The product data model
records stop position, replay, early abandonment, and completion while
treating these events as ambiguous behavioral proxies rather than
clinical outcomes. We contribute an evidence-to-requirement mapping, a
traceable guardrail architecture with explicit acceptance thresholds,
technical evaluation criteria, and a prospective protocol for testing
sleep-onset effects. No clinical efficacy is claimed; the system is
positioned as a relaxation and attentional-support tool whose benefit
and possible reliance must be evaluated empirically.

## I Introduction

Audio stories are increasingly offered as pre-sleep content, yet their
design objective differs from that of conventional spoken media. For an
entertainment service, finishing a story and requesting another episode
are desirable outcomes. For a sleep-onset aid, continuous engagement to
the end may instead indicate that the listener remained awake.
Optimizing ordinary completion metrics can therefore work against the
intended use.

The empirical basis also requires restraint. An early audiobook
comparator did not improve sleep quality over control \[1\]; small app
and intensive-care studies reported partial or context-specific
signals \[2, 3, 4\]; and a 2026 randomized trial of 495 working adults
found that bedtime stories did not outperform an active digital control
on its primary sleep-disturbance outcome \[5\]. At the same time, in a
self-selected online survey of 1,136 Swedish adults, respondents with
sleep-onset insomnia symptoms more often reported using bedtime
audiobooks to reduce worry, and daily listeners more often reported
returning to the same book \[6\]. These results motivate a system that
is usable and testable without claiming that narration is a treatment
for insomnia.

This paper describes the design and implementation of such a system in
the [Nedimo web application and project
website](https://nedimo.com) \[7\] and its Android and iOS apps \[8,
9\]. The work addresses three questions: how sleep-related evidence can
be translated into controllable generation parameters; how those
constraints can be enforced across text generation, synthesis, and
delivery; and how the system can be evaluated without equating media
engagement with sleep.

The principal contributions are:

- •
  an evidence-to-requirement mapping for adult sleep-story generation;
- •
  a deterministic, multilingual orchestration pipeline with a
  parameterized narrative contract and four-phase attention taper;
- •
  layered structural, lexical, and acoustic guardrails with stated
  acceptance thresholds, bounded retry, and quarantine; and
- •
  a three-level evaluation framework separating technical conformance,
  product telemetry, and clinical sleep outcomes.

## II Evidence Base and Design Requirements

### II-A Narration, attention, and adult sleep

The most plausible role of a story is attentional redirection: it can
occupy enough cognitive capacity to interrupt rumination while avoiding
the uncertainty and emotional activation of ordinary narrative. Harvey
and Payne found that people with insomnia who elaborated an interesting,
engaging, pleasant, and relaxing image reported shorter sleep-onset
latency and less uncomfortable pre-sleep thought than participants
assigned general distraction or no instruction \[10\]. This finding
argues against a completely flat story. It instead suggests an
attentional middle: concrete and gently absorbing, but without
consequential goals.

Direct bedtime-audio evidence remains heterogeneous (Table I). Harmat et
al. compared relaxing music, an audiobook, and control in 94 students;
music improved subjective sleep quality, whereas the audiobook did
not \[1\]. A 40-person crossover study found actigraphy-estimated
sleep-efficiency and pre-sleep heart-rate-variability changes across
sleepcasts, music, and mindfulness, but only mindfulness improved
subjective PSQI scores \[2\]. A 300-person pilot supported the
feasibility of app-delivered stories and music but lacked the stronger
active-control evidence needed for efficacy claims \[3\]. Mermer and
Arslan reported favorable perceived sleep and physiological measures
after a 30-minute audiobook-plus-music session in 56 intensive-care
patients, a setting that limits generalization \[4\]. Most decisively,
the larger USleep trial found only a very small, nonsignificant primary
effect for bedtime stories relative to digital control ($`g=0.12`$, 95%
CI $`[-0.13,0.37]`$) \[5\].

### II-B Sound and delivery

Adjacent music evidence is more developed than story evidence. A
Cochrane review found improvement in subjective sleep quality, while
effects on objective measures were less certain \[11\]. Sleep-associated
music is generally softer and slower than music overall, but exhibits
substantial individual and subgroup variation \[12\]. Reviews of white
and pink noise are heterogeneous, so an ambient layer should remain
optional rather than be presented as universally effective \[13\]. WHO
environmental guidance recommends very low continuous bedroom noise;
this is a boundary for avoiding disturbance, not a validated audiobook
playback dose \[14\]. Likewise, no fixed carrier, tuning, binaural, or
“brainwave” frequency has been established as an effective property of
narrated sleep audio.

Delivery may matter independently of story content. In an adjacent
experiment, 35 participants heard a soothing narrated voice, a robotic
voice, or silence after a standardized stress-induction task; salivary
cortisol fell faster in the soothing-voice group, whereas electrodermal
recovery did not differ between groups \[15\]. This is a stress-recovery
finding, not a sleep outcome, and we treat it only as a rationale for
testing calm, stable prosody. Screen-off playback is preferable because
evening exposure to light-emitting readers can delay sleep and circadian
timing \[16\]. These findings motivate stable prosody, low dynamic
variation, a timer, a gradual fade, and disabled autoplay; they do not
establish a universal narrator pitch or speech rate.

### II-C Constrained generation and speech synthesis

Large language models have been used to generate personalized,
age-appropriate fairy tales for children, together with prompts for
accompanying illustrations \[17\]. Sleep narration for adults adds
constraints that such storytelling systems do not target: low arousal, a
controlled attentional taper, and a duration budget. Prompting alone
does not guarantee schema-valid output. Structured-output benchmarks
show that even capable language models can violate nontrivial schemas,
supporting the use of an external parser and validator \[18\]. Precise
length following is also unreliable in zero-shot generation; target
adjustment, filtering, and automated revision improve compliance \[19\].
For synthesis, SSML provides a provider-independent representation for
pronunciation, pauses, rate, pitch, and volume, although provider
behavior remains implementation specific \[20\]. Modern neural TTS
systems also expose one-to-many variation in duration, pitch, and
rhythm \[21\]; consequently, textual correctness alone cannot guarantee
acoustic conformance. The final waveform must be measured. We use
loudness, loudness-range, and true-peak descriptors consistent with EBU
R 128 terminology, without assuming that its broadcast target is itself
an appropriate bedtime level \[22\].

## III System Architecture

The production system executes a nightly sequence from deterministic
slot planning to publication and feedback (Fig. 1). Generation is
deliberately one stage in a larger controlled process.

Fig. 1: End-to-end production pipeline. Solid arrows show the
publication path; dashed arrows show correction, quarantine, and
aggregate feedback paths.

### III-A Deterministic planning

For each run date and language, the planner resolves a slot record
before any model call. The record includes target duration, engagement
arm, story domain, movement mode, point of view, familiarity mode,
setting, sensory palette, weather and time-of-day profile, repetition
strategy, and voice configuration. Repeating the same date–language run
is idempotent. Variation is therefore scheduled over controlled pools
rather than obtained by repeatedly asking a model to be “creative.” This
prevents accidental local theme collapse and makes each artifact
traceable to a resolved input.

The engagement arm is also assigned by the planner. Fixed allocation
across low-, medium-, and higher-engagement variants permits later
comparative experiments while preventing production traffic from
drifting toward whichever content maximizes completion.

### III-B Prompt contract and duration budget

The prompt is composed from stable system rules, the sleep-story skill,
the resolved slot, an output schema, and optional retry diagnostics. It
requests machine-parseable front matter, a title, one continuous
narration body, and a short synthesis-notes block. The body must not
expose its phase labels, duration budget, or validation state.

For language $`\ell`$, duration planning can be expressed as

```math
W_{\ell}^{\star}=T\frac{r_{s,\ell}}{\bar{s}_{\ell}},\qquad W_{\ell}\in[0.93W_{\ell}^{\star},1.07W_{\ell}^{\star}], \tag{1}
```

where $`T`$ is target minutes, $`r_{s,\ell}`$ is the configured syllable
rate, and $`\bar{s}_{\ell}`$ is an empirical syllables-per-word
estimate. When a language-specific direct word-rate estimate is
available, it can replace the ratio. The $`\pm 7\%`$ band is the target
range given to the generator; the blocking validator check rejects a
script whose syllable-estimated duration deviates from $`T`$ by more
than 8%. Both are implementation tolerances, not evidence-based sleep
thresholds. Languages for which words are a poor duration unit require a
different estimator; Japanese mora-level validation remains an
identified implementation gap.

### III-C Four-phase attention taper

The narrative contract partitions the script into Arrival (10%),
Exploration (55%), Settling (20%), and Fade (15%). The unit is the word
index of the script, not measured playback time: for a target of $`W`$
words, the contract places the boundaries at $`\mathrm{round}(0.10W)`$,
$`\mathrm{round}(0.65W)`$, and $`\mathrm{round}(0.85W)`$. After
generation, the server recomputes the Settling–Fade boundary from the
realized word count $`W_{r}`$ as
$`w_{f}=\mathrm{round}\big(W_{r}(1-T_{f}/T)\big)`$, where the fade
duration $`T_{f}`$ is 4 min for stories of 15 min or longer and
$`\max(2,\lfloor T/4\rfloor)`$ min otherwise; $`T_{f}/T`$ equals the
nominal 15% at about 25 min. The configured synthesis rate is constant
within a story, but sentence and paragraph pauses lengthen after
$`w_{f}`$ (for English, from 800 to 1000 ms and from 1600 to 2200 ms;
other locales use slightly longer values), and providers that accept a
style instruction are asked to slow slightly toward the end. Word-index
boundaries therefore do not map linearly to playback time, and Fade
occupies a larger share of playback time than of words. Phase boundaries
are not yet located in the rendered waveform; forced alignment of text
to audio would allow this and is future work. Arrival immediately
provides a concrete safe place, object, or activity. Exploration
introduces benign descriptive discoveries along one simple thread.
Settling decreases the rate of new entities and scene changes. Fade
introduces no important names, goals, locations, or questions and ends
without a cliffhanger. Figure 2 shows the conceptual profile.

The system seeks curiosity about _what will be described next_, not
concern about _what will happen_. Hard prompt constraints exclude
consequential stakes, open mysteries, direct sleep instructions,
body-state claims, exclamations, and direct questions. Movement
modes—journey, process, cycle, catalogue, or tending—produce variety
while retaining a low-consequence arc.

Fig. 2: Conceptual attention taper. Attentional demand is sufficient to
redirect thought early, then declines; the permitted arousal band
remains low throughout. The curves are design targets, not measured
physiology.

### III-D Guarded generation and bounded retry

Validation proceeds in layers, and each check is either blocking (an
error) or advisory (a warning recorded with the story). The parser first
reads the front matter and narration body. Blocking checks reject a
syllable-estimated duration more than 8% from target, any question mark
or exclamation mark, any hit in per-language lists of banned
high-arousal and sleep-instruction terms, anticipation phrasing,
listener self-address, or meta-reference to the story, and a closing
gesture that resolves the narrative. Advisory checks report word-count
deviation above 7%, median sentence length outside 12–22 words, an
abstraction-rate proxy, and two taper checks after the Fade boundary
$`w_{f}`$: the new-word rate relative to the second half of the body,
and mean sentence length relative to the body. Taper checks remain
advisory because rule-based proxies cannot reliably infer narrative
stakes; these warnings are reviewed by an operator rather than used to
reject automatically.

If validation fails, one retry is issued with the original resolved slot
and targeted diagnostics. The retry asks for correction of named
violations rather than unconstrained regeneration. A second failure is
quarantined for review. This limit bounds cost and prevents a failing
slot from silently entering an open-ended generation loop.

### III-E Synthesis, mastering, and delivery

Accepted scripts are divided at paragraph boundaries and passed through
a provider adapter supporting SSML or plain text. The adapter resolves
locale, voice, pronunciation substitutions, rate, and inter-paragraph
pauses. Chunks are cached and joined with generated silence so a
transient failure does not require resynthesizing the entire story.

The joined waveform is resampled to 44.1 kHz mono and mastered with
FFmpeg’s loudnorm filter, which measures according to EBU R 128 \[22\].
The sequence is: (i) a measurement pass on the lossless source; (ii) a
normalization pass that uses the measured values, with targets of
$`-22`$ LUFS integrated loudness, $`-3`$ dBTP true peak, and 4 LU
loudness range, followed by a 150 s fade-out; (iii) AAC encoding at
96 kb/s; and (iv) a second measurement of the saved, encoded file.
Publication requires integrated loudness in $`[-26,-18]`$ LUFS, true
peak $`\leq-2.5`$ dBTP, and loudness range $`\leq 4.5`$ LU on that saved
file. If it fails, mastering restarts from the lossless source with
dynamic-range compression of ratio 2:1 and then 4:1, for at most three
attempts. The limits are never relaxed, and a file that still fails is
withheld from publication. The application disables automatic
continuation, offers a sleep timer, and supports screen-off playback.
These limits fix level relative to digital full scale; device volume
remains under user control, so they cannot guarantee the sound-pressure
level at the pillow.

## IV Implementation and Guardrail Layers

The prototype separates configuration from generated artifacts. A slot
record identifies the planner version, language, content parameters,
prompt version, model/provider identifiers, validation results, retry
state, synthesis settings, and audio measurements. This separation
permits a script to be regenerated or resynthesized without losing
provenance. Recording why each artifact was accepted, flagged, or
quarantined follows a human-centered view of explainable AI, in which
automated decisions remain inspectable by the people who oversee
them \[23\].

Figure 3 groups controls into five layers. A failure at a lower layer
cannot be compensated by fluent text at a higher layer: a well-written
script with an invalid duration, an unsafe claim, or a clipped waveform
remains unpublishable.

### IV-A Multilingual operation

Language is a first-class planning key rather than a final translation
step. Each locale can define pools, sentence-complexity limits, duration
coefficients, banned expressions, pronunciation rules, voices, and
punctuation constraints. This design avoids assuming that an English
word budget or prosody profile transfers directly to other languages.
Current limitations include incomplete mora-based duration validation
for Japanese and the absence of a completed cross-language perceptual
study. Multilingual support should therefore be understood as an
implemented architecture with language-specific calibration still in
progress.

### IV-B Current implementation boundary

We separate four levels of status. _Implemented and unit-tested:_ the
duration and taper metadata, the multilingual validator, truncation
handling for model output, SSML construction, lexical paragraph repair,
and the saved-file mastering gate. _Implemented:_ planning, the prompt
contract, retry and quarantine, provider adapters, and publication
metadata. _Deployed:_ the pipeline has published stories in English,
German, French, Hindi, and Japanese to the production catalogue.
_Designed but not implemented:_ mora-based Japanese duration validation,
phase timing measured in rendered audio, a cross-language perceptual
study, and the controlled study of Section V. Apart from the five-story
pilot in Section V, this paper does not report conformance results on a
fixed corpus, production-scale reliability statistics, or clinical
outcome data; the other evaluation measures below are a plan, not
results. Those omissions define the present evaluation boundary rather
than being treated as positive results.

## V Evaluation Framework

Evaluation is divided into technical conformance, behavioral telemetry,
and controlled sleep outcomes. Mixing these levels would invite an
efficacy claim from an engineering metric.

### V-A Technical conformance

The first evaluation level can be run without users. Recommended
measures include first-pass schema validity, first-pass acceptance,
retry recovery, quarantine rate, absolute word-count error, phase-budget
error, banned-cue precision and recall on an annotated test set,
late-story entity-introduction rate, synthesis failure rate, duration
error, integrated loudness, loudness range, maximum true peak, and
boundary-discontinuity counts. Results should be reported by language,
voice, generation model, and story domain. A fixed regression corpus is
necessary to detect prompt or provider changes.

### V-B Pilot measurement

As an initial check, Table III reports the five stories published from
one nightly run (12 October 2026): one 20-min slot each in English,
German, French, Hindi, and Japanese, all voiced with the same ElevenLabs
voice. All five passed the blocking script checks with no warnings and
passed the saved-file gate. Integrated loudness was within 0.3 LU of the
$`-22`$ LUFS target. True peak and loudness range met the acceptance
limits but sat slightly above the mastering targets of $`-3`$ dBTP (all
five) and 4 LU (four of five). Rendered duration, which no gate checks,
was within 3% of target for English and German but 13%, 24%, and 38%
short for French, Japanese, and Hindi. The syllables-per-word estimate
in (1) therefore overestimates speaking time for these languages with
this voice; a script can pass the 8% check and still render far from its
target. Five stories from a single run are a pilot, not a fixed-corpus
evaluation, and published records cannot show first-pass acceptance,
retry, or quarantine rates.

### V-C Behavioral telemetry

Let $`T_{a}`$ denote audio duration and $`t_{s}`$ the final playhead
position in a nocturnal session. The system records a normalized stop
fraction

```math
f_{s}=\min\left(\frac{t_{s}}{T_{a}},1\right), \tag{2}
```

along with a reached-end indicator, early abandonment before 120 s, and
same-night replay. These metrics support content-fit diagnosis, but none
identifies sleep. A low $`f_{s}`$ may indicate sleep, rejection,
interruption, or technical failure; a high $`f_{s}`$ may indicate
wakefulness, enjoyment, or a short timer. Accordingly, aggregate
telemetry is used to generate hypotheses and select material for trials,
not to label an individual as asleep.

The system also avoids a completion-maximizing objective and monitors
repeated nightly use as a possible reliance signal. No streak reward or
escalating notification should be attached to bedtime listening.
Telemetry collection should be minimized, consented, access-controlled,
and retained only as long as required for the stated evaluation.

### V-D Prospective controlled study

A preregistered at-home study should compare: (A) generated stories with
the four-phase taper, (B) duration- and narrator-matched stories without
the taper constraints, and (C) a digital control with the same interface
but no narrated content. A within-person randomized design can reduce
between-person variability. The primary endpoint should be sleep-onset
latency measured with validated home EEG where feasible; actigraphy
alone can misclassify quiet wakefulness. Secondary endpoints should
include wake after sleep onset, total sleep time, sleep efficiency,
pre-sleep cognitive arousal, morning restoration, next-day sleepiness,
adverse events, and perceived reliance.

A factorial extension can test engagement level, 15 versus 30 minutes,
voice-only versus optional ambience, and unfamiliar versus
recurring-world content. The success criterion is not higher completion.
It is shorter sleep-onset latency without increased awakenings, next-day
impairment, or reliance. Analyses should report average effects and
responder distributions because personalized audio may benefit some
listeners while disturbing others.

## VI Safety, Ethics, and Limitations

The system is a relaxation product, not CBT-I or a diagnostic device.
Interface and narration text must not promise treatment, instruct users
to ignore persistent symptoms, or imply that a specific frequency
changes brain rhythms. Users reporting chronic insomnia, marked daytime
impairment, loud snoring, witnessed breathing pauses, or gasping should
be directed toward qualified care.

Dependence is a separate concern from acute safety. The observational
association between frequent listening and difficulty sleeping without
audio does not establish causation \[6\], but it supports conservative
product mechanics and explicit measurement. Privacy is also material:
nighttime playback patterns can reveal routines and health-related
behavior. The architecture should avoid microphone capture by default
and separate operational logs from research datasets.

Several limitations remain. First, the attention taper is an
evidence-informed design hypothesis, not a validated dose. Second, the
literature transfers findings from imagery, music, noise, and clinical
settings to generated narration. Third, lexical and structural rules
cannot guarantee low emotional arousal, especially across cultures and
languages. Fourth, TTS voices and consumer playback devices introduce
uncontrolled prosodic and acoustic variation. Fifth, stop-position
telemetry is intrinsically ambiguous. Finally, the present work reports
architecture and implementation status rather than clinical
effectiveness or comparative generation-quality results.

## VII Conclusion

We presented an evidence-grounded pipeline for AI-generated, narrated
sleep stories. Its central design decision is to optimize neither
flatness nor ordinary engagement, but sufficient attentional capture
under a low-arousal constraint followed by a controlled taper.
Deterministic planning, explicit prompt contracts, external validation
with stated thresholds, bounded retry, provider-independent synthesis,
saved-file loudness checks, and non-completion-centric telemetry make
each published artifact traceable to its inputs and checks. The
architecture creates a credible platform for controlled evaluation; it
does not itself demonstrate improved sleep. Future work should publish
language-stratified technical results and a preregistered trial using
objective and subjective sleep outcomes.
