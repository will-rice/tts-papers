# KOMOS: Automatic Mean Opinion Score Estimation for Korean Speech

Sangchun Ha    Junwon Moon    Kyuhong Shim

###### Abstract

As speech generation and real-time spoken dialogue systems advance,
neural mean opinion score (MOS) predictors are increasingly used to
evaluate speech quality. However, many existing predictors are trained
primarily on English read speech and generalize inconsistently across
languages and speaking styles. In particular, their reliability on
Korean speech has not been adequately validated. We propose KOMOS, a
Korean MOS predictor for evaluating the overall quality of both read and
spontaneous speech. To train and evaluate KOMOS, we collect 5,000
ratings from 29 native Korean listeners for 1,000 utterances spanning
real recordings, synthesized speech, and degraded speech. KOMOS achieves
an utterance-level Spearman rank correlation (SRCC) of 0.900, compared
with 0.703 for UTMOS v1 and 0.767 for UTMOS v2. The inference code is
publicly available at [![[Uncaptioned
image]](arxiv-2610-15353v1--76292ab2a51d.figures/figure-1.webp)](https://github.com/upskyy/komos) [https://github.com/upskyy/komos](https://github.com/upskyy/komos)
and the trained model at [![[Uncaptioned
image]](arxiv-2610-15353v1--76292ab2a51d.figures/figure-2.webp)](https://huggingface.co/upskyy/komos) [https://huggingface.co/upskyy/komos](https://huggingface.co/upskyy/komos).

## 1 Introduction

Recent advances in text-to-speech (TTS) and spoken dialogue systems have
increased the need to evaluate perceived speech quality across both read
and spontaneous speech \[2, 15, 18\]. Subjective mean opinion score
(MOS) tests remain the standard for speech quality assessment, but
collecting human ratings is costly and time-consuming. Neural MOS
predictors therefore serve as practical tools for scalable evaluation.
In particular, UTMOS v1 \[22\] is widely used as an automatic evaluation
metric for zero-shot TTS systems.

However, many existing MOS predictors are trained primarily on English
read speech from datasets such as BVCC \[5\], and prior studies report
inconsistent generalization across languages and domains \[3, 4, 20\].
UTMOS has also been used to evaluate Korean TTS systems \[12, 13\],
although its reliability on Korean speech has not been adequately
validated against native Korean listeners’ judgments. For example, our
analysis shows that the calibrated mean predictions of UTMOS v1
overestimate human MOS in the 1.0–2.0 range and underestimate it in the
4.0–5.0 range (Figure 1). These discrepancies highlight the need to
align MOS predictions with Korean listeners’ judgments across speaking
styles and quality levels.

Figure 1: Predicted mean opinion score of KOMOS and UTMOS v1 within each
human-MOS bucket. Predictions are affine-calibrated on the validation
set; the dashed line indicates perfect alignment. KOMOS follows the
human MOS scale more closely.

To address this problem, we propose KOMOS, a Korean MOS predictor
trained on human speech quality ratings covering both read and
spontaneous speech. We fine-tune a pretrained speech encoder and train a
listener-conditioned prediction head using human ratings of Korean
speech. To train and evaluate KOMOS, we construct a dataset of 1,000
utterances with 5,000 ratings from 29 native Korean listeners. The
dataset includes real recordings, synthesized speech, and degraded
speech to cover varied recording conditions and quality levels. We
compare multiple pretrained backbones and fine-tuning strategies under a
common evaluation protocol to select the final KOMOS configuration.

KOMOS achieves an utterance-level test Spearman rank correlation
coefficient (SRCC) of 0.900 and linear correlation coefficient (LCC) of
0.950, outperforming UTMOS v1 and v2 on both metrics. The SRCC
improvements also extend to speaker-disjoint and text-disjoint subsets
of the test set. Beyond correlation, Figure 1 shows that KOMOS follows
the human MOS scale more closely than UTMOS v1, including in the
high-MOS range. In a separate diagnostic using speech from an unseen TTS
system, KOMOS’s calibrated mean predictions across all ten categories
are consistent with the high-quality screening criterion (Sec. 4.4).
Together, these results support the value of Korean human ratings for
adapting MOS predictors and demonstrate improved alignment with human
judgments in both relative quality rankings and absolute MOS values.

Our contributions are as follows:

- •
  We propose KOMOS, a Korean MOS predictor for both read and spontaneous
  speech.
- •
  We construct a Korean MOS dataset comprising 1,000 utterances and
  5,000 ratings from 29 native Korean listeners, covering diverse speech
  sources, styles, and quality levels.
- •
  We analyze the limitations of existing MOS predictors on Korean speech
  and systematically compare pretrained backbones and fine-tuning
  strategies, demonstrating improved agreement between KOMOS predictions
  and human ratings.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Neural MOS Prediction and Generalization

Non-intrusive MOS prediction has progressed from MOSNet \[17\] to
listener-aware models such as MBNet \[16\] and LDNet \[8\], as well as
regression using self-supervised speech features in SSL-MOS \[3\]. UTMOS
v1 \[22\] combines listener embeddings with contrastive learning and
achieved leading performance on multiple metrics in the VoiceMOS
Challenge 2022. UTMOS v2 \[1\] additionally adopts feature fusion with
multi-stage training and attained top-tier performance in the 2024
VoiceMOS Challenge. Training resources remain primarily
English-centered, including BVCC \[5\] and SOMOS \[19\]. SQuId \[23\]
demonstrates multilingual MOS prediction, using a large in-house
human-rating corpus that is not publicly released. IndicMOS \[24\] also
extends MOS prediction beyond English. However, cross-lingual analyses
of SSL-MOS and VoiceMOS Challenge tracks report inconsistent
generalization \[3, 4\], and MOS-Bench \[7\] considers
out-of-distribution generalization as a distinct challenge. The
reliability of existing predictors on Korean speech across speaking
styles and quality levels remains insufficiently characterized.

### 2.2 Korean Speech Quality Assessment

Korean prosody is characterized by tonal patterns at the accentual
phrase level \[9\]. Korean speech also exhibits phonological processes
such as liaison and tensification \[10, 14\]. Recent Korean TTS studies
employ UTMOS as an automatic evaluation metric; PEFT-TTS reports UTMOS
scores for both single- and multi-speaker Korean speech
synthesis \[12\], and CoreaSpeech uses the same metric for both TTS
evaluation and quality-based filtering of Korean speech data \[13\].
Emilia \[6\], a multilingual speech corpus that includes Korean,
likewise uses an automated speech quality assessment tool to filter
recordings collected from diverse online sources. By learning from
native Korean listeners’ overall quality ratings, KOMOS may also support
quality-based selection of Korean speech data across speaking styles.

## 3 KOMOS: A Korean MOS Predictor

### 3.1 Motivation

To motivate the inclusion of spontaneous speech in Korean MOS
prediction, we examine whether UTMOS v1 assigns comparable scores to
read and spontaneous speech within similar human MOS ranges (Fig. 2). We
group utterances by rounding their human MOS to the nearest 0.5. We
compare the four buckets centered at 3.0, 3.5, 4.0, and 4.5, each
containing at least five utterances of each speaking style. The numbers
of read/spontaneous utterances are 21/19, 49/10, 125/10, and 36/5,
respectively.

Figure 2: UTMOS v1 consistently underestimates spontaneous Korean speech
relative to read speech within the same human-MOS buckets. Bars show the
mean raw UTMOS v1 output within each human-MOS bucket, and the dotted
line is the corresponding human rating.

Within these buckets, UTMOS v1 scores spontaneous speech 0.85–1.28
points below read speech, with an unweighted mean gap of 1.05 points
across four buckets. All four comparisons yield unadjusted $`p<.001`$
under two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests and remain significant after
Bonferroni correction. Because this analysis is not a paired experiment
controlling for sentence and speaker, the observed differences cannot be
attributed solely to speaking style.

### 3.2 Dataset Construction

We construct a dataset of 1,000 Korean utterances comprising 420 real
recordings (42%), 310 TTS utterances (31%), and 270 degraded utterances
(27%). The audio includes studio read speech, spontaneous and noisy real
recordings, diverse commercial and open-source TTS systems, and vocoder-
or codec-degraded speech. To cover various utterance types and Korean
linguistic phenomena, we organize the dataset into six general
categories—Command/Information (C), Free-talk (F), Question (Q), Emotion
(E), Interjection (P), and Syntactic Complexity (S)—and four
Korean-specific categories: tensification, liaison, Sino-Korean numeral
readings, and honorifics. The collection contains more than 200 human
speakers and TTS voices.

In the dataset, the read/spontaneous distinction is based on linguistic
style: read speech uses phrasing typical of written texts, whereas
spontaneous speech uses conversational phrasing. These categories
describe the style of the utterance rather than whether it was produced
without a script.

### 3.3 Rating Protocol and Data Split

Twenty-nine native Korean listeners rated overall speech quality from 1
to 5 through an in-house web interface. Listeners were not shown
transcripts and were instructed not to penalize speech merely for being
formal or informal. Five different listeners rated each utterance,
yielding 5,000 ratings. We define utterance-level MOS as the arithmetic
mean of these five ratings. Inter-rater reliability, measured by ordinal
Krippendorff’s $`\alpha`$, is $`0.700`$ \[11\].

We stratify the dataset by utterance-level MOS and construct train,
validation, and test splits of 810, 90, and 100 utterances,
respectively. The main split is a seen-system setting in which source
domains and synthesis systems may occur across splits. Within the test
set, we additionally define a 34-utterance speaker-disjoint subset whose
speakers do not occur in train or validation, and a 55-utterance
text-disjoint subset whose sentences do not occur in either set.

### 3.4 Model and Training

KOMOS follows the listener-conditioned model architecture of
UTMOS \[22\]: a pretrained speech encoder, a 128-dimensional listener
embedding, and a regression head. We compare multiple pretrained
encoders and fine-tuning strategies and select the final configuration
based on validation SRCC. The selected model utilizes the Whisper-medium
encoder \[21\], discarding its decoder and truncating its positional
embedding table to 500 positions for 10-second inputs. Audio is
converted to 16-kHz mono. We freeze the convolutional front end and
fine-tune the remaining encoder parameters.

The listener embedding table contains 31 entries: 29 listeners and two
aggregate entries, mean and median. During training, each utterance is
associated with $`K=7`$ targets: five individual quality ratings, their
mean, and their median. The encoder processes each utterance once, and
its output is reused across targets. For each target, the corresponding
embedding is repeated over time and concatenated with each encoder
frame. The resulting sequence is passed through a single-layer
bidirectional LSTM with a hidden size of 256, followed by a linear
projection to a scalar score at each frame. During inference, we use the
mean embedding and average the scores over valid frames to obtain a
single utterance-level MOS prediction.

We jointly optimize a frame-level regression loss for rating prediction
and an utterance-level ranking loss for relative quality ordering. Let
$`s_{ikf}`$ be the head’s score at valid frame $`f`$ for target $`k`$ of
utterance $`i`$, $`y_{ik}`$ the corresponding target, and $`F_{i}`$ the
number of valid frames after masking padding. We use a frame-level
clipped-MSE variant \[22, 16, 8\],

```math
\displaystyle\ell_{ik} \displaystyle=\frac{1}{F_{i}}\sum_{f=1}^{F_{i}}\left[\max\!\left(\lvert s_{ikf}-y_{ik}\rvert-\tau,\,0\right)\right]^{2}, \tag{1}
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{frame}} \displaystyle=\frac{1}{BK}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\ell_{ik}, \tag{2}
```

where $`B`$ is the batch size and $`\tau=0.25`$ defines an error
tolerance. Errors within this threshold incur no penalty, while larger
errors are penalized by their squared excess over the threshold. We
average this loss over valid frames, $`K`$ targets, and $`B`$ utterances
in the batch.

For the ranking loss, we average the frame scores conditioned on the
mean embedding over valid frames. We denote these utterance-level
predictions by $`\hat{y}_{i}`$ and the corresponding mean human ratings
by $`y_{i}`$. Within each batch, we select utterance pairs whose mean
human ratings differ by at least $`\delta=0.5`$, forming a set of pairs
$`\mathcal{P}=\{(i,j):i<j,\ \lvert y_{j}-y_{i}\rvert\geq\delta\}`$. The
ranking loss is

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rank}}=\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{P}\rvert}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}}\max\!\left(0,\,m-\sigma_{ij}\!(\hat{y}_{j}-\hat{y}_{i})\right), \tag{3}
```

where $`\sigma_{ij}=\operatorname{sgn}(y_{j}-y_{i})`$ and $`m=0.1`$. A
pair incurs no loss once its predictions have the correct order with a
margin of at least $`m`$. The final objective is
$`\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{frame}}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rank}}`$,
with $`\lambda=0.5`$.

We train KOMOS using AdamW with learning rates of $`1\times 10^{-5}`$
for the encoder and $`3\times 10^{-4}`$ for the head and embeddings. We
use weight decay of 0.01, batch size of 32, and bf16 precision. Training
runs for up to 20 epochs, with early stopping after five consecutive
epochs without improvement in validation SRCC. For absolute-score
analyses, we fit an affine calibration
$`\hat{y}_{\text{cal}}=a\hat{y}+b`$, from model predictions to human MOS
on the validation set, and clip the calibrated predictions to the range
$`[1,5]`$. The calibration procedure is applied separately to each
predictor included in these analyses.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Setup

We evaluate utterance-level predictions using Spearman’s rank
correlation coefficient (SRCC) and Pearson’s linear correlation
coefficient (LCC), with SRCC as the primary metric. All training
experiments use three random seeds, and we report the mean of the
metrics computed separately for each seed. Checkpoints and the final
configuration are selected by validation SRCC. We use public UTMOS v1
and UTMOS v2 checkpoints as zero-shot baselines without parameter
updates on Korean data.

For Korean adaptation, we compare wav2vec2-base, WavLM-base+,
WavLM-large, XLS-R-300M, and Whisper encoders from tiny to large-v3.
“UTMOS v1 init.” denotes initialization with encoder weights from the
released UTMOS v1 checkpoint. All adapted models share the same
prediction head architecture and training objectives. Parameter counts
refer to the encoder for adapted models and the complete predictor for
the baselines.

We compare four training strategies. _Full-FT_ (FT in Table 1) updates
the encoder, prediction head, and listener embeddings, except for
Whisper’s frozen convolutional front end. The other strategies keep the
encoder fixed while training the head and listener embeddings.
_Frozen-ws_ combines the outputs of all encoder layers by linear
combination using learnable weights, whereas _frozen-last_ uses only the
final-layer output. _Frozen-early_ (FE) uses the output of the single
layer with the largest learned weight in the corresponding _frozen-ws_
model.

### 4.2 Korean MOS Prediction and Model Selection

Table 1 reports the strategy with the highest validation SRCC for each
KOMOS backbone. First, all backbones adapted using Korean ratings
outperform both ZS baselines, including Whisper-tiny, whose encoder is
an order of magnitude smaller than the baselines. This indicates that
model capacity alone does not explain the gap and highlights the value
of in-language supervision. Second, Whisper-medium achieves the highest
validation SRCC among all evaluated backbones and is selected as the
final backbone. Although larger Whisper variants obtain slightly higher
test SRCCs, they have lower validation SRCCs and require more
parameters. For Whisper-medium, full fine-tuning outperforms all three
frozen-encoder strategies on both metrics (Table 2).

The selected KOMOS configuration achieves a test SRCC of 0.900 and LCC
of 0.950. A paired bootstrap over the 100 test utterances with 10,000
resamples, averaging SRCC across three seeds within each resample,
yields 95% confidence intervals for the SRCC improvement of 0.100–0.320
over UTMOS v1 and 0.056–0.231 over UTMOS v2 (both p $`<`$ 0.001).

### 4.3 Speaker- and Text-Disjoint Evaluation

KOMOS achieves higher SRCC than both baselines on the speaker- and
text-disjoint subsets, achieving 0.951 and 0.878, respectively
(Table 3). All three predictors show an increase in SRCC on the
speaker-disjoint subset relative to the full test set, suggesting that
subset composition may contribute to this increase. These results
support generalization to held-out speakers and texts within the main
seen-system setting. Note that overall SRCC is not a weighted average of
subset SRCCs and can therefore be lower than both.

### 4.4 Score Alignment on an Unseen TTS System

Correlation alone does not establish alignment with the absolute human
MOS scale. We therefore conduct a diagnostic evaluation on 100
utterances synthesized with a single fixed voice from an unseen Korean
TTS system that does not appear during training. The set contains ten
utterances per category (Sec. 3.2), each filtered to meet a score
threshold of 4.5. This experiment shows whether the score-placement
pattern is confined to a particular category. Bracketed forms in Table 4
indicate surface pronunciations.

After validation-based affine calibration, KOMOS assigns a mean score of
at least 4.5 to all ten categories, whereas neither baseline reaches
this threshold in any category. Its overall mean prediction is 4.702,
compared with 3.129 for UTMOS v1 and 3.342 for UTMOS v2.

## 5 Conclusion

We proposed KOMOS, a Korean MOS predictor trained on human quality
ratings covering both read and spontaneous speech. To train KOMOS, we
compared multiple pretrained speech encoders and fine-tuning strategies.
Using 5,000 ratings from 29 native Korean listeners for 1,000
utterances, KOMOS achieved a test SRCC of 0.900 and LCC of 0.950,
outperforming UTMOS v1 and v2. KOMOS also showed closer alignment with
the human MOS scale than UTMOS v1. In the unseen-system diagnostic, the
model’s mean predictions across all ten categories were consistent with
the human annotators’ high-quality screening criterion. These results
highlight the value of in-language supervision for Korean MOS
prediction. We publicly released the inference code and trained model to
support Korean speech quality assessment.

## 6 Compliance with Ethical Standards

The listening study was conducted with adult participants who
voluntarily provided quality ratings of speech samples on a 1–5 scale.
No sensitive personal information was collected, and no personally
identifiable participant information was reported or released.
