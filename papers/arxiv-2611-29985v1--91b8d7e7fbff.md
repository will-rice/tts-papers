# Scoring L2 Pronunciation Without Synthesis Counterfactual Velocity Contrast in Frozen Flow-Matching TTS

Zehan Liu Affiliation: Beijing Jiaotong University

###### Abstract

Flow-matching text-to-speech (TTS) models learn how native speakers
realize a text, yet this knowledge is used only for synthesis. We show
that a frozen TTS model can score non-native (L2) pronunciation without
generating audio. For a learner utterance, we compare the model’s
velocity-prediction error under the target text with its error under a
counterfactual text (dropped or swapped) on the same noised input; the
difference behaves like a generative goodness-of-pronunciation score.
The signal is confined to a narrow band of high-noise timesteps, which a
native-speech criterion locates and a closed-form rule transfers across
backbones. On speaker-disjoint speechocean762, four frozen TTS models
reach $`r{=}0.58`$–$`0.63`$ with human scores, within the range of
zero-shot ASR and phone-level baselines, and adding them to those
baselines in a label-free fusion raises $`r`$ from $`0.698`$ to
$`0.757`$. Cached frame scores also flag word-level errors at no extra
TTS cost.

Keywords: pronunciation assessment, flow matching, text-to-speech,
computer-assisted pronunciation training, zero-shot evaluation.

## 1  Introduction

Automatic pronunciation assessment (APA) scores how well a non-native
(L2) speaker reads a given text and is the core of computer-assisted
pronunciation training (CAPT). Supervised scorers such as GOPT \[1\] are
accurate but need expert-rated L2 speech for every language and learner
population. Zero-shot scorers avoid this cost by reusing models trained
on native speech: Goodness of Pronunciation (GOP) compares phone
likelihoods or posteriors from an acoustic model \[2, 3\], recent
variants read CTC posteriors or logits \[4, 5\], and Liu et al. \[6\]
count the masked units that a self-supervised model fails to recover.
All of them are _discriminative_: they ask how well the audio predicts
the text or its units.

TTS models answer the opposite question: given the text, how would a
native speaker sound? CAPT has so far used them only as generators,
either to create training speech with errors \[7\] or to synthesize a
native “golden” reference that is aligned with the learner’s
audio \[8\]. The second route costs a full synthesis per prompt and
compares two different voices.

In vision, text-to-image diffusion models have been turned into
zero-shot classifiers without generating anything: the prompt that best
helps the model denoise an image wins \[9, 10\], and normalizing by the
error without text makes scores comparable across inputs \[11\]. We ask
whether the same holds for speech, where the task is graded scoring
rather than a choice among classes. A flow-matching TTS model \[12, 13,
14, 15, 16\] is trained on native speech to predict a text-conditioned
velocity field; does its prediction error on _learner_ speech reveal
pronunciation quality?

Used naively, it does not: the raw error is uncorrelated with human
scores ($`r{<}0.06`$), because it is dominated by signal energy, speaker
and channel. Three choices turn it into a usable score: contrasting the
target text with a counterfactual text on the same noised input, pooling
over speech frames only, and evaluating a narrow band of high-noise
timesteps. Our contributions are:

- •
  A synthesis-free scorer. One batched forward pass of a frozen TTS
  model yields utterance and word scores; an exact identity shows what
  the contrast removes.
- •
  A transferable timestep rule. A signal-to-noise criterion on native
  speech locates the useful timesteps, and an equal-SNR map carries them
  to a backbone with a different feature scale without retuning.
- •
  Evidence of complementarity. On unseen speakers, the score matches
  zero-shot baselines, improves them in label-free fusion, and responds
  to text edits but not to gain or silence.

## 2  Method

Figure 1: Overview. The learner’s noised mel-spectrogram $`x_{t}`$ is
passed through the same frozen TTS model under the target text $`T`$ and
a counterfactual text $`T_{\mathrm{cf}}`$. The per-frame gap between the
two velocity errors, $`\Delta\ell_{\tau}`$, is pooled into utterance or
word scores.

### 2.1  Velocity contrast as a generative GOP

Let $`x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D}`$ be the mel frames of a learner
reading text $`T`$. Flow matching with the optimal-transport path \[12\]
mixes data with noise $`\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)`$,

```math
x_{t}=(1-t)\,\varepsilon+t\,x_{1},\qquad u=x_{1}-\varepsilon,\qquad t\in(0,1), \tag{1}
```

and trains $`v_{\theta}(x_{t},t,T)`$ to predict the velocity $`u`$. The
intuition (Fig. 1) is that if the learner pronounces $`T`$ as a native
speaker would, knowing $`T`$ helps the model predict $`u`$; if the
speech departs from $`T`$, the text helps less. We measure this help
against a counterfactual text $`T_{\mathrm{cf}}`$. With
$`v_{\mathrm{tgt}}=v_{\theta}(x_{t},t,T)`$ and
$`v_{\mathrm{cf}}=v_{\theta}(x_{t},t,T_{\mathrm{cf}})`$ computed on the
_same_ $`(x_{t},t,\varepsilon)`$, the contrast at frame $`\tau`$ is

```math
\Delta\ell_{\tau}(t,\varepsilon)=\tfrac{1}{D}\big(\lVert v_{\mathrm{cf},\tau}-u_{\tau}\rVert_{2}^{2}-\lVert v_{\mathrm{tgt},\tau}-u_{\tau}\rVert_{2}^{2}\big). \tag{2}
```

Sharing the noise draw follows \[9\]: the error itself varies widely
across draws, the difference much less.

We use two counterfactuals. Uncond drops the text, which the models
support because text is randomly dropped in training for classifier-free
guidance \[17\]; it mirrors the unconditional normalization of \[11\].
Swap uses the transcript of another utterance whose length differs by at
most two characters, a competing hypothesis as in \[9\].

Eq. (2) has the shape of GOP \[2, 3\], a log-ratio of the likelihood
under the canonical text to that under an alternative. For an ideal
denoiser, log-likelihood equals, up to a constant, the negative
denoising error integrated over all noise levels \[18\], and velocity
error is denoising error rescaled,
$`\lVert\hat{x}_{1}-x_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}=(1-t)^{2}\lVert v_{\theta}-u\rVert_{2}^{2}`$.
Integrated over $`t`$ with the proper weights, the gap would thus
estimate $`\log p(x_{1}|T)-\log p(x_{1}|T_{\mathrm{cf}})`$. We use a few
timesteps with uniform weights, so our score is a likelihood-ratio
_surrogate_, not an estimate.

### 2.2  What the contrast removes

Let $`\delta_{v}=v_{\mathrm{tgt},\tau}-v_{\mathrm{cf},\tau}`$ be the
change in predicted velocity caused by the text. Expanding Eq. (2)
gives, exactly,

```math
D\,\Delta\ell_{\tau}=2\langle\delta_{v},\,u_{\tau}-v_{\mathrm{cf},\tau}\rangle-\lVert\delta_{v}\rVert_{2}^{2}. \tag{3}
```

The score is the projection of the counterfactual residual onto the
text-induced direction, minus the size of that direction. First, the
energy term $`\lVert u_{\tau}\rVert_{2}^{2}`$, which dominates the raw
error, cancels identically. Second, whatever $`v_{\mathrm{cf}}`$ already
predicts from $`x_{t}`$ (timbre, gain, channel) leaves the residual, and
the rest counts only through its component along $`\delta_{v}`$; this
part is empirical and is tested in Sec. 4.3. Third, nothing protects
$`\delta_{v}`$ itself: heavy noise or a changed speaking rate weakens
it, and the score with it.

### 2.3  Utterance and word scores

We average over $`M`$ fixed timesteps $`t_{m}`$ with paired noise draws
$`\varepsilon_{m}`$ and over the speech frames $`\mathcal{A}`$ found by
an energy-based voice activity detector (frames within 25 dB of the peak
RMS):

```math
S(x_{1},T)=\frac{1}{M|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{A}}\Delta\ell_{\tau}(t_{m},\varepsilon_{m}). \tag{4}
```

Both branches for all $`M`$ draws run as one batch of size $`2M`$, with
no ODE solver and no vocoder. Averaging the cached per-frame values over
a word’s time span gives a word score with no further TTS computation.

### 2.4  Which timesteps carry the signal

Figure 2: Correlation with human scores when the contrast is evaluated
at a single timestep. (a) MC-SNR of the contrast on 41 native
LibriSpeech clips, normalized to its peak; no L2 data are used. (b)
Pearson $`r`$ with Total on 200 unseen L2 utterances; shading marks the
F5-TTS window $`[0.05,0.14]`$. (c) The same diagnostic for ZipVoice on
60 utterances: the copied window misses the useful timesteps; the window
mapped by Eq. (6) covers them.

Which timesteps are used matters, as in image classification \[9, 10\].
As $`t{\to}1`$, $`x_{t}`$ is almost clean speech, both branches copy the
local acoustics, and $`\delta_{v}{\to}0`$. As $`t{\to}0`$, $`x_{t}`$ is
almost pure noise and carries little of the learner’s audio. A pilot on
150 labelled utterances (Sec. 3) placed the useful window for F5-TTS at
$`t\in[0.05,0.14]`$.

The same window can be found without L2 labels. On unlabelled native
LibriSpeech clips \[19\] we compute a Monte-Carlo signal-to-noise ratio
of the contrast,

```math
\mathrm{MC\text{-}SNR}(t)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x,\varepsilon}\left[\Delta\ell(x,t,\varepsilon)\right]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{Var}_{\varepsilon}\left(\Delta\ell(x,t,\varepsilon)\right)\right]}}, \tag{5}
```

the help from the text on native speech relative to the spread across
noise draws. It peaks at $`t{=}0.08`$, stays above 80% of its peak on
$`[0.05,0.15]`$, and tracks the L2 correlation curve across timesteps
(Pearson $`0.883`$ for Uncond, $`0.874`$ for Swap; Fig. 2a,b).

A window found on one backbone does not carry over literally, because
backbones scale their features differently: ZipVoice multiplies mel
features by $`0.1`$ \[16\]. With feature scale $`s`$, the
signal-to-noise amplitude ratio of $`x_{t}`$ is $`ts/(1-t)`$. Equating
it between backbones $`A`$ and $`B`$ gives

```math
t_{B}=\frac{t_{A}/s}{1-t_{A}+t_{A}/s},\qquad s=s_{B}/s_{A}, \tag{6}
```

which sends $`[0.05,0.14]`$ to $`[0.345,0.619]`$ for $`s{=}0.1`$. We
apply this window to ZipVoice with no tuning. On a 500-utterance
diagnostic subset (25 speakers), copying the F5-TTS window gives
$`r{=}{-}0.154`$, while the mapped window gives $`0.481`$ (F5-TTS on the
same subset: $`0.384`$); on the test set the mapped window reaches
$`0.624`$ (Fig. 2c).

## 4  Results

### 4.1  Can a frozen TTS model score pronunciation?

The contrast is necessary and sufficient for a usable score. Without it,
the velocity error carries no information (Table 1, $`r{\leq}0.058`$).
With it, all six configurations of the four backbones reach Total
$`r{=}0.58`$–$`0.63`$, inside the range of the zero-shot ASR and phone
baselines ($`0.55`$–$`0.67`$); alignment-free phone CTC remains the best
single model ($`0.665`$). ZipVoice, with a third of the parameters and
under 1% of the training data of F5-TTS, performs equally well through
the mapped window. Restricting ZipVoice to speech frames helps Swap
($`+0.021`$, $`p{=}0.003`$) but not significantly Uncond ($`+0.017`$,
$`p{=}0.066`$); the contrast on non-speech frames alone is weak
($`0.201`$).

Corpus-level $`r`$ is easy to obtain from length, so we control for it.
A regression on duration, character count, speaking rate and age already
gives $`r{=}0.627`$. The masked-unit count of \[6\] reaches $`0.622`$,
but it correlates $`-0.937`$ with duration and falls to $`0.025`$ once
divided by length. Our scores do not reduce to length: beyond seven
acoustic covariates they explain a further 12.2% (ZipVoice) and 10.6%
(F5-TTS) of the variance in Total, against 0.8% for the masked-unit
count. Within speakers, VAD-masked TTS scores ($`0.19`$–$`0.23`$) fall
among the baselines ($`0.14`$–$`0.29`$), with Whisper highest. All
systems are much weaker on children (TTS: $`0.30`$–$`0.38`$; baselines:
$`0.30`$–$`0.39`$).

### 4.2  Does it add to discriminative scorers?

Figure 3: Controlled interventions on 100 utterances from 15 unseen
speakers (F5-TTS v1, Swap). (a) Mean change in score in the low-$`t`$
band, with all frames or speech frames only. Dotted lines separate text

Generative scores help where more discriminative ones do not. Starting
from phone CTC plus Whisper ($`0.693`$), adding two further
discriminative models barely moves the result ($`0.698`$). Adding one
TTS model gives $`0.743`$ (F5-TTS) or $`0.745`$ (ZipVoice), and adding
both gives $`0.757`$ ($`+0.060`$ over the four-model discriminative
fusion, 95% CI $`[0.038,0.085]`$, $`p{\leq}0.001`$). Within-speaker
$`r`$ rises from $`0.303`$ to $`0.344`$ ($`p{=}0.003`$), and the gain
appears in every age band: under 12 ($`0.424{\rightarrow}0.476`$), 12–17
($`0.326{\rightarrow}0.403`$) and adults ($`0.813{\rightarrow}0.847`$).
Two TTS models alone, with no ASR, reach $`0.701`$. Rank fusion
($`0.638{\rightarrow}0.714`$) and a 150-label regression
($`0.713{\rightarrow}0.744`$) agree.

The contribution survives covariates. We regress Total on thirteen
covariates (seven acoustic, the five ASR and phone scores, and the
masked-unit count) plus fixed effects for all 95 speakers, with
speaker-clustered standard errors. Both TTS scores remain significant
(ZipVoice: $`t{=}4.24`$, $`p{=}5.3{\times}10^{-5}`$; F5-TTS:
$`t{=}3.16`$, $`p{=}0.002`$), whereas the non-speech-frame control does
not ($`t{=}{-}0.08`$).

### 4.3  What does the score respond to?

It responds to the text, not to the recording level. We edit the text or
audio of 100 utterances (paired Wilcoxon tests; Fig. 3a). Replacing one
word with another of the same length lowers the score by 40.1%
($`d{=}{-}1.40`$), two words by 64.8%, and one character by 10.6%. A
$`+6`$ dB gain leaves it unchanged ($`+0.1\%`$, $`p{=}0.78`$) and
$`-6`$ dB moves it by $`-1.3\%`$. Padding one second of silence at each
end lowers the all-frames score by 32.5%, but the speech-frame score is
unaffected ($`+2.8\%`$, $`p{=}0.13`$). The predicted limits also appear:
a 15% time-stretch costs 20–29% and pink noise at 10 dB SNR costs 88%.

Only the selected timesteps respond as expected. Word substitutions and
character edits have effect sizes 3 to 8 times larger in the low-$`t`$
band than in the other bands, and in the middle and high bands deleting
a word even raises the score ($`d{\approx}{+}0.85`$; Fig. 3b).

The choice of counterfactual is not critical. Among five counterfactual
texts (F5-TTS, 300 utterances, 15 unseen speakers), an empty string
without F5-TTS’s token-length mask still gives Total $`r{=}0.543`$, and
$`0.399`$ after partialling out length and duration. A random swap is
the one variant whose score correlates with transcript length
($`-0.240`$); matching lengths removes this ($`-0.044`$), and averaging
three matched swaps is the most accurate ($`r{=}0.619`$).

$`p{\leq}0.001`$; top-1 $`0.542`$).

On corpora without human ratings, the score separates native LibriSpeech
from low-scoring L2 utterances (AUROC $`0.978`$) and accented from
native CommonAccent speech \[27\] ($`0.976`$; Wav2Vec2: $`0.904`$).

## 5  Limitations and Conclusion

The score measures agreement with the TTS model’s prior, not phonetic
truth: in 80 clips synthesized by F5-TTS it misses the model’s own
grapheme-to-phoneme errors. It needs clean audio at a normal speaking
rate and correct casing (all-capital text drops F5-TTS to
$`r{=}0.073`$). The window was first chosen with 150 labelled
utterances, and the label-free SNR criterion was verified afterwards on
one backbone. Alone, the score does not surpass the best phone-level
baseline, and all human-rated results come from one corpus with one L1.

Within these limits, the velocity contrast of a frozen TTS model is a
synthesis-free, GOP-like pronunciation score that complements ASR-based
scorers.
