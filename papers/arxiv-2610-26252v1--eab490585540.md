###### Abstract

Expanding the vocabulary of conditional text-to-speech (TTS) models
typically requires extensive fine-tuning with paired audio, creating a
major bottleneck for low-resource and cross-lingual adaptation. To
address this, we introduce SEAM, a zero-audio initialization method that
constructs embeddings for unseen tokens from known subtokens. Unlike
standard pooling strategies that ignore sequential structure, SEAM
applies a spectral filter to preserve temporal phonetic order and uses
closed-form ridge regression to align the new embeddings with the
pretrained conditioning manifold. Across three languages and two TTS
architectures, SEAM approaches or matches baseline performance using up
to four times less training data, reducing sample complexity by 75%
compared to random initialization and mean pooling. Our approach
provides a lightweight, reproducible, and theoretically grounded
solution for vocabulary expansion in low-resource speech synthesis.

## 1 Introduction

The rapid proliferation of Agentic AI has positioned text-to-speech
(TTS) systems as foundational voice interfaces for autonomous,
multimodal agents. As these agents scale to global deployments, the
ability to rapidly adapt to new languages particularly low-resource and
under-documented ones has become a critical bottleneck. Extending
conditional TTS models to novel linguistic settings is fundamentally
constrained by vocabulary expansion: every unseen phonetic or graphemic
unit requires a corresponding embedding in the model’s conditioning
space. In practice, these tokens are initialized randomly and fine-tuned
with extensive paired audio, a requirement that is infeasible when
target-language data is scarce or costly to collect. This initialization
bottleneck severely limits cross-lingual generalization and perpetuates
a stark accessibility gap in speech technology.

We address this gap with SEAM (Spectral Embedding Alignment & Merging),
a principled, self-calibrating initialization method. SEAM treats
subtoken embeddings as a discrete signal, applies spectral low-pass
filtering to preserve low-frequency phonetic compositionality, and
projects the result onto the pretrained embedding manifold via
closed-form ridge regression. Grounded in the Lipschitz continuity of
conditional generation, our approach is designed to promote acoustic
stability and compositional faithfulness from the initialization stage.
Our contributions are: 1) A spectrally grounded merging operator that
preserves temporal phonetic structure and suppresses boundary artifacts; 2) A closed-form, self-calibration protocol that requires no
gradient-based optimization or target-language audio; 3) Data-efficiency
gains over standard initialization heuristics, enabling rapid vocabulary
expansion for low-resource TTS.

## 2 Related Work

Recent cross-lingual and low-resource TTS research has focused on
model-level adaptation strategies, including zero-shot multi-speaker
transfer via external speaker encoders Casanova et al. (2022),
cross-lingual phoneme sharing Huang et al. (2022b), and meta-learning
for rapid speaker alignment Huang et al. (2022a). While these methods
improve transferability, they typically assume access to substantial
target-language data for embedding alignment or rely on gradient-based
optimization that becomes unstable when data is extremely scarce.
Consequently, the foundational step of vocabulary expansion remains
underexplored. Current practice adapts heuristics from language
modeling, most notably decomposing unseen tokens into known subtokens
and averaging their embeddings Tikhomirov and Chernyshev (2024).
Although computationally trivial, this permutation-invariant pooling
discards the strict temporal order and articulatory dynamics essential
to speech synthesis, forcing the model to relearn sequential structure
from scratch during fine-tuning and inflating sample complexity. Our
method provides an order-preserving, spectrally grounded initialization
that aligns novel embeddings with the pretrained manifold, thereby
stabilizing optimization and drastically reducing the target-language
data required for subsequent adaptation. To our knowledge, no prior work
proposes an audio-guided initialization specifically for vocabulary
expansion in autoregressive transformer-based TTS, making the zero-audio
property of SEAM particularly important.

## 3 Method

We address the problem of initializing conditioning embeddings for novel
phonetic tokens in llm-diffusion based TTS systems. Let
$`\mathcal{V}_{\text{old}}`$ denote a pretrained vocabulary with
embedding lookup
$`E:\mathcal{V}_{\text{old}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}`$, and let
$`\Phi_{\theta}`$ be the conditional generator. For a new token
$`t\in\mathcal{V}_{\text{new}}`$ decomposable into known subtokens
$`\{s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\}\subset\mathcal{V}_{\text{old}}`$, we seek a
conditioning vector $`c_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ that satisfies: (i)
stability under the generative process, (ii) preservation of temporal
phonetic order, and (iii) alignment with the pretrained embedding
manifold. We achieve this via a spectral merging operator $`\Psi`$,
calibrated in closed form.

### 3.1 Continuity Principle

Well-trained conditional TTS generators assume that the mapping from
conditioning embeddings to audio is locally smooth: small, structured
changes in the input should produce bounded, predictable changes in the
output. To respect this property, our initialization must construct new
token embeddings as smooth functions of their constituent subtokens,
explicitly preserving their sequential order. This prevents
distributional shifts during early fine-tuning. A formal continuity
assumption and order-sensitivity analysis are provided in Appendix A.1
and A.2.

### 3.2 Spectral Merging Operator

We define a spectral merging operator
$`\Psi:\bigcup_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}`$
that maps a variable-length sequence of subtoken embeddings to a single
conditioning vector. To preserve sequential structure while satisfying
the continuity principle, we treat the embedding matrix
$`\mathbf{S}=[\mathbf{e}_{1};\dots;\mathbf{e}_{m}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}`$
as a set of $`d`$ discrete signals and apply a two-stage pipeline.

#### Fourier Downsampling.

For each embedding dimension $`j\in\{1,\dots,d\}`$, we compute the
discrete Fourier transform (DFT) $`\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j}(k)`$. In speech
representations, low-frequency components capture slow, global phonetic
compositionality, whereas high-frequency components often reflect sharp
boundary artifacts between subtokens Rabiner and Juang (1993). We
therefore apply a Gaussian low-pass filter:

```math
\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{j}(k)=\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j}(k)\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{k^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right), \tag{1}
```

retaining only the first $`K_{\mathrm{eff}}`$ frequency bins, where
$`K_{\mathrm{eff}}=\min(K,\lfloor m/2\rfloor+1)`$, to obtain
$`\widetilde{S}^{\downarrow}\in\mathbb{C}^{K_{\mathrm{eff}}\times d}`$.
In practice, we use $`K=4`$ as the default maximum number of retained
bins. This operation projects the subtoken sequence onto a smooth,
bandlimited subspace that naturally suppresses discontinuities at token
boundaries.

#### Linear Readout.

We reconstruct a conditioning vector by flattening the real and
imaginary components of the filtered coefficients into a feature vector
$`\phi(\mathbf{S})\in\mathbb{R}^{2Kd}`$, followed by a linear
projection:

```math
\Psi(\mathbf{S})=\mathbf{W}\phi(\mathbf{S})+\mathbf{b},\quad\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 2Kd},\;\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}. \tag{2}
```

Critically, by preserving the complex DFT coefficients, $`\Psi`$ remains
sensitive to phase information, which directly encodes the temporal
progression of the subtoken sequence. Unlike mean pooling, which is
permutation-invariant, under the conditions of Proposition 1, our
operator is order-sensitive: reversing the input order produces a
different output embedding.
($`\Psi(\mathbf{S})\neq\Psi(\mathbf{S}^{\text{rev}})`$ for
non-palindromic sequences). A formal order-sensitivity analysis is
provided in Appendix A.2.

### 3.3 Closed-Form Self-Calibration

To align the spectral operator $`\Psi`$ with the geometry of the
pretrained conditioning space $`\mathcal{C}`$ without requiring
target-language audio, we derive the projection parameters
$`(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{b})`$ analytically. For each token
$`t\in\mathcal{V}_{\text{old}}`$ that admits a valid subtoken
decomposition, we construct a self-supervised pair
$`(\mathbf{S}^{(i)},\mathbf{c}_{\text{target}}^{(i)}=E(t))`$.
Decompositions are obtained using the original BPE tokenizer that
generated $`\mathcal{V}_{\text{old}}`$, ensuring all constituent
subtokens belong to the pretrained vocabulary. Stacking $`N`$ such pairs
yields a feature matrix $`\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2Kd}`$ and
target matrix $`\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}`$. We solve the
ridge regression objective:

```math
\min_{\mathbf{W},\mathbf{b}}\left\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^{\top}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{b}^{\top}-\mathbf{Y}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}^{2}, \tag{3}
```

which admits the closed-form solution:

```math
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{W}^{*\top}\\
\mathbf{b}^{*\top}\end{bmatrix}=\left(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}+\lambda\mathbf{R}\right)^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}, \tag{4}
```

where $`\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=[\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{1}]`$ augments the
features with a bias column, and
$`\mathbf{R}=\operatorname{diag}(1,\dots,1,0)`$ excludes the bias term
from $`L_{2}`$ regularization. The matrix $`\mathbf{W}^{*}`$ and vector
$`\mathbf{b}^{*}`$ are obtained from the first $`2Kd`$ rows and the
final row of the solution, respectively. This calibration protocol
ensures: (i) manifold alignment by minimizing projection error onto
$`\mathcal{C}`$; (ii) order consistency by learning from linguistically
ordered subtoken sequences; and (iii) continuity preservation by
bounding the conditioning error, which directly limits acoustic
deviation via Assumption 1.

#### Non-Latin Script Handling.

For languages with non-Latin orthographies (e.g., Cyrillic), we first
transliterate the target token into an English IPA representation using
a standard mapping table. This phonetic string is then segmented into
subtokens from $`\mathcal{V}_{\text{old}}`$, yielding the sequence
$`\mathbf{s}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{s}_{m}`$. At inference, the novel
embedding is computed as
$`\mathbf{c}_{\text{new}}=\Psi^{*}(\mathbf{S}_{\text{new}})=\mathbf{W}^{*}\phi(\mathbf{S}_{\text{new}})+\mathbf{b}^{*}`$
and subsequently norm-normalized to match the statistical distribution
of the pretrained embeddings. Default values for the hyperparameters are
$`K=4`$, $`\sigma=2.0`$ and $`\lambda=1e^{-6}`$ we refer to Appendix.A.2
for more details.

SEAM Algorithm Summary. Given subtoken embeddings
$`\mathbf{s}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{s}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$, let
$`K_{\mathrm{eff}}=\min\left(K,\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor+1\right)`$:
$`\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{S}}(k)`$
$`\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{m}\mathbf{s}_{n}e^{-2\pi ik(n-1)/m},`$
$`\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\text{LP}}(k)`$
$`\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{S}}(k)\cdot e^{-k^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})},\quad k=0,\dots,K_{\mathrm{eff}}-1,`$
$`\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{s}_{1:m})`$
$`\displaystyle=\big[\operatorname{Re}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\text{LP}}(0)),\operatorname{Im}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\text{LP}}(0)),\ldots,`$
$`\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\text{LP}}(K_{\mathrm{eff}}-1)),\operatorname{Im}(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\text{LP}}(K_{\mathrm{eff}}-1))\big]^{\top},`$
$`\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{\text{new}}`$
$`\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}\,\phi(\mathbf{s}_{1:m})+\mathbf{b},`$
$`\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}`$
$`\displaystyle=\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}+\lambda\mathbf{R}\bigr)^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}.`$

#### Implementation summary.

The SEAM algorithm proceeds in two stages:

1. Learning the projector. From the old vocabulary
   $`\mathcal{V}_{\text{old}}`$, we collect self-supervised training pairs
   $`(S^{(i)},c_{\text{target}}^{(i)})`$ by decomposing each existing token
   $`t`$ into its constituent subtokens $`\{s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\}`$ (all also
   in $`\mathcal{V}_{\text{old}}`$) using the original BPE tokenizer, and
   setting $`c_{\text{target}}^{(i)}=E(t)`$. For each such pair we compute
   the spectral feature vector $`\phi(S^{(i)})`$ and then solve the ridge
   regression problem (Eq. 3) to obtain the closed-form linear projection
   $`W,b`$.

2. Embedding a new token. For a novel token $`t_{\text{new}}`$ (e.g., a
   Kazakh Cyrillic word), we first map it to an English IPA representation
   using a standard transliteration table. We then decompose this IPA
   string into subtokens present in $`\mathcal{V}_{\text{old}}`$, obtaining
   $`\mathbf{s}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{s}_{m}`$. The final embedding is computed
   as
   $`\mathbf{c}_{\text{new}}=\mathbf{W}\,\phi(\mathbf{s}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{s}_{m})+\mathbf{b}`$
   and normalized to match the average norm of the original ones.

## 4 Experiments

We evaluate SEAM across three diverse languages Russian (Slavic,
Cyrillic), Kazakh (Turkic, Cyrillic/Latin), and French (Latin) to assess
its effectiveness in low-resource and cross-lingual adaptation. All
primary experiments use Chatterbox Resemble AI (2025) as the TTS
backbone. In this paper we mainly focus on three core evaluation
dimensions: (1) sample efficiency under fixed training budgets (5h, 25h,
and 100h), quantified via automatic speech recognition metrics (CER);
and (2) perceptual quality, validated through a native-speaker
Side-By-Side comparison (SBS) study. (3) New Vocabulary size investigate
how other methods perform in comparison with SEAM when the number of new
added tokens increases. Experiments with a different architecture,
VibeVoice Peng et al. (2025), and dataset-split robustness are provided
in Appendix B.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Evaluation metrics.

We evaluate model performance using the Character Error Rate (CER),
computed via pretrained automatic speech recognition (ASR) models.
Specifically, we employ nvidia/stt_kk_ru_fastconformer_hybrid_large,
nvidia/stt_fr_fastconformer_hybrid_large_pc for Russian, Kazakh and
French; refer to Appendix B.2 for a detailed analysis of these ASR
baselines. All reported results are averaged across three independent
random seeds. Evaluation is performed on a fixed set of 100 audio
samples drawn from rulibrispeech, KazakhTTS, and
AdrienB134/french-unique-speaker-tts.

#### Baselines.

We compare against: 1) Random: $`c_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I)`$
with $`\sigma`$ matched to pretrained embedding norm.  
Mean: $`c_{t}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}E(s_{i})`$, the arithmetic mean
of subtoken embeddings.

All methods are fine-tuned under identical optimization schedules.
Implementation details and additional metrics are provided in
Appendix B.1.

#### Transliteration

We transliterate non-Latin scripts using the transliterate library
(English/Russian), qazaq_transliterator (Kazakh), and standard text
normalization (French).

### 4.3 Human Evaluation

We conduct a blind Side-by-Side (SBS) preference study with 5 native
speakers per language. Each annotator evaluates 25 synthesized
utterances, comparing audio from two conditions: (1) Model A (Ours):
SEAM-initialized embeddings, fine-tuned on 25h of speech. (2) Model B
(Baseline): mean initialization, fine-tuned on 100h of speech.

For each pair, annotators select their preferred sample based on overall
quality (naturalness + intelligibility), or indicate a tie. Evaluation
criteria and annotator instructions are provided in Appendix C. Figure 1
visualizes the SBS preference distribution. Model A (SEAM) against mean

### 4.4 Sensitivity to Vocabulary Expansion Size

We investigate the sensitivity of our method to vocabulary expansion
size by varying the number of newly added tokens
($`|\mathcal{V}_{\text{new}}|\in\{200,400,600,800,1000\}`$) and
evaluating CER after fine-tuning on 25 hours of data. As shown in
Figure 2, SEAM scales gracefully, exhibiting only marginal performance
degradation as $`|\mathcal{V}_{\text{new}}|`$ increases. In contrast,
both random and average initialization suffer sharp deterioration beyond
500 tokens, demonstrating that SEAM produces robust embeddings even
under substantial vocabulary expansion. While we constrained
$`|\mathcal{V}_{\text{new}}|=200`$ in our main experiments to ensure a
competitive comparison, larger vocabularies inherently yield shorter
token sequences and faster inference. Notably, SEAM’s relative advantage
becomes increasingly pronounced at higher expansion sizes, highlighting
its strong scalability and efficiency in practical deployment scenarios.

performance as vocabulary size increases, while mean and random
initialization degrade

### 4.5 Extended Baseline Comparison

We compare SEAM with three additional order-aware initialization
strategies on Russian with a 5-hour budget under identical optimization
schedules.

| Initialization Method  | CER (%) | WER (%) |
| ---------------------- | ------- | ------- |
| Random                 | 20.80   | 42.49   |
| Mean Pooling           | 17.53   | 36.73   |
| Position-Aware Pooling | 23.37   | 54.70   |
| Attention Pooling      | 20.14   | 50.03   |
| Learned Attention      | 15.30   | 42.86   |
| SEAM (Ours)            | 14.88   | 34.41   |

Table 2: Extended baseline comparison on Russian (5h fine-tuning). Lower
is better.

Position-Aware Pooling extracts permutation-variant statistics (mean,
max, first, last, and positionally weighted sum) and projects them via
closed-form ridge regression with $`\lambda=10^{-6}`$.  
Attention Pooling applies fixed random query vectors for scaled
dot-product attention over subtoken embeddings, followed by ridge
projection, with no learned parameters. Learned Attention trains a
single-head attention layer via AdamW (lr $`=5\times 10^{-4}`$) on the
same self-supervised pairs used by SEAM, and is the strongest
order-aware baseline. SEAM outperforms all baselines because its
spectral low-pass filter suppresses high-frequency tokenizer-boundary
artifacts, explaining the poor results of Position-Aware Pooling (CER
23.37%) and Attention Pooling (CER 20.14%), which treat all frequency
components equally. Learned Attention is competitive (CER 15.30%) but
converges poorly under the limited calibration data, yielding
approximately 10% lower cosine similarity to ground-truth embeddings and
higher MSE than SEAM’s closed-form solution. This confirms that
gradient-based attention requires substantially more data than SEAM’s
analytic ridge regression in the extreme low-resource initialization
setting.

### 4.6 Ablation: Order Sensitivity

To test whether temporal order preservation drives SEAM’s gains, we
disrupt subtoken order before spectral filtering on Russian with a
5-hour fine-tuning budget.

| Method          | CER (%) | WER (%) |
| --------------- | ------- | ------- |
| SEAM (Original) | 14.88   | 34.41   |
| SEAM (Reversed) | 19.67   | 51.99   |
| SEAM (Shuffled) | 22.91   | 53.66   |

Table 3: Order sensitivity ablation on Russian (5h).

Reversed strictly inverts the subtoken sequence
($`s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\rightarrow s_{m},\dots,s_{1}`$), while Shuffled
randomly permutes it (seed=42), completely destroying temporal order. In
both cases, spectral filtering and ridge regression are applied
identically to the original SEAM pipeline.

Reversal increases CER by 4.79 absolute points (14.88% $`\rightarrow`$
19.67%), while shuffling increases it by 8.03 points (14.88%
$`\rightarrow`$ 22.91%). Shuffling also nearly doubles WER (34.41%
$`\rightarrow`$ 53.66%). Since shuffling is more damaging than reversal,
SEAM relies not only on sequence directionality but on the precise
temporal phonetic trajectory encoded in the DFT phase. This confirms
that order-preserving spectral merging, rather than ridge alignment
alone, is the primary source of SEAM’s advantage.

## 5 Conclusion

We presented SEAM, a zero-audio initialization method for low-resource
TTS vocabulary expansion. By spectrally merging subtoken embeddings and
aligning them via closed-form regression, SEAM preserves phonetic order
and reduces sample complexity by up to 4× across languages and
architectures. While dependent on subtoken decomposability and IPA
transliteration, SEAM offers a lightweight, reproducible pathway to
stabilize early-stage TTS adaptation.

## 6 Limitations

Despite its data efficiency, SEAM operates within three primary
constraints:

- •
  Subtoken & IPA Dependency: Initialization requires reliable
  decomposition into known subtokens. For non-Latin scripts or languages
  lacking robust IPA transliteration tables, mapping errors may
  propagate to the conditioning space.
- •
  Fine-Tuning Requirement: SEAM addresses the initialization bottleneck
  but does not replace downstream adaptation; acoustic alignment still
  requires paired target-language audio but much less than other
  initialization methods.
- •
  Evaluation Variance in Extreme Low-Resource Regimes: At very small
  budgets, word-level overlap between training and test splits can
  inflate metrics non-monotonically, as observed for Kazakh.

## 7 Ethical Considerations

SEAM aims to reduce barriers for low-resource language TTS, promoting
equitable access to speech technology. However, like all voice synthesis
methods, it carries dual-use risks: malicious actors could exploit
improved initialization for unauthorized voice cloning or
disinformation. We mitigate this by releasing only embedding
initialization code not full model weights and requiring explicit
consent for any deployment involving human voices. All experiments use
publicly available, ethically sourced corpora. We encourage adopters to
follow local regulations and community guidelines, particularly when
working with under-documented languages.

## Appendix A Theoretical Overview

### A.1 Continuity Principle and Acoustic Stability

Let $`\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ denote the pretrained
conditioning space and $`\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}`$ the acoustic
output space. The TTS generator defines a stochastic mapping
$`\mathbf{x}=\Phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{z})`$, where
$`\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}`$ is a conditioning vector and
$`\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})`$ represents
diffusion or autoregressive noise. Under standard regularity conditions
for modern generative audio models, the conditional expectation operator
is Lipschitz continuous Chen et al. (2023):

###### Assumption 1 (Lipschitz Continuity).

There exists a constant $`L>0`$ such that for all
$`\mathbf{c}_{a},\mathbf{c}_{b}\in\mathcal{C}`$:

```math
\big\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}[\Phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{c}_{a},\mathbf{z})]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}[\Phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{c}_{b},\mathbf{z})]\big\|_{2}\leq L\|\mathbf{c}_{a}-\mathbf{c}_{b}\|_{2}. \tag{5}
```

Intuition for TTS. Assumption 1 formalizes a fundamental property of
stable generative models: the acoustic output space is metrically
aligned with the conditioning manifold. Consequently, large or erratic
perturbations to a token embedding can cause disproportionate acoustic
artifacts, distributional shift, or gradient instability during
fine-tuning. To mitigate this, the embedding $`\mathbf{c}_{t}`$ for a
novel token must be constructed as a smooth, structure-preserving
function of its subtoken embeddings
$`\{\mathbf{e}_{i}=E(s_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}`$. By enforcing temporal order
and spectral smoothness (Section 3.2), SEAM ensures that
$`\|\mathbf{c}_{t}-\mathbf{c}_{\text{ref}}\|_{2}`$ remains small for
phonetically similar tokens, thereby bounding the expected acoustic
deviation during early adaptation stages and stabilizing optimization
from step zero.

### A.2 Order-Sensitivity Analysis

###### Proposition 1 (Order sensitivity; sufficient conditions).

Let

```math
S=[s_{1};\ldots;s_{m}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d},\qquad m\geq 2,
```

and let

```math
S^{\mathrm{rev}}=[s_{m};\ldots;s_{1}]
```

denote the linearly reversed sequence. Let

```math
K_{\mathrm{eff}}=\min\left(K,\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right) \tag{6}
```

denote the number of unique retained DFT bins. Suppose that:

1.  1.  $`K_{\mathrm{eff}}\geq 2`$;
2.  2.  at least one retained non-DC bin is reversal-asymmetric, i.e., there
        exist $`k\in\{1,\ldots,K_{\mathrm{eff}}-1\}`$ and embedding
        dimension $`j`$ such that

    ```math
    e^{-2\pi ik(m-1)/m}\,\hat{S}_{j}[k]^{*}\neq\hat{S}_{j}[k]; \tag{7}
    ```

3.  3.  the projection matrix $`W`$ does not annihilate the corresponding
        feature difference.

Then

```math
\Psi(S^{\mathrm{rev}})\neq\Psi(S). \tag{8}
```

###### Proof.

For each embedding dimension $`j`$, define the DFT coefficient

```math
\hat{S}_{j}[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}S_{j}[n]\,e^{-2\pi ikn/m}. \tag{9}
```

For the linearly reversed sequence $`S^{\mathrm{rev}}[n]=S[m-1-n],`$ the
DFT satisfies

```math
\displaystyle\hat{S}^{\mathrm{rev}}_{j}[k] \displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}S_{j}[m-1-n]\,e^{-2\pi ikn/m}
```

```math
\displaystyle=e^{-2\pi ik(m-1)/m}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}S_{j}[r]\,e^{2\pi ikr/m}
```

```math
\displaystyle=e^{-2\pi ik(m-1)/m}\hat{S}_{j}[-k]
```

```math
\displaystyle=e^{-2\pi ik(m-1)/m}\hat{S}_{j}[k]^{*}, \tag{10}
```

where the last equality follows because each embedding dimension is
real-valued.

Thus, reversal changes the complex phase structure of the retained
spectrum. For real-valued embedding dimensions, this generally affects
the imaginary components retained by $`\phi(S)`$. If at least one
retained non-DC frequency component is reversal-asymmetric, then

```math
\phi(S^{\mathrm{rev}})\neq\phi(S). \tag{11}
```

If, in addition, $`W`$ does not annihilate this difference, i.e.,

```math
W\left(\phi(S^{\mathrm{rev}})-\phi(S)\right)\neq 0, \tag{12}
```

then

```math
\Psi(S^{\mathrm{rev}})=W\phi(S^{\mathrm{rev}})+b\neq W\phi(S)+b=\Psi(S). \tag{13}
```

∎

This is a sufficient-condition statement rather than an unconditional
guarantee. If all retained spectral coefficients are reversal-invariant,
for example when only the DC component or other perfectly symmetric
components are retained, the operator may map $`S`$ and
$`S^{\mathrm{rev}}`$ to the same conditioning vector. The proposition
therefore requires asymmetry in at least one retained non-DC frequency
bin.

#### Choice of $`K`$ and $`\sigma`$.

We set $`K=4`$ as the default maximum number of retained frequency bins
and $`\sigma=2.0`$. For short subtoken sequences, $`K`$ should be
interpreted as an upper bound: the number of unique retained DFT
coefficients is effectively

```math
K_{\mathrm{eff}}=\min\left(K,\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right).
```

The low-pass argument is strongest when the sequence length permits
$`K_{\mathrm{eff}}\ll m/2`$, so that the retained coefficients remain
well below the Nyquist region where boundary artifacts dominate. For
very short decompositions, the sufficient conditions of Proposition 1
may not hold, and the order-sensitivity claim is instead supported
empirically by the order-perturbation ablation in Table 3.

Empirical spectral analysis (Figure 3) shows that cumulative energy
exceeds 99% by $`k=3`$, indicating that the subtoken embedding
trajectory is effectively bandlimited. Retaining bins
$`k\in\{0,1,2,3\}`$ therefore preserves the dominant low-frequency
phonetic structure while strongly attenuating high-frequency boundary
artifacts. The Gaussian gain with $`\sigma=2.0`$ satisfies

```math
G(k)=\exp\left(-\frac{k^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),
```

giving

```math
G(0)=1.0,\;G(1)\approx 0.88,\;
```

```math
G(2)\approx 0.61,\;G(3)\approx 0.32,\;G(4)\approx 0.14.
```

Thus, high-frequency components are suppressed by more than $`85\%`$ by
$`k=4`$.

Figure 3: Spectral energy decay in subtoken embedding sequences.
Cumulative energy (blue) exceeds 99% by $`k=3`$, confirming bandlimited
compositionality. The Gaussian filter $`\sigma=2.0`$ (green) suppresses
high-frequency noise while retaining bins $`k\in\{0,1,2,3\}`$ (red
dashed line). This empirically validates $`(K=4,\sigma=2.0)`$ without
hyperparameter tuning.

## Appendix B Further Experiments

### B.1 Implementation details.

All experiments were conducted on a single workstation equipped with an
NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU (24 GB VRAM) and an Intel Core i9-14900K CPU (24
cores, 32 logical processors, 3.2 GHz base clock). Training pipelines
were implemented using the Hugging Face transformers and accelerate
ecosystems with mixed-precision (bf16) enabled.

#### Chatterbox (0.5B) Fine-Tuning.

We perform full-parameter fine-tuning of the English-only Chatterbox
base model. Optimization uses AdamW with a learning rate of
$`5\times 10^{-4}`$, 10% warmup steps, and a cosine decay schedule. The
per-device batch size is 8 with 8 gradient accumulation steps (effective
batch size 64) over 1 epoch. Data loading utilizes 8 workers with pinned
memory disabled to optimize CPU–GPU transfer. All automatic metrics are
computed post-hoc on the held-out test set.

#### VibeVoice (1.5B) Fine-Tuning.

For cross-architecture validation, we adapt VibeVoice using Low-Rank
Adaptation (LoRA). We apply LoRA to all linear projection layers
(q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, o_proj, gate_proj, up_proj, down_proj) with
rank $`r=128`$ and scaling factor $`\alpha=256`$. The diffusion head is
frozen , and loss weights are set to 1.4 for the diffusion objective and
0.04 for cross-entropy. Training uses a learning rate of
$`5\times 10^{-5}`$, a cosine scheduler with 3% warmup, maximum gradient
norm of 0.8, and voice prompt dropout of 0.2. The effective batch size
is 16 (4 per device $`\times`$ 4 accumulation steps) over 3 epochs, with
a DDPM set to 10 steps to stabilize diffusion sampling.

All runs use fixed random seeds for reproducibility, and hyperparameters
are kept constant across languages to ensure fair comparison.

#### Ridge Regularization.

The ridge regression regularization coefficient in Eq. 3 is set to
$`\lambda=10^{-6}`$ for all experiments across all languages and
architectures. This value provides minimal regularization sufficient to
ensure numerical stability of the closed-form solution (Eq. 4) without
biasing the projection away from the least-squares optimum. No
language-specific or architecture-specific tuning of $`\lambda`$ was
performed.

#### IPA Transliteration Setup.

For languages with non-Latin orthographies, SEAM requires mapping
target-language tokens to an English IPA representation before subtoken
decomposition. We use the following tools:

- •
  Russian: The transliterate Python library maps Cyrillic graphemes to
  their English IPA equivalents. Standard Russian phonological rules
  (vowel reduction, consonant palatalization) are applied during
  normalization.
- •
  Kazakh: The qazaq_transliterator library handles Cyrillic-to-Latin
  conversion, followed by IPA mapping. Kazakh-specific vowel harmony and
  consonant assimilation rules are respected during transliteration.
- •
  French: Standard orthographic-to-phonemic normalization is applied,
  handling liaison, elision, and nasal vowel conventions.

The resulting IPA strings are segmented using the original BPE tokenizer
that generated $`\mathcal{V}_{\text{old}}`$, ensuring all constituent
subtokens belong to the pretrained vocabulary.

### B.5 Ablation: Sensitivity to Dataset Splits

To evaluate SEAM’s sensitivity to training data composition, we conduct
an ablation study on the Kazakh corpus using three independent random
splits. Each split preserves the same dataset size, speaker diversity,
and phonemic coverage while varying the exact utterance selection. All
models are fine-tuned under identical optimization schedules and
evaluated on a fixed held-out test set. Performance remains highly
consistent across partitions: the WER ranges from 14.97% to 16.51%
(mean: 15.61%, $`\sigma=0.66`$) and the CER ranges from 4.79% to 5.67%
(mean: 5.27%, $`\sigma=0.36`$). The low standard deviations
($`\sigma^{2}_{\text{WER}}=0.43`$, $`\sigma^{2}_{\text{CER}}=0.13`$)
confirm that SEAM’s performance is robust to partition-level variations
in the training data. The low standard deviation across splits indicates
that SEAM’s initialization is robust to dataset composition and does not
rely on high-quality subsets.

#### Non-Monotonic Kazakh Trend (5h $`\rightarrow`$ 25h).

Table 1 reveals a counter-intuitive increase in CER from the 5h to the
25h budget for Random and Mean initialization on Kazakh (e.g., Random:
11.62% $`\rightarrow`$ 32.60%). We clarify that this non-monotonic
behavior is an artifact of word-level (token-level) lexical overlap
between the training and test splits, rather than a failure of model
capacity. Although we enforce strict utterance-level disjointness
between training and test sets, in extremely low-resource regimes the
specific utterances randomly sampled for the 5h subset may contain a
higher density of words that also appear in the 100-utterance test set,
artificially inflating performance metrics. Conversely, the 25h subset
introduces greater lexical diversity and reduced overlap with the test
set, making evaluation inherently harder. Critically, this pattern is
consistent across all initialization methods, SEAM also exhibits a
modest increase (4.99% $`\rightarrow`$ 5.26%) confirming that the trend
is driven by data composition rather than any method-specific
deficiency. At 100h, all methods stabilize, and SEAM achieves its lowest
Kazakh CER of 4.39%, well above the ASR error floor of 0.98% (Table 4).

### B.7 Ablation: Audio-Guided Initialization

To assess whether incorporating target-language audio during
initialization improves upon SEAM’s zero-audio design, we conduct an
ablation comparing zero-audio SEAM against an audio-guided variant. We
note that, to the best of our knowledge, no prior published work
proposes an independent audio-guided initialization method specifically
for vocabulary expansion in autoregressive transformer-based TTS. We
therefore position this experiment as an ablation validating the
zero-audio property, rather than a comparison against an existing
baseline.

The Audio-Guided variant proceeds in two stages: (1) SEAM initialization
is applied identically to the zero-audio setting; (2) the newly
initialized embeddings and the LM head are further refined via AdamW
optimization on 1 hour of calibration audio from the target language
before the main fine-tuning phase begins.

#### Results.

Table 6 reports the training loss dynamics for both variants on Russian
(5h budget).

| Variant           | Initial Loss | Final Loss   |
| ----------------- | ------------ | ------------ |
| SEAM (Zero-Audio) | 10.2         | $`\sim`$4.0  |
| Audio-Guided      | 7.2          | $`\sim`$10.0 |

Table 6: Training loss dynamics: Zero-audio SEAM vs. Audio-Guided
variant (Russian, 5h). The audio-guided variant exhibits severe
optimization instability.

While audio-guided refinement achieves a lower initial training loss
(7.2 vs. 10.2), it introduces severe optimization instability. The
refinement step causes embedding norms to inflate to atypical values,
distorting the geometric alignment established by SEAM’s closed-form
calibration. When the main fine-tuning phase begins from this
checkpoint, the loss spikes from 7.2 to 15.0 before slowly recovering to
approximately 10.0 still substantially worse than zero-audio SEAM’s
converged loss of $`\sim`$4.0. In contrast, zero-audio SEAM converges
smoothly and monotonically from 10.2 to $`\sim`$4.0.

This result demonstrates that SEAM’s zero-audio initialization is not
merely a convenience but a deliberate design choice. The closed-form
ridge regression produces embeddings that are geometrically
well-calibrated to the pretrained manifold; subsequent audio-guided
refinement disrupts this calibration, causing the model to fall into a
poor local minimum that requires significantly more data to escape. The
absence of any independent audio-based initialization baseline in the
literature further underscores that SEAM’s zero-audio property fills a
genuine methodological gap.

## Appendix C Side-by-Side (SBS) Human Evaluation Guidelines

Annotators evaluated synthesized audio samples using a Side-by-Side
(SBS) preference framework, focusing on two orthogonal dimensions:
Realism (acoustic naturalness) and Linguistic Correctness (fidelity to
orthographic and phonological norms of the target language). Below are
the standardized criteria and decision protocols provided to annotators.

### C.1 Dimension 1: Realism (Naturalness)

Annotators rated each sample on a 5-point scale. The following anchors
guided the assessment:

- •
  5 (Excellent): Indistinguishable from professional human speech.
  Prosody is fluid, timbre is natural, and paralinguistic cues (e.g.,
  breath, micro-pauses) are appropriately integrated.
- •
  4 (Good): Largely natural but exhibits minor artifacts, such as slight
  pitch irregularities, mild robotic timbre, or subtle prosodic
  mismatches (e.g., flattened intonation on interrogatives).
  Comprehension remains effortless.
- •
  3 (Fair): Noticeable synthesis artifacts or pacing irregularities
  (e.g., unnatural pauses, fragmented rhythm), but speech remains fully
  intelligible. Pronunciation may occasionally mismatch syntactic
  context.
- •
  2 (Poor): Severe articulation errors, phoneme hallucinations, or
  repetitive stuttering. Speech is strained or partially unintelligible,
  significantly impeding comprehension.
- •
  1 (Bad): Highly robotic, garbled, or completely unintelligible.
  Contains nonsensical syllable sequences or arbitrary breaks that
  destroy syntactic coherence.

### C.2 Dimension 2: Linguistic Correctness

Linguistic correctness measures adherence to the phonological,
morphological, and orthographic conventions of the target language
(French, Kazakh, or Russian). Annotators identified and penalized the
following error categories:

- •
  Phoneme & Stress Accuracy:
  - –
    Russian/Kazakh: Correct realization of vowel reduction (akanye),
    palatalization (soft vs. hard consonants), and lexical stress
    placement. Misplaced stress that changes word meaning is a critical
    error.
  - –
    French: Correct liaison, elision, and nasal vowel production.
    Failure to apply obligatory liaison (e.g., les_amis) or incorrect
    nasalization (e.g., bon vs. bonne) is penalized.
  - –
    Kazakh: Accurate pronunciation of vowel harmony and consonant
    assimilation rules. Errors in front/back vowel alignment within a
    word constitute a linguistic error.

- •
  Lexical & Orthographic Fidelity:
  - –
    Any substitution, omission, or addition of words compared to the
    reference text is penalized.
  - –
    Numbers, abbreviations, and proper nouns must be pronounced
    according to target-language conventions (e.g., Russian: “1995”
    $`\rightarrow`$ tysyacha devyatsot devyanosto pyat’; French: “M.
    Dupont” $`\rightarrow`$ monsieur Dupont; Kazakh: “2024”
    $`\rightarrow`$ eki myń jıyrma tört).
  - –
    For Cyrillic scripts (Russian/Kazakh), the model must distinguish
    visually similar letters with distinct phonemes.

- •
  Prosody & Intonation:
  - –
    Intonation contours must match sentence type (e.g., rising pitch for
    questions in French/Russian; declarative fall for statements).
  - –
    Sentence-level stress and phrasing should reflect syntactic
    boundaries. Arbitrary pauses within phrases or unnatural emphasis on
    function words are penalized.

### C.3 Scoring and Preference Decision

For each side-by-side trial, annotators assessed both Model A and Model
B against the above criteria. The final preference was determined as
follows:

- •
  If one model demonstrated significantly higher realism and/or fewer
  linguistic errors, it was selected as preferred.
- •
  If both models exhibited comparable levels of quality and error rates,
  the annotator selected “Tie.”
- •
  Each linguistic error (phoneme, stress, lexical, or prosodic) resulted
  in a proportional downgrade in the overall quality assessment for that
  sample.
- •
  Annotators were instructed to prioritize intelligibility over minor
  prosodic artifacts when making close calls.

#### Annotator Instructions.

1.  1.  Listen to both samples (A and B) for the same input text before
        making a decision.
2.  2.  Evaluate each dimension independently, then aggregate for the final
        preference.
3.  3.  If audio quality issues (e.g., clipping, background noise) affect
        both samples equally, ignore them and focus on synthesis artifacts.
4.  4.  When in doubt, select “Tie” rather than guessing.

confirms the robustness of the perceptual quality gains.

| Kazakh  | 65.1%            | \[55.6, 73.6\] | 0.002     |

## Appendix D Extended Related Work

#### Cross-Lingual and Zero-Shot TTS Adaptation.

Recent advances in zero-shot and cross-lingual TTS have dramatically
expanded speaker and language coverage. Models such as VALL-E,
VoiceCraft, and CosyVoice leverage discrete acoustic tokenizers and
autoregressive or diffusion-based decoding to synthesize speech from
short prompts Wang et al. (2023); Peng et al. (2024); Du et al. (2024).
While highly effective for well-resourced languages, their adaptation to
under-documented languages remains constrained by the need for paired
text-speech data or language-specific phoneme inventories .
Cross-lingual transfer methods typically rely on shared phoneme spaces,
speaker-invariant conditioning, or meta-learning for rapid alignment
Casanova et al. (2022). However, these approaches still require
substantial target-language fine-tuning to stabilize novel token
embeddings, leaving the foundational step of vocabulary expansion
underexplored.

#### Vocabulary Expansion and Subtoken Initialization.

The standard pipeline for handling out-of-vocabulary (OOV) tokens
decomposes unseen units into known subtokens via byte-pair encoding
(BPE) or phoneme-grapheme mappings, then initializes embeddings using
random draws or arithmetic mean pooling Tikhomirov and Chernyshev
(2024). While computationally trivial, mean pooling is
permutation-invariant and discards the sequential articulatory structure
essential to speech synthesis.

Positioning. SEAM bridges these research threads by introducing a
unified, zero-audio pipeline that combines spectral low-pass filtering
with closed-form ridge regression. Unlike iterative alignment methods,
SEAM computes the optimal projection analytically, ensuring geometric
stability from step zero. By explicitly retaining DFT phase information,
