# FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates

Jiaqi Li¹    Chaoren Wang¹    Xiaohai Tian²    Mingjie Chen¹    Xinyu
Liang¹    Xu Li¹ Affiliation: Yufan Lin¹, Junwen Qiu¹, Jun Zhang², Lu

###### Abstract

Spoken language models (SLMs) extend LLMs to speech input and output,
but existing systems use fixed frame rates (e.g., 25 or 12.5 Hz),
overlooking speech’s time-varying information density and limiting
inference-time quality–speed tradeoffs. Recent dynamic-frame-rate audio
tokenizers enable very low average frame rates and controllability, yet
had not been applied to SLMs. We introduce FlexiSLM, the first SLM with
dynamic, controllable frame rates, using pretrained FlexiCodec for
dynamic speech output tokens. It integrates this representation into a
multi-task speech-to-speech SLM, extends it with input-side frame
compression, and adds direct frame-rate conditioning for accurate
control during inference. FlexiSLM outperforms fixed-frame-rate 7B
models, including Qwen2.5-Omni and Kimi-Audio, at 12.5 and 6.25 Hz; it
can be steered down to 4.0 Hz, and at 6.25 Hz roughly halves inference
time relative to 12.5 Hz while retaining strong speech-to-speech
quality. Audio samples:
[https://flexislm.github.io](https://flexislm.github.io); code and data:
[https://github.com/AmphionTeam/FlexiSLM](https://github.com/AmphionTeam/FlexiSLM).

## 1 Introduction

Spoken language models (SLMs) have emerged as a unified framework for
speech understanding and generation, covering speech-to-speech dialogue,
automatic speech recognition (ASR), text-to-speech (TTS), and audio
understanding ([Xu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib59); [Ding et al.,
2025](#bib.bib15); [Zeng et al., 2024](#bib.bib65); [Défossez et al.,
2024](#bib.bib14)). These models jointly model text and speech with a
large language model (LLM) backbone, but typically represent speech at a
fixed frame rate¹¹ 1 The frame rate is the number of discrete or
continuous speech-encoding frames used to represent one second of audio;
lower frame rates use fewer tokens., e.g., $`25`$ Hz for Qwen2.5-Omni
([Xu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib59)) and $`12.5`$ Hz for Kimi-Audio ([Ding
et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)). Fixed-rate tokenization ignores the
time-varying information density of speech, wasting compute on silences
and other information-sparse segments. It also prevents inference-time
quality–speed control across devices, networks, and deployment budgets.

FlexiCodec ([Li et al., 2025b](#bib.bib33)) addresses these limitations
with a dynamic-frame-rate codec that uses frame merging to achieve
strong audio reconstruction quality at an average of $`6.25`$ Hz, while
allowing the average frame rate to be steered at inference time.
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")
illustrates this concept. However, FlexiCodec was validated only in a
0.3B-parameter TTS pipeline. Extending dynamic-rate coding to end-to-end
SLMs is more challenging, but also more valuable: SLMs are more
compute-intensive, and their broader capability set makes frame-rate
controllability useful for heterogeneous deployment budgets.

Motivated by these benefits, we develop Flexible Spoken Language Model
(FlexiSLM), the first spoken language model with dynamic and
controllable frame rates. FlexiSLM is a thinker-talker speech-in,
speech-out model: for output, we reuse FlexiCodec as the Talker
prediction target for dynamic-frame-rate tokens and reuse its pretrained
flow-matching audio decoder for reconstruction; for input, we mirror
FlexiCodec’s dynamic-rate strategy by training a new frame merging
module from scratch to dynamically compress input speech
representations. Building on this dynamic-rate representation, we
introduce a direct control method that lets the user specify the average
output frame rate, allowing one FlexiSLM to operate at any frame rate
$`\leq 12.5`$ Hz without retraining. Our contributions are summarized as
follows:

  Dynamic frame-rate SLM framework and validation. We introduce
  FlexiSLM, the first dynamic frame-rate SLM framework, with dynamic

  including speech QA and audio understanding. Experiments confirm

## 2 Related Work

##### Speech Tokenization.

Speech tokenization converts continuous audio into discrete tokens
suitable for speech language modeling. Early neural audio codecs such as
SoundStream ([Zeghidour et al., 2021](#bib.bib64)) and EnCodec
([Défossez et al., 2022](#bib.bib13)) use residual vector quantization
(RVQ) to produce acoustic tokens at fixed frame rates (e.g., 50 Hz or
75 Hz), prioritizing reconstruction fidelity. Semantic tokens derived
from self-supervised models like HuBERT ([Hsu et al., 2021](#bib.bib24))
capture linguistic content and are increasingly used in speech language
modeling ([Borsos et al., 2023](#bib.bib7); [Li et al.,
2024](#bib.bib34); [Du et al., 2024a](#bib.bib17); [Ding et al.,
2025](#bib.bib15)). Recent work has pushed toward more efficient
representations while maintaining high audio quality: single-codebook
approaches (50–75 Hz) ([Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib58); [Ji et al.,
2024](#bib.bib27)) and semantic-enhanced codecs (12.5–50 Hz) ([Li
et al., 2025a](#bib.bib32); [Zhang et al., 2023](#bib.bib68)).

Recent work has explored dynamic frame rates, leveraging the temporal
sparsity of speech so that lower average frame rates reduce the
computational cost of speech language models. FlexiCodec ([Li et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib33)), the tokenizer used in this work, merges $`12.5`$ Hz
semantic features based on similarity to achieve dynamic-rate
tokenization at an average of $`6.25`$ Hz. The authors of FlexiCodec
also demonstrate controllable frame-rate tokenization and TTS. Other
dynamic-frame-rate works, including CodecSlime ([Wang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib57)), TFC ([Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib67)), and
VARSTok ([Zheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib70)), explore higher average frame
rates from 18.75 Hz to 40 Hz. However, dynamic-rate codecs have not been
applied within a spoken language model framework, where low frame rates
and frame-rate controllability offer larger practical benefits.

##### Spoken Language Models.

Spoken language models (SLMs) are general-purpose speech processing
systems ([Arora et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)). Analogous to text LLMs, they
can follow natural-language instructions across diverse speech tasks.
Recent end-to-end SLMs extend text-based LLMs to directly comprehend and
generate speech. A dominant design follows a three-stage paradigm: a
pretrained speech encoder extracts acoustic features, which condition a
decoder-only LLM, followed by an additional transformer module or
prediction head that predicts speech tokens ([Wang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib56); [Xu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib59); [Ding et al.,
2025](#bib.bib15)). Kimi-Audio ([Ding et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)) models
parallel speech-text at 12.5 Hz using a separate LM head for speech
tokens. Qwen2.5-Omni ([Xu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib59)) adopts a
thinker–talker architecture operating at 25 Hz, whose talker module
predicts speech tokens. Active research areas include full-duplex
capability (e.g., Moshi ([Défossez et al., 2024](#bib.bib14))),
interleaved speech-text sequences (e.g., GLM-4-Voice ([Zeng et al.,
2024](#bib.bib65))), low-frame-rate audio tokenization for SLMs (e.g.,
Mimi ([Défossez et al., 2024](#bib.bib14))), and dual-resolution speech
representations (e.g., Fun-Audio-Chat/DrVoice ([Team et al.,
2025](#bib.bib54); [Tan et al., 2025](#bib.bib52)) and
Mimo-Audio ([Zhang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib66)) group speech into 5 Hz or
6.25 Hz sequences).

## 3 Method

### 3.1 Architecture Overview

Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Speech Tokenization. ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")
illustrates the overall architecture of FlexiSLM, a parallel speech-text
model with the following components:

![Refer to caption](2606.31247v3/talker_detail.png)

Figure 3: Talker Transformer input-output structure. At each position,
the Talker jointly predicts two parallel streams: FlexiCodec FSQ token
codes and per-token frame lengths for dynamic-rate decoding. Delays are
applied to provide lookahead; tokens in the user’s turn and the
text-generation delay region are masked from the loss.

Audio Encoder. This module encodes the user’s speech into a semantically
rich continuous representation suitable for LLM understanding. We adopt
the pretrained Qwen2.5-Omni audio encoder ([Xu et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib59)), which extracts 25 Hz continuous speech features
from waveforms.

Frame Merging Module. This module compresses the number of frames in a
sequence. It appears twice in our model: (1) on the input side, it
reduces the 25 Hz continuous features from the Audio Encoder to a
dynamic-rate sequence $`\leq`$12.5 Hz; (2) inside the pretrained
FlexiCodec audio tokenizer, it merges 12.5 Hz ASR features before
quantization. The input-side module contains newly initialized
parameters and is trained end-to-end within FlexiSLM, while the
output-side module is frozen. Both instances share the same merging
mechanism. We describe this module in
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Frame Merging Module ‣ 3 Method ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates").

LLM Backbone (Thinker). We use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct ([Yang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib63)) as initialization. This LLM has also been used in
Qwen2.5-Omni and Kimi-Audio to initialize their backbones.

FlexiCodec Audio Tokenizer. We use the open-source pretrained FlexiCodec
to obtain discrete speech tokens as the prediction target of FlexiSLM’s
Talker module. As illustrated in
Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Speech Tokenization. ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")(a),
the codec discretizes each frame with Finite Scalar Quantization
(FSQ ([Mentzer et al., 2023](#bib.bib41))); each token is paired with a
frame length attribute for audio reconstruction. We use FlexiCodec’s
semantic tokens and omit its RVQ acoustic tokens.
Appendix [J](#A10 "Appendix J Audio Tokenizer Details ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")
provides more details.

Talker Transformer. The Talker decodes the Thinker LLM’s hidden states
and outputs into FlexiCodec’s dynamic-frame-rate speech tokens.

- •
  Input: As shown in
  Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.1 Architecture Overview ‣ 3 Method ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates"),
  its input sequence runs over the entire user + assistant context. At
  each position, the Talker input embedding is projected from the
  concatenation of (1) the backbone LLM’s last-layer hidden state, (2) a
  sinusoidal embedding of the target frame rate
  (Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Controllable Frame Rate ‣ 3 Method ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")),
  and (3) the embeddings of the previously emitted speech and frame
  length tokens.
- •
  Output: The Talker produces two parallel output streams: FlexiCodec
  FSQ codes and their associated frame lengths, enabling dynamic-rate
  output. The Talker uses two output LM heads to predict the streams in
  parallel.
- •
  Token delay: As shown in
  Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.1 Architecture Overview ‣ 3 Method ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates"),
  the Talker’s FSQ token stream is delayed by 5 tokens from the text
  stream. This provides a small lookahead that prevents speech from
  preceding its corresponding text ([Du et al., 2024b](#bib.bib18)). The
  frame length tokens are delayed by one additional position, allowing
  the model to predict a frame’s duration after knowing its
  corresponding speech token ([Li et al., 2025b](#bib.bib33)).

Audio Decoder. The audio decoder is a frozen non-autoregressive (NAR)
flow-matching ([Lipman et al., 2022](#bib.bib38)) Transformer that
decodes mel spectrograms from the speech tokens. A Vocos ([Siuzdak,
2023](#bib.bib50)) neural vocoder then converts the mel spectrogram into
24 kHz speech. We use the pretrained flow-matching model and vocoder
from the open-source FlexiCodec repository. We provide additional
details in
Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I Flow-Matching Audio Decoder Details ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates").

Talker-to-Thinker Connection. In addition to the standard cascaded
Thinker-to-Talker information flow ([Xu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib59)),
FlexiSLM contains an optional Talker-to-Thinker connection ([Tan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib52)) (the red dashed arrow in
Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Speech Tokenization. ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates"))
that feeds the Talker’s previously emitted speech-token embeddings back
into the Thinker LLM Backbone at the next step. This gives the Thinker
LLM explicit access to what has already been spoken. The connection
projects the concatenation of the Talker’s embeddings (speech code +
frame length) and the text embedding into the Thinker LLM’s hidden
state. We can disable this connection by zeroing out the contribution of
the Talker’s embeddings in the concatenation.

### 3.2 Frame Merging Module

As shown in
Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Speech Tokenization. ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")(b),
the Frame Merging Module adaptively compresses a fixed-rate semantic
feature sequence by merging adjacent frames that carry redundant
information. Given a sequence of feature vectors
$`\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T}`$ at a base frame
rate, we compute the cosine similarity between consecutive frames:
$`s_{t}=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{t+1}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{t}\|\,\|\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\|},\quad t=1,\ldots,T{-}1.`$
If $`s_{t}`$ exceeds a merging threshold $`\tau`$, frames
$`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ and $`\mathbf{x}_{t+1}`$ are grouped and their
average is computed. This process runs greedily from left to right, with
contiguous high-similarity frames merged into a single averaged
representation.

After merging, each group produces an averaged feature
$`\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{k}`$ and a frame length attribute $`l_{k}`$ denoting
the number of original frames in the group. We interleave the original
and averaged features to form an augmented sequence, which is processed
by a lightweight Transformer with local attention. Finally, we retrieve
the representations at positions corresponding to the averaged features,
yielding the merged sequence with associated frame lengths.

### 3.3 Controllable Frame Rate

A key feature of FlexiSLM is its ability to control the output frame
rate at inference time, enabling a single deployed model to operate
across a range of compute budgets without retraining. For a
dynamic-frame-rate sequence, the average frame rate is defined as
$`\frac{\text{Total number of frames after merging}}{\text{Audio duration in seconds}}`$.
We first describe a baseline threshold-based strategy and its
limitations, then introduce our proposed direct frame-rate control. We
focus on output frame-rate control; input frame rate is controlled by
computing the target number of merged frames from the desired rate and
selecting a merging threshold per utterance to match it.

##### Merging Threshold Control (Baseline).

A straightforward approach is to control the merging threshold $`\tau`$:
a higher $`\tau`$ merges fewer frames (higher frame rate), while a lower
$`\tau`$ merges more (lower frame rate). This approach is used in
FlexiCodec-TTS ([Li et al., 2025b](#bib.bib33)). However, it provides
only _indirect_ control, with several limitations: (1) the resulting
frame rate varies significantly across utterances and datasets, making
the speedup difficult to predict
(Table [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.2 Main Evaluation Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates"));
(2) it is a one-to-many mapping: a single threshold maps to a wide
distribution of average frame rates, increasing modeling complexity; and
(3) it is unintuitive for users unfamiliar with the model architecture.

##### Direct Frame-Rate Control.

To overcome these limitations, we directly condition the Talker
Transformer and the Frame Merging Module on the target average frame
rate. During training, we randomly sample merging thresholds, compute
the resulting average frame rate for each utterance, and feed this
empirical rate as a conditioning signal. At inference, the user simply
specifies the desired frame rate.

To enable continuous control over a range of frame rates, we encode the
scalar frame rate $`r`$ using sinusoidal positional encoding ([Vaswani
et al., 2017](#bib.bib55)):
$`\text{PE}(r)=[\sin(r\omega_{1}),\cos(r\omega_{1}),\ldots,\sin(r\omega_{d}),\cos(r\omega_{d})]`$,
where the $`\omega_{i}`$ are frequency bases derived from $`10{,}000`$.
This encoding serves as one of the inputs to the Talker. Each position
in the Talker input sequence receives the same frame-rate condition.

### 3.4 Training Procedure

Training proceeds in three stages:

##### Stage 1: Talker Pre-training.

In this stage, we freeze the LLM backbone and train only the randomly
initialized Talker. We train FlexiSLM end to end with TTS data. This
stage uses English samples from Emilia ([He et al., 2024](#bib.bib23))
and Multilingual LibriSpeech (MLS) ([Pratap et al., 2020](#bib.bib46)),
totaling about 100K training hours. The Talker-to-Thinker connection is
disabled in Stages 1 and 2.

##### Stage 2: Multi-Task LoRA Fine-Tuning.

In this stage, we activate the speech input-side Frame Merging Module,
Thinker, and Talker. LoRA ([Hu et al., 2021](#bib.bib25)) is applied to
the Thinker. The Frame Merging Module is randomly initialized. We train
on a mixture of speech processing tasks.

shows the distribution of the data sources in Stage 2. A central
component is FlexiSLM-Data, a speech-to-speech dialogue corpus
constructed for this work. FlexiSLM-Data is distilled from a 30B
Qwen3-Omni ([Xu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib60)) SLM, with data sourced from
a wide range of datasets. We detail our data construction process in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Construction of FlexiSLM-Data ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates").

##### Stage 3: Full Fine-Tuning.

This stage further unlocks the backbone’s capacity and improves speech
perception. It continues from the Stage 2 checkpoint and uses the same
data, with two differences. First, we perform full-parameter training
after merging the LoRA updates back into the target LLM weight matrices.
Second, we enable and train the Talker-to-Thinker connection.

### 3.5 Training Loss and Configuration

Our model is trained end to end with a weighted cross-entropy loss over
multiple token streams. The total loss is:
$`\mathcal{L}=\lambda_{\text{text}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{text}}+\lambda_{\text{speech}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{speech}}+\lambda_{\text{speech\_len}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{speech\_len}}`$,
where $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{text}}`$, $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{speech}}`$,
and $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{speech\_len}}`$ are the cross-entropy losses
for text tokens, FlexiCodec speech codes, and per-token speech frame
lengths, respectively. We set $`\lambda_{\text{text}}=2`$ and
$`\lambda_{\text{speech}}=\lambda_{\text{speech\_len}}=1`$. For
non-speech sequences, $`\mathcal{L}_{\text{speech}}`$ and
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{speech\_len}}`$ are set to 0.

We train FlexiSLM on 24 A100 80G GPUs. During training, we randomly
sample an input frame rate target from $`\mathcal{U}(4,12.5)`$ Hz and a
FlexiCodec merging threshold $`\tau\sim\mathcal{U}(0.85,1.0)`$ to
support multiple frame rates. The Talker has 630M parameters; the Audio
Encoder has 640M parameters; each Merging Transformer has 20M
parameters. We discuss more training details in
Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Additional Model and Training Details ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates").

### 4.3 Frame Rate Controllability

#### 4.3.1 Frame Rate Control Accuracy

We evaluate how accurately FlexiSLM can control the average output frame
rate. We compare two variants that differ only in their control
mechanism: merging-threshold control and direct frame-rate control.
Table [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.2 Main Evaluation Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")
reports the resulting average output frame rates across benchmark
datasets. We focus on the output side because the input frame rate is
controlled deterministically and therefore does not require a separate
analysis.

$`\bullet`$ Direct frame-rate control is more accurate than threshold
control. Merging threshold control exhibits high per-utterance variance:
for example, targeting roughly 8 Hz via $`\tau{=}0.90`$ yields a wide
range of frame rates ($`3.91`$~$`10.74`$ Hz, $`\sigma{\approx}0.70`$)
and varying averages across datasets ($`7.91`$~$`8.34`$ Hz). In
contrast, direct frame-rate control, used in our final FlexiSLM, closely
matches the requested average rates, with errors below $`0.1`$ Hz across
all settings.

$`\bullet`$ When might merging threshold control still be preferable?
One could argue that a dynamic frame-rate system should adapt to
utterance-level redundancy, and that the higher variance of threshold
control reflects this adaptivity. We view the two strategies as
complementary. Threshold control fixes the _compression intensity_
applied to local frame pairs, allowing the resulting frame rate to vary
with the redundancy of each utterance. Direct frame-rate control instead
fixes the _average frame rate_ of each utterance and adjusts the
effective compression intensity accordingly. Thus, threshold control may
be preferable when the goal is to apply a consistent compression
criterion across utterances.

For FlexiSLM, we adopt direct frame-rate control for two reasons. First,
it reduces the modeling ambiguity faced by the Talker. As discussed in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Controllable Frame Rate ‣ 3 Method ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates"),
a single merging threshold can correspond to a wide distribution of
output frame rates, making the threshold condition an indirect and
ambiguous generation target. Second, predictable compute budgets are
important for deployment. In practical SLM serving, committing to a
target frame rate, and therefore to an approximate real-time factor, is
useful.

#### 4.3.2 Frame Rate and Inference Efficiency

Table [5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 4.3.1 Frame Rate Control Accuracy ‣ 4.3 Frame Rate Controllability ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")
reports full-response real-time factor (RTF), and
Table [6](#S4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ 4.3.1 Frame Rate Control Accuracy ‣ 4.3 Frame Rate Controllability ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")
complements this metric with first-$`1`$ s-chunk latency and a
component-wise latency breakdown. All measurements are conducted on a
single A100 GPU with batch size $`1`$, averaged over $`30`$ runs on an
OpenAudioBench subset, using the same audio prompts for all FlexiSLM
configurations (average prompt duration: $`7.7`$ s). We also report an
approximate model-side compute estimate based on each module’s sequence
length and parameter count. This estimate excludes the
non-autoregressive flow-matching audio decoder, whose parallel decoding
cost is not directly reflected in the measured RTF. For chunk-based
inference, we modify FlexiSLM to emit decoded audio immediately after
the tokens for each $`1`$ s audio chunk are predicted.

$`\bullet`$ Output frame rate is the main driver of speed and latency
improvements. Reducing the output frame rate from $`12.5`$ to
$`6.25`$ Hz nearly halves RTF ($`1.17\to 0.59`$ with $`12.5`$ Hz input;
$`1.09\to 0.57`$ with $`6.25`$ Hz input) and reduces first-$`1`$ s-chunk
latency from $`1.62`$ s to $`1.21`$ s ($`0.75\times`$) or from
$`1.60`$ s to $`1.17`$ s ($`0.72\times`$). In contrast, reducing only
the input frame rate brings much smaller gains, indicating that
output-side autoregressive speech generation dominates the efficiency
benefit. The component-wise latency breakdown in
Table [6](#S4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ 4.3.1 Frame Rate Control Accuracy ‣ 4.3 Frame Rate Controllability ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")
shows that lowering the output frame rate from $`12.5`$ to $`6.25`$ Hz
reduces the latency shares of both the Thinker and Talker. This is
expected: completing the first one-second chunk requires fewer output
tokens, which reduces the number of Thinker and Talker forward passes.
The first-chunk latency reduction ($`0.72`$–$`0.75\times`$), however, is
smaller than the full-response RTF reduction (about $`0.5\times`$),
likely because fixed overheads and audio-decoder latency account for a
larger fraction of first-chunk generation, whereas full-response
generation is dominated more strongly by repeated Thinker–Talker
autoregressive computation.

$`\bullet`$ FlexiSLM offers controllable deployment tradeoffs, covering
a wide range of operating points. By varying the input and output frame
rates, the same model covers operating points from $`1.17`$ to $`0.57`$
RTF, and first-chunk latencies from $`1.62`$ s down to $`1.17`$ s,
without retraining. This gives FlexiSLM a practical speed-quality
tradeoff control that fixed-rate SLMs do not provide. These first-chunk
latency reductions further highlight the deployment value of output
frame-rate control, since first-chunk latency is closely tied to
perceived responsiveness in conversational systems.

$`\bullet`$ Comparison with Qwen2.5-Omni-7B in RTF. At the
$`12.5`$/$`12.5`$ Hz setting, FlexiSLM is $`1.3\times`$ faster than
Qwen2.5-Omni-7B ($`1.17`$ vs. $`1.57`$ RTF). When the FlexiSLM output
frame rate is reduced to $`6.25`$ Hz, the speedup increases to
$`2.7\times`$. The TFLOPs estimates show a similar trend: FlexiSLM at
$`12.5`$/$`12.5`$ Hz requires less estimated model-side compute than
Qwen2.5-Omni-7B ($`4.57`$ vs. $`5.26`$), and the $`6.25`$/$`6.25`$ Hz
setting further reduces this cost to $`2.73`$ TFLOPs. This gain is
smaller than the raw output-rate ratio between Qwen2.5-Omni and FlexiSLM
($`50`$ vs. $`6.25`$ Hz) because the two systems use different inference
structures: FlexiSLM’s Thinker processes a speech-length sequence to
maintain bidirectional Thinker–Talker information flow, whereas
Qwen2.5-Omni’s Thinker stops after text generation. FlexiSLM’s design
trades part of the rate-reduction speedup for stronger speech-aware
response generation and is also aligned with future full-duplex and
interactive SLM settings.

from $`63.0`$ to $`61.0`$ and LibriSpeech TTS WER increasing by a

from $`68.7`$ to $`67.7`$, suggesting that lower-quality output
tokenization can hurt the LLM backbone during end-to-end training.

evaluate dynamic frame rates on the input side, we retrain FlexiSLM with
uniform input merging and evaluate it at $`6.25`$ Hz. Replacing dynamic
input merging with uniform merging reduces s2t Overall AVG from $`68.7`$
to $`67.5`$ and worsens ASR test-other WER from $`7.20`$ to $`7.97`$,
while s2s Overall AVG and ASR test-clean remain nearly unchanged. This
suggests that dynamic input merging is most beneficial for tasks that
require finer-grained speech understanding, such as instruction
following and harder ASR cases.

frame-rate conditioning with merging-threshold control degrades s2s and
TTS performance, while leaving s2t and ASR nearly unchanged. We
attribute this to the ambiguity of threshold control discussed in

This less predictable target likely slows Talker convergence under our
limited training budget, leading to weaker generation quality.

## 6 Conclusion

We presented FlexiSLM, to our knowledge the first spoken language model
with dynamic and controllable frame rates. FlexiSLM applies dynamic
frame rates to both speech input and output, and introduces direct
frame-rate conditioning so that a single model operates from $`12.5`$
down to $`4.0`$ Hz without retraining. Experiments show that FlexiSLM
achieves the best speech-to-speech QA score among 7B SLMs at $`12.5`$ Hz
and remains stronger in s2s than fixed-rate 7B baselines at $`6.25`$ Hz.
More aggressive $`5.0`$ and $`4.0`$ Hz settings provide additional
low-token operating points with larger but smooth quality degradation.
Halving the output frame rate halves inference time with only a
$`1.0`$-point s2s loss. FlexiSLM demonstrates a practical paradigm for
efficient spoken language models.

## Limitations

FlexiSLM has several limitations. First, we have not explored
post-training techniques such as preference optimization, which may
further improve response quality, alignment, and speech naturalness.
Second, the current model is non-streaming on both input and output;
adapting the frame-merging module and audio decoder to causal or
chunk-wise operation is an important step toward real-time conversation.
Third, our training data has limited coverage of reasoning-intensive
tasks, multi-turn dialogue, and multiple-choice formats, which may limit
generalization to these scenarios. Finally, the human listening test in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Human Listening Test ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")
shows that FlexiSLM still has room to improve output audio quality
compared with the Qwen-Omni series, and the additional ablation in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Additional Ablation Study ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")
shows that the controllable dynamic-frame-rate model does not yet match
the strongest non-controllable grouped-patching variant. We discuss
future work in
Appendix [K](#A11 "Appendix K Future Work ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates").

This section details the pipeline for building a large-scale
speech-to-speech dialogue dataset, which we call FlexiSLM-Data.
Instructions for downloading FlexiSLM-Data are provided in our
open-source repository. The construction pipeline has the following
steps:

- •
  Prompt collection. We collect text prompts from public QA,
  instruction-following, and dialogue datasets
  (Table [8](#A1.T8 "Table 8 ‣ Appendix A Construction of FlexiSLM-Data ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")).
  For multi-turn datasets, we use the user’s first-turn utterance as the
  prompt, skipping samples whose first turn is a generic greeting (e.g.,
  “Hello”).
- •
  Response generation. We use Qwen3-Omni ([Xu et al.,
  2025b](#bib.bib60)) to generate text responses for these prompts. We
  choose a spoken language model rather than a text-only LLM because
  Qwen3-Omni produces speech-friendly responses that are short,
  conversational, and free of formatting that does not transfer to
  speech (e.g., bullet points, code blocks, or long enumerations). We
  feed the _text_ version of the prompt rather than its synthesized
  speech, since text-input responses are typically more accurate.
- •
  Speech synthesis. The generated responses are synthesized into speech
  by Qwen3-TTS ([Hu et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)) with a fixed speaker
  ‘‘Ryan’’. The speech prompts are synthesized using Fish-Audio TTS³³ 3
  [https://huggingface.co/fishaudio/s1-mini](https://huggingface.co/fishaudio/s1-mini) ([Liao
  et al., 2024](#bib.bib37)), with speaker prompts randomly sampled from
  English Emilia utterances longer than 5 seconds ([He et al.,
  2024](#bib.bib23)). This produces 4M samples with a total audio
  duration of 30K hours.
- •
  Quality filtering. We apply format-based filtering to remove samples
  containing code, formulas, excessive punctuation, or non-target
  languages; correctness filtering using the DeepSeek-V4-Flash API; and
  ASR-based filtering with Whisper-medium to discard cases with WER
  greater than 20%.
- •
  Final scale. After filtering, we obtain $`1.4`$M speech-to-speech
  dialogue samples, totaling $`9.9`$K hours of speech ($`2.7`$K hours of
  user prompts and $`7.2`$K hours of assistant responses). Sample data
  are shown as text transcriptions in
  Table [20](#A12.T20 "Table 20 ‣ Appendix L SLM Prompt Template ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates").

FlexiSLM-Data is currently limited to single-turn interactions. In the
future, we plan to extend it to multi-turn natural dialogue.

$`\bullet`$ FlexiSLM supports different LLM backbones; performance

default text LLM-based backbone provides a stronger knowledge and
reasoning prior than an SLM backbone. In contrast, the speech-pretrained
SLM backbone requires less adaptation for speech recognition.

$`\bullet`$ The input merging Transformer is important. Removing the
merging transformer and feeding raw merged features directly to the LLM

features into a representation the LLM can consume.

in Stage 2 instead of Stage 3 produces worse results than the baseline.

information that the limited LoRA capacity cannot absorb, creating a
destabilizing feedback loop between the partially trained Talker and the
frozen backbone. This justifies deferring the connection to Stage 3,
where full-parameter fine-tuning provides sufficient capacity to
integrate the bidirectional signal.

## Appendix C Human Listening Test

To assess the perceptual speech quality of FlexiSLM, we conduct a human
mean opinion score (MOS) evaluation comparing three FlexiSLM operating
points with three representative SLM baselines.

##### Protocol.

Eight listeners with experience in speech or audio research evaluated
the audio outputs of six SLMs on the same 10 spoken-dialogue prompts.
Listeners were instructed to judge only the perceptual audio quality of
each response, independent of factual correctness, using a five-point
scale from 1 to 5 in increments of 0.5. System identities were hidden,
and presentation order was randomized. Listeners rated three dimensions:
(1) naturalness, covering rhythm, intonation, expressiveness, and
absence of robotic delivery; (2) fidelity, reflecting overall audio
quality; and (3) intelligibility, measuring how easily the spoken words
can be understood. We report the mean and 95% confidence interval,
computed as $`1.96`$ times the standard error over the 80 ratings for
each system and dimension.

$`\bullet`$ The Qwen systems achieve the highest MOS scores, followed by
FlexiSLM. The two Qwen systems receive the highest overall scores, with
their largest advantage over FlexiSLM appearing in fidelity. Several
factors may contribute to their higher audio quality: frame rate,
training data, and RL post-training. In terms of frame rate,
Qwen2.5-Omni generates at a substantially higher output frame rate
(50 Hz) than FlexiSLM, which provides more temporal capacity for
acoustic detail. Frame rate alone cannot explain Qwen3-Omni’s advantage,
however, because it operates at $`12.5`$ Hz. Its larger model size,
larger-scale audio training corpus, and reinforcement-learning
post-training likely improve generation quality. Although detailed data
statistics are not publicly specified, both Qwen systems appear to use
substantially more high-quality audio data, whereas FlexiSLM’s dialog
data is synthetic and comparatively limited. Both Qwen systems also use
RL for speech-generation quality, whereas FlexiSLM does not. This
comparison suggests concrete directions for improving FlexiSLM: scaling
curated speech-generation data and adding rewards for naturalness,
fidelity, prosody, and stability are promising ways to improve FlexiSLM
without giving up its low-frame-rate efficiency.

$`\bullet`$ Reducing FlexiSLM from $`12.5`$ to $`6.25`$ Hz causes no
clear perceptual loss. The two settings have nearly identical overall
scores ($`4.22`$ and $`4.21`$), and their confidence intervals overlap.
Together with the approximately $`2{\times}`$ lower RTF in
Table [5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 4.3.1 Frame Rate Control Accuracy ‣ 4.3 Frame Rate Controllability ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates"),
this result supports $`6.25`$ Hz as a favorable efficiency-quality
operating point.

$`\bullet`$ At $`4.0`$ Hz, speech primarily loses naturalness and
fidelity. FlexiSLM at $`4.0`$ Hz shows only a modest decrease in
intelligibility ($`4.21`$), but naturalness and fidelity drop more
substantially to $`3.69`$ and $`3.66`$, respectively. This pattern
complements the very-low-frame-rate analysis in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Visualization and Analysis of Low Frame Rates ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates"),
indicating that coarse temporal resolution and more difficult
frame-length prediction primarily weaken prosodic and acoustic detail.

##### Recommended operating points.

Considering the visualization and low-frame-rate analysis in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Visualization and Analysis of Low Frame Rates ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates"),
together with the human listening results above, we recommend selecting
the target frame rate according to deployment requirements. For the
input frame rate, we recommend keeping $`12.5`$ Hz for dialogue tasks
and using task-specific low frame rates for audio-understanding tasks
(Table [17](#A6.T17 "Table 17 ‣ Error analysis. ‣ Appendix F Speech Generation Quality Evaluation ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")).
For the output frame rate, the $`12.5`$ Hz setting is appropriate when
stable speech generation, word-level intelligibility, and answer
correctness are the main priorities, since it gives the strongest
benchmark results and avoids duration prediction errors from frame
merging. The $`6.25`$ Hz setting is our general recommendation for
spoken-dialogue serving: it provides a good quality-efficiency balance,
nearly halves the output-token rate and RTF relative to $`12.5`$ Hz, and
preserves comparable spoken-QA scores and human listening quality.
Intermediate settings such as $`8.0`$ Hz can be used for intermediate
budgets. By contrast, $`5.0`$ and especially $`4.0`$ Hz should be
treated as aggressive compression modes rather than default user-facing
settings. They may be suitable for edge-device or resource-constrained
scenarios where latency, throughput, or token budget is more important
than high naturalness and fidelity.

##### Visualization analysis.

Compared with the fixed $`12.5`$ Hz representation, FlexiSLM uses fewer
and longer-duration speech tokens as the requested frame rate decreases,
while preserving a subword-level alignment with the generated utterance.
This example illustrates how frame-rate controllability changes the
temporal granularity of the speech-token sequence rather than simply
truncating or uniformly dropping output content.

##### Visualization analysis for very low frame rates.

The visualization also helps explain the degradation observed at
aggressive operating points such as $`5.0`$ and $`4.0`$ Hz. At moderate
compression levels such as $`8.0`$ and $`6.25`$ Hz, the generated token
sequence still covers the full response and keeps a mostly subword- or
phoneme-level alignment with the intended utterance, which explains the
modest audio quality decrease at these intermediate operating points. At
$`4.0`$ Hz, however, each speech token must cover a much longer span,
and the generated sequence becomes too coarse to preserve some local
phonetic details (e.g., “Space Teles”, “in nineteen”). This suggests
that very-low-frame-rate degradation may be driven primarily by phonetic
information loss and the increased difficulty of assigning accurate
frame lengths to long-duration tokens. Reduced temporal resolution may
also weaken prosodic detail, since fewer tokens are available to model
fine-grained duration and transition patterns.

merging is needed and the length prediction is trivial ($`0`$ loss,
$`100\%`$ top-1 accuracy), whereas the length-token loss increases from
$`0.68`$ at $`6.25`$ Hz to $`0.78`$ at $`5.0`$ Hz and $`0.93`$ at
$`4.0`$ Hz, with top-1 accuracy dropping from $`29\%`$ to $`23\%`$ and
$`19\%`$. Rows with the same output frame rate but different input frame
rates have identical losses, indicating that this diagnostic mainly
captures an output-side duration-modeling difficulty rather than an
input-side ASR effect. Together with the visualization, these results
suggest that very-low-frame-rate degradation is partly caused by
unstable or inaccurate frame-length prediction: each token must
represent a longer acoustic span, making it harder to assign correct
durations and preserve fine-grained phonetic and prosodic details.

## Appendix H Additional Model and Training Details

##### Talker Transformer configuration.

The Talker module of FlexiSLM uses the same Transformer implementation
as the Thinker LLM. It has a hidden size of 1280, 20 Transformer layers,
8 attention heads, and an intermediate size of 5,120. FlexiCodec and its
SenseVoice ([An et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)) semantic encoder have 450M
parameters in total.

##### Training configuration.

We use AdamW optimizer ([Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017](#bib.bib39)) for
training. The Talker Transformer, Talker-to-Thinker connection, and
Frame Merging Module use a learning rate of $`2\times 10^{-4}`$ in each
stage. The LLM backbone uses a learning rate of $`2\times 10^{-5}`$ in
Stage 2 and $`1\times 10^{-6}`$ in Stage 3. We apply LoRA to the LLM
backbone with rank $`r=32`$ and LoRA scaling parameter $`\alpha=64`$.
For each stage, we use a 5% linear learning-rate warmup followed by
cosine decay to 1/10 of the maximum learning rate. All stages use a
dynamic batch size of 3K tokens per GPU. Stages 1 and 2 use Distributed
Data Parallel; Stage 3 uses DeepSpeed ZeRO-2 ([Rasley et al.,
2020](#bib.bib49)). Stage 1 spans $`300\text{K}`$ steps, Stage 2 spans
$`240\text{K}`$ steps (3 epochs), and Stage 3 spans $`160\text{K}`$
steps. Gradient accumulation is set to $`1`$ in Stages 1 and 2, and to
$`2`$ in Stage 3.

## Appendix I Flow-Matching Audio Decoder Details

FlexiSLM uses the audio decoder provided in the open-source FlexiCodec
repository⁴⁴ 4
[https://github.com/amphionteam/flexicodec](https://github.com/amphionteam/flexicodec).
The decoder is a two-stage pipeline: a VoiceBox ([Le et al.,
2023](#bib.bib30))-style NAR flow-matching model followed by a vocoder.

The NAR flow-matching model takes FlexiCodec’s FSQ semantic tokens and
associated frame lengths as input, and generates mel spectrograms
conditioned on a short reference audio prompt that defines the target
speaker identity. The model input is a $`12.5`$ Hz fixed-frame-rate
sequence interpolated to 50 Hz to match the mel-spectrogram rate. To
construct the $`12.5`$ Hz sequence, each FlexiCodec FSQ semantic token
is repeated according to its frame length. The flow-matching model
learns a velocity field $`v_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{t},t)`$ that transports
samples from a Gaussian prior $`\mathbf{z}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)`$ to
the mel-spectrogram distribution $`\mathbf{z}_{1}`$ under the
conditioning inputs. The mel spectrograms are then converted to 24 kHz
waveforms by a pretrained Vocos ([Siuzdak, 2023](#bib.bib50)) vocoder.

## Appendix J Audio Tokenizer Details

We use pretrained FlexiCodec ([Li et al., 2025b](#bib.bib33)) to encode
dynamic-frame-rate speech tokens as FlexiSLM’s training target. Our
Talker Transformer predicts FlexiCodec’s FSQ tokens and the associated
per-token frame lengths, and the flow-matching decoder
(Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I Flow-Matching Audio Decoder Details ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates"))
synthesizes the final waveform. FlexiCodec follows an
encoder-quantizer-decoder architecture and has the following two
features:

##### ASR-Feature-Assisted Dual-Stream Encoding.

FlexiCodec encodes FSQ semantic tokens through a semantic-encoding
branch. The semantic-encoding branch encodes SenseVoice ([An et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2)) ASR features at $`12.5`$ Hz, while a parallel
acoustic-encoding branch encodes RVQ tokens from raw waveforms. In
FlexiSLM, we use only the semantic branch; acoustic reconstruction is
handled by the flow-matching audio decoder.

##### Dynamic Frame Rate via Frame Merging.

The Frame Merging Module is applied to the $`12.5`$ Hz ASR features.
Adjacent frames whose cosine similarity exceeds a threshold $`\tau`$ are
merged, producing a variable-frame-length sequence. The merged features
are then quantized via FSQ (in the semantic-encoding branch) or RVQ (in
the acoustic-encoding branch). By varying $`\tau`$ from 0.8 to 1.0,
FlexiCodec supports average frame rates from $`3`$ Hz to $`12.5`$ Hz. At
$`12.5`$ Hz, no merging occurs and FlexiCodec behaves as a standard
fixed-rate codec.

## Appendix K Future Work

While FlexiSLM validates dynamic and controllable frame rates inside an
SLM, several directions remain open and motivate our future work:
$`\bullet`$ Reasoning, multi-turn, and tool-use coverage. FlexiSLM-Data
is dominated by single-turn factual and instruction-following responses.
Extending the data construction pipeline to multi-turn dialogues,
chain-of-thought reasoning, and tool-augmented interactions would
broaden the model’s capability surface while keeping the same
dynamic-rate backbone.

$`\bullet`$ Streaming and on-device deployment. The current FlexiSLM is
non-streaming on both input and output. Adapting the Frame Merging
Module and audio decoder to causal or chunk-based streaming, combined
with FlexiSLM’s frame-rate controllability, would enable real-time
conversation and let a single model match heterogeneous compute budgets
from cloud to edge devices.

$`\bullet`$ Full-duplex SLMs. The Talker-to-Thinker connection
introduced in
Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Architecture Overview ‣ 3 Method ‣ FlexiSLM: A Spoken Language Model with Dynamic and Controllable Frame Rates")
routes the actually-rendered speech tokens back to the Thinker LLM,
which gives the Thinker a coherent acoustic context across turns. This
is a natural foundation for full-duplex SLMs that can listen, think, and
speak concurrently. However, dynamic-frame-rate compression in streaming
full-duplex scenarios may be challenging, as the merging strategies for
the user stream and the assistant stream may differ.

$`\bullet`$ Post-training for spoken dialogue. We have not yet explored
RLHF ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib43)) or DPO ([Rafailov et al.,
2023](#bib.bib48)) on top of FlexiSLM. Spoken-dialogue-specific reward
models, including prosody, naturalness, and turn-taking signals, are
likely to improve response quality beyond what supervised distillation
alone can provide.

$`\bullet`$ Unified low-rate audio modeling. The frame-merging and
frame-rate-conditioning machinery is not specific to speech. Applying
the same controllable dynamic-rate recipe to general audio (music,
environmental sound, mixed audio) is a promising direction toward
unified, efficient audio language models.

$`\bullet`$ Improved TTS with reasoning. Since FlexiSLM is capable of
both understanding and generation, future models can leverage
speech-understanding capabilities as well as the LLM’s reasoning ability
to enhance TTS.
