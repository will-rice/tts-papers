# ArtifactNet: Detecting AI-Generated Music via Forensic Residual Physics

Heewon Oh ^(†)^(†)thanks: Heewon Oh is with Intrect / MARTE Lab, Dongguk
University, Seoul, South Korea (e-mail: heewon.oh@intrect.io). An
earlier version of this work appeared as an April 2026 preprint. This
work has been submitted to the IEEE for possible publication. Copyright
may be transferred without notice, after which this version may no
longer be accessible.

###### Abstract

Detecting artificially generated music requires distinguishing
synthesis-related traces from musical content and artifacts introduced
by audio distribution. We present ArtifactNet, a compact framework based
on learned forensic residuals. A pretrained music source separator
supplies residual targets during training, but the final system replaces
it with ArtifactUNet, a task-directed bounded-mask extractor. Seven
channels describe harmonic and percussive residual structure, their
balance, and temporal variation, and a lightweight convolutional
classifier produces recording scores. The extractor and classifier
contain 4.03 million parameters. We evaluate the retained checkpoints on
a frozen, recording-level test containing 534 generated and 479 real
recordings after conservative source-family and identity exclusions from
the reconstructed training lineage. At the pre-established threshold of
0.225, ArtifactNet obtains F1=0.9671, 93.63% recall, and no observed
false positive; AUROC and average precision are 0.9982 and 0.9988.
Released SpecTTTra and CLAM checkpoints are evaluated on the same files
under their model-specific frontends. A diagnostic further shows that
the released CLAM head changes substantially with execution batch
composition. In a separate paired four-codec study on 100 real and 100
generated recordings, codec-aware extraction reduces the mean score
range from 0.276 to 0.033 for real music but increases it from 0.035 to
0.249 for generated music; Opus recall falls from 96% to 74%. The
results support compact residual detection while exposing
class-dependent codec and implementation sensitivities.

AI-generated music detection, audio forensics, residual extraction,

## I Introduction

### I-A AI-Generated Music Detection as an Audio-Forensics Problem

The proliferation of AI-generated music motivates detection methods that
rely on acoustic evidence of audio generation rather than surface-level
pattern matching. Existing approaches include representation learning,
which adapts pretrained encoders to labeled music, and autoencoder
fingerprinting, which examines reconstruction or decoder-specific
patterns \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2), [3](#bib.bib3),
[4](#bib.bib4)\]. Their performance depends on the generators and
recording conditions represented during training. A detector must also
distinguish synthesis-related traces from artifacts introduced by
conventional audio compression.

Afchar _et al._ identify generalization to unseen decoders and
robustness to audio transformations as important obstacles
\[[3](#bib.bib3)\]. These obstacles motivate a representation that makes
residual structure explicit, while retaining enough flexibility to learn
which structure matters for detection. High accuracy within a familiar
distribution is useful, but does not establish that a detector
recognizes synthesis independently of musical content or recording
provenance.

### I-B Motivation for a Residual-Based Approach

We approach this problem through source-separation residuals. A source
separator reconstructs a mixture as a collection of estimated stems. The
component not reconstructed by their sum contains separation error and
fine acoustic structure. Our starting observation was that these
residuals can differ between generated music and human recordings. We
use the term _forensic residual amplification_ for the representational
role of exposing such differences, rather than for a claim that the
residual consists exclusively of synthesis artifacts.

Neural audio codecs offer one motivation for this approach. EnCodec and
DAC use residual vector quantization (RVQ), in which successive
codebooks approximate a continuous latent representation
\[[5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\]. Finite representations and learned
decoders can introduce characteristic structure. However, RVQ is not
common to every generator: continuous autoencoders also support music
generation \[[7](#bib.bib7)\], and the internal architectures of
proprietary systems are not established by their outputs alone. Our
hypothesis concerns learnable residual differences, not a universal RVQ
signature.

Running a full source separator solely to obtain this residual is costly
and optimizes a different task. We therefore develop a compact extractor
that learns a bounded component of the input spectrogram. This changes
the role of source separation: it supplies an initial training signal,
while the final extractor is refined for discrimination.

### I-C Contributions and Scope

This paper develops ArtifactNet around three connected contributions.
First, ArtifactUNet replaces full source separation at inference with
bounded-mask residual extraction. Second, harmonic–percussive
decomposition and temporal features form a seven-channel residual
representation for a lightweight classifier. Third, staged learning
combines residual distillation, classifier-guided refinement, and
distribution-codec augmentation. The contribution lies in this
task-directed design and its empirical behavior, rather than in claiming
U-Net, HPSS, or distillation as new algorithms.

An earlier version of ArtifactNet appeared in our April 2026 preprint
\[[8](#bib.bib8)\]. Here, we focus on the residual-extraction method and
its development. The primary evaluation reruns ArtifactNet and two
released baselines on a common, recording-level test split separated
from the retained training lineage. Historical SONICS and Melody or
Machine (MoM) results are retained only as developmental evidence, and a
separate paired experiment examines transformation sensitivity.
Benchmark construction and subsequent production decision systems are
outside the scope of this paper.

## II Related Work

### II-A AI-Generated Music Detection and Neural Decoder Artifacts

Autoencoder fingerprinting learns differences between original audio and
its reconstruction, or examines structure associated with decoder
architectures \[[3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4)\]. CLAM combines
pretrained audio representations with contrastive training
\[[1](#bib.bib1)\], while SpecTTTra models temporal structure in the
SONICS setting \[[2](#bib.bib2)\]. These methods provide different
inductive biases; a residual-based method must be assessed against them
under explicit data and inference protocols.

Related work published after the original ArtifactNet preprint includes
MusicDET, which uses frequency-guided normalizing flows trained on real
music \[[9](#bib.bib9)\], and work on token representations and CoMoE
under generator shift \[[10](#bib.bib10)\]. MusicDET’s real-only
objective differs from the supervised classification used here. These
newer methods are relevant alternatives, but the present experiments do
not establish a matched numerical comparison with them.

### II-B Source Separation and Residual Representations

Demucs reconstructs musical sources using a learned separation model
\[[11](#bib.bib11), [12](#bib.bib12)\]. In ArtifactNet, its
reconstruction residual becomes a teacher representation for a smaller
network. Knowledge distillation \[[13](#bib.bib13)\] transfers that
representation without retaining the full teacher at inference, and the
U-Net architecture \[[14](#bib.bib14)\] provides a spatially structured
mask predictor.

HPSS separates sustained and transient spectrogram structure using
median filtering \[[15](#bib.bib15)\]. We apply it to an extracted
residual rather than to the original mixture alone. The resulting
features describe what remains after task-directed extraction. This use
of an intermediate residual is related in motivation to residual and
noise-feature approaches in other forensic domains \[[16](#bib.bib16)\];
it does not imply that mechanisms established for images transfer
unchanged to audio.

### II-C Compact Detectors and Codec Robustness

A compact detector offers a lower parameter budget, but size alone does
not determine robustness or runtime. Conventional MP3, AAC, and Opus
encoding may alter the same spectral details used for synthesis
detection. Codec-aware training must therefore expose the detector to
distribution codecs across both classes. Its effectiveness is an
empirical question, distinct from whether the synthesis model itself
uses a neural codec.

## III From Source-Separation Residuals to a Forensic Detector

### III-A Initial Observations with Demucs Residuals

The initial pipeline used a pretrained separator to estimate musical
stems, formed a residual from the difference between the mixture and the
reconstructed mixture, and classified a spectral representation of that
residual. This construction provided a way to examine acoustic structure
that a separator trained for musical source reconstruction did not fully
explain. It also made the separation model responsible for most of the
computation, even though the detector required neither individual stems
nor high-quality source reconstruction.

The development objective consequently became narrower: retain a
residual representation useful for discrimination while removing the
full separator from inference. A direct residual predictor could address
the computational cost, but without a structural restriction it could
simply return musical content that a downstream classifier found
convenient. This motivated a multiplicative mask and an explicit upper
bound.

### III-B Limitations of Reconstruction-Only and Joint Training

Reconstruction fidelity and discriminative usefulness are different
objectives. A student can imitate a teacher residual without improving
the distinction between real and generated music. Conversely, optimizing
only classification can encourage shortcuts unrelated to the desired
residual representation. Early development experiments also exhibited
instability when extractor and classifier were updated together. We use
these observations as design motivation, rather than as a controlled
quantitative ablation.

Staged training separates these objectives. Residual distillation
initializes the extractor from a source-separation target. A trained
classifier then provides a fixed discriminative reference while the
extractor is refined. The classifier is not being learned simultaneously
in that refinement step. This sequence preserves a role for the residual
teacher while allowing the final representation to depart from literal
teacher reconstruction when discrimination requires it.

### III-C Design Requirements for Compact Residual Detection

Three requirements follow from this development. The extractor should
produce a constrained component of the observed signal; the classifier
should receive explicit descriptions of residual structure; and training
should account for distribution codecs that affect both generated and
real recordings. ArtifactNet implements these requirements with bounded
masking, harmonic–percussive and temporal channels, and codec-augmented
training, respectively.

The development path should not be read as a claim that every earlier
model differed in only one factor. Data coverage, classifier variants,
and training procedures changed during development. Quantitative results
below are attached to named evaluation snapshots, while the development
narrative explains the method’s design choices.

## IV ArtifactNet

### IV-A Bounded-Mask ArtifactUNet

ArtifactNet processes audio through three stages: forensic residual
extraction via ArtifactUNet, seven-channel HPSS feature computation, and
classification via a compact CNN with recording-level aggregation.
Figure [1](#S4.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ IV-A Bounded-Mask ArtifactUNet ‣ IV ArtifactNet ‣ ArtifactNet: Detecting AI-Generated Music via Forensic Residual Physics")
summarizes this inference path.

![Refer to caption](2604.16254v3/fig1_pipeline.png)

Fig. 1: ArtifactNet inference. Source separation supplies a training
signal but is not required in this inference path.

Unlike direct residual generation, ArtifactUNet predicts a
multiplicative mask on the input STFT magnitude. For waveform $`x`$, let
$`X=|\operatorname{STFT}(x)|`$. The residual magnitude is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
M_{\theta}(X)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(Z_{\theta}(X)),\qquad R=M_{\theta}(X)\odot X,
``` |  | (1) |

where $`Z_{\theta}`$ denotes the U-Net output before sigmoid scaling.
The mask lies in $`[0,0.5]`$, so the residual magnitude in each bin is
at most half the input magnitude. The corresponding squared-magnitude
bound is one quarter of the input bin power. This restriction prevents
exact identity-magnitude transmission; it does not by itself rule out
all content shortcuts.

ArtifactUNet follows an encoder–decoder architecture with skip
connections and a gated residual bottleneck. It contains 3,603,937
parameters. Its output has the same time–frequency dimensions as the
input magnitude, enabling a residual representation without running the
full source separator. The term “forensic residual” refers to this
constrained, learned representation, not to a uniquely identified
physical noise source.

### IV-B Residual Distillation and Classifier-Guided Refinement

Phase 1 uses source-separation residuals as teacher targets. The
distillation objective combines residual reconstruction with spectral
reconstruction terms, as described in the initial method
\[[8](#bib.bib8)\]. This stage transfers the structural form of
residuals rather than the complete stem-separation task. The teacher is
used during training, not as a component of the final inference network.

Phase 2 refines the extractor under the supervision of a trained, frozen
classifier. Classification gradients are propagated through a
differentiable spectral feature transform to the extractor. Writing
$`C_{\phi}`$ for that fixed classifier and $`\Phi`$ for the feature
transform used in the refinement stage, the discriminative objective has
the form

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}=\operatorname{BCE}\bigl(C_{\phi}(\Phi(R)),y\bigr),
``` |  | (2) |

with updates applied to the extractor parameters $`\theta`$, not to
$`\phi`$. This expresses the frozen-classifier principle without
implying that all developmental stages used the same classifier
topology. The seven-channel classifier described below is the final
detector head.

The stages serve different purposes: distillation establishes a residual
representation, and classification-guided refinement makes it useful for
detection. The staged procedure is a design response to the competing
objectives observed during development; it is not a general convergence
guarantee for extractor–classifier systems.

### IV-C Harmonic–Percussive Seven-Channel Features

We apply HPSS to the extracted residual magnitude spectrogram,
decomposing it into harmonic ($`H`$) and percussive ($`P`$) components
through median filtering along time and frequency axes. Log-mel
representations of $`R`$, $`H`$, and $`P`$, together with temporal
differences and harmonic–percussive balance, yield the seven channels in
Table [I](#S4.T1 "TABLE I ‣ IV-C Harmonic–Percussive Seven-Channel Features ‣ IV ArtifactNet ‣ ArtifactNet: Detecting AI-Generated Music via Forensic Residual Physics").

| Channel |  Representation                                    |
|---------|----------------------------------------------------|
| 1       |  Residual log-mel spectrum, $`L_{R}`$              |
| 2       |  Harmonic log-mel spectrum, $`L_{H}`$              |
| 3       |  Percussive log-mel spectrum, $`L_{P}`$            |
| 4       |  First temporal difference, $`\Delta L_{R}`$       |
| 5       |  Second temporal difference, $`\Delta^{2}L_{R}`$   |
| 6       |  Harmonic–percussive balance, $`L_{H}-L_{P}`$      |
| 7       |  Absolute temporal difference, $`|\Delta L_{R}|`$  |

TABLE I: Seven-channel forensic representation.

The harmonic channel describes sustained residual structure, while the
percussive channel describes transient structure. Temporal differences
expose changes that a static residual spectrum may obscure. The balance
channel is implemented as a difference of log-mel features, not as a
direct division of raw spectra. These channels provide an explicit
inductive bias, while the classifier learns their relationship to the
label. They are complementary descriptions, not statistically
independent measurements of seven distinct artifact mechanisms. HPSS
alone does not guarantee invariance to codecs or uniquely identify
synthesis.

### IV-D Lightweight Classification and Recording-Level Aggregation

A compact CNN processes the seven-channel features of four-second audio
segments. The implemented classifier has four Conv–BatchNorm–ReLU–Pool
blocks, adaptive average pooling, and fully connected classification
layers, totaling 423,553 parameters. A sigmoid produces a segment score
between zero and one. The extractor and classifier together contain
4,027,490 parameters.

The detector specification aggregates segment scores by their median.
The final classifier checkpoint uses a previously selected operating
threshold of 0.225; results at 0.5 are also reported as a secondary
reference. This is a whole-recording classification task; no temporal
localization claim is made. Section V specifies the evaluated model
variants and sampling protocols. Recording-level scores from different
variants are evaluated separately rather than combined into a single
performance estimate.

### IV-E Codec-Aware Training

The third stage exposes the extractor to WAV, MP3, AAC, and Opus
variants. The lossy variants in the reported four-codec comparison use
128 kb/s. Codec augmentation is intended to reduce dependence on
compression signatures that occur in real as well as generated music. It
does not assume that a conventional distribution codec and a synthesis
decoder produce the same residual.

This training-time augmentation should be distinguished from
inference-time transformation or ensemble methods. The model studied
here uses a single residual-extraction path at inference.
Section [VI-F](#S6.SS6 "VI-F Paired Sensitivity to Distribution Codecs ‣ VI Results and Analysis ‣ ArtifactNet: Detecting AI-Generated Music via Forensic Residual Physics")
compares the earlier and codec-aware extractors with a fixed downstream
classifier to examine how distribution encoding affects their scores.

## V Experimental Setup

The experiments address three questions: whether the compact residual
detector separates generated and real recordings on a training-disjoint
common-file test, how its sensitivity varies across generator sources,
and how distribution encoding changes its score. The common-file test is
the primary detection experiment. Historical SONICS and MoM analyses
describe the development path, while a separate four-codec experiment
isolates transformation sensitivity. These populations are not pooled.

### V-A Datasets and Evaluation Tasks

SONICS (Synthetic Or Not—Identifying Counterfeit Songs) is a public
dataset for end-to-end synthetic-song detection that includes real
recordings and synthetic songs from Suno and Udio \[[2](#bib.bib2)\].
MoM (Melody or Machine) combines music from multiple generation systems
with real music and an out-of-distribution evaluation design
\[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. The primary test draws recording-level items from
these and other retained research collections. It contains 534 generated
and 479 real recordings across 18 source labels
(Table [II](#S5.T2 "TABLE II ‣ V-A Datasets and Evaluation Tasks ‣ V Experimental Setup ‣ ArtifactNet: Detecting AI-Generated Music via Forensic Residual Physics")).
Source labels also represented in classifier training remain present, so
this is a training-disjoint recording test, not an
unseen-generator-family test.

The archived SONICS evaluation contains 23,288 prediction records for
ArtifactNet and SpecTTTra, and 23,277 for CLAM. Each contains 10,510
real-labeled records. Reconciliation against the original manifest
resolves 23,288 unique file paths, although the shorter source/track key
has only 18,374 unique values because generated renditions share

The MoM analysis contains 9,596 generated-recording predictions: 4,596
DiffRhythm, 2,000 Udio, 1,500 Yue, and 1,500 Riffusion. The original
path/name warning identified 157 matches to a training/validation
manifest: 140 in its training partition and 17 in validation. The
evaluation runner did not remove them. A broader retrospective search
finds additional candidate exposure matches, so 157 is not an exhaustive
overlap count. These matches identify possible shared items, not proven
byte-identical audio or complete checkpoint lineage. We report this
analysis as descriptive, not held-out or zero-shot. It contains no
matched real partition, so neither an MoM false-positive rate nor an MoM
F1 score is reported.

The primary test was frozen before the present three-model run. Its
starting point was the 1,414 original-view recordings in a pre-existing
test partition; codec-derived views were omitted. For the February
ArtifactUNet stages, the surviving chunks do not retain original file
identities. We therefore reconstructed their source scope from the
archived data-generation code and excluded every corresponding source
family wholesale, rather than relying on uncertain filename matches. For
the April codec-aware fine-tune, both the 4,579-item training partition
and the 507-item model-selection partition retain paths; we removed
every remaining test item whose case-folded filename stem occurred in
either partition. Finally, the classifier manifest’s origin-identity
audit found zero overlap with its 285,310 training entries, 11,441
validation entries, or 100-item mining addendum. These filters leave
1,013 recordings. The frozen manifest, model hashes, per-file audio
hashes, and raw probabilities are retained for audit.

### V-B Training and Inference Configuration

ArtifactNet operates on audio at 44.1 kHz, with a 2,048-point STFT, a
hop of 512 samples, and 128 mel bands in the implemented frontend.
Four-second segments form the classification inputs. The model size
refers only to the extractor and classifier; HPSS and spectral
transforms add computation without learned parameters.

The primary ArtifactNet checkpoint combines the 3.60M-parameter
codec-aware extractor with a 0.42M-parameter seven-channel classifier.
The classifier training manifest contains 285,310 training entries and
11,441 validation entries; these counts include augmented views and are
not recording counts. We disclose the split and source-lineage firewall
without exposing subsequent commercial routing, auxiliary decision
models, customer audio, or proprietary mining logic, none of which is
used in this paper.

ArtifactNet scores seven uniformly spaced four-second excerpts from the
first 120 seconds and takes their median. Its operating threshold,
0.225, was fixed before this test. SpecTTTra uses its released
120-second checkpoint with the released evaluation crop, padding, and
waveform standardization. CLAM uses the common initial 90 seconds,
resampled and padded separately for its MERT and Wav2Vec2 branches; each
branch time-pools each of its 13 hidden states. Thus, all models receive
the same hash-verified files while retaining their released or
model-specific frontends. No model is retrained and no threshold is
fitted on the test. The paired transcoding study separately evaluates
100 real and 100 generated recordings under the controls in
Section [VI-F](#S6.SS6 "VI-F Paired Sensitivity to Distribution Codecs ‣ VI Results and Analysis ‣ ArtifactNet: Detecting AI-Generated Music via Forensic Residual Physics").

### V-C Baselines, Metrics, and Comparison Protocol

The primary comparison uses released CLAM \[[1](#bib.bib1)\] and
SpecTTTra \[[2](#bib.bib2)\] checkpoints without retraining. It corrects
wrapper deviations found in the archived developmental runs: SpecTTTra
now uses its released evaluation crop and normalization, and CLAM’s two
branches use the same initial excerpt. The historical wrapper results
are not used for the primary model ranking.

The released CLAM head introduces another comparison constraint. Its
attention layers receive batch-first feature tensors while retaining
sequence-first attention, coupling predictions across recordings in a
batch. We preserve the released head and weights rather than silently
changing the architecture. The primary comparison evaluates one
recording per head call. A separate sensitivity analysis groups the same
frozen features into batches of 16 under three orders fixed before the
full run (seeds 7, 42, and 20260920); the final shorter batch is
retained. All three conditions are reported without selecting the best
order. These are execution-condition diagnostics, not a correction of
CLAM’s training.

AI is the positive class. We compute F1, recall, false-positive rate,
area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUROC), and
average precision from recording scores, with

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{F1}=\frac{2\mathrm{TP}}{2\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN}},\quad\mathrm{FPR}=\frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TN}}.
``` |  | (3) |

Wilson 95% intervals accompany TPR and FPR, and exact paired tests
compare recording-level correctness. The original developmental
comparisons changed more than one component. Accordingly, we do not
assign a quantitative isolated gain to mask bounds, staged training, or
individual feature channels from those comparisons. The numerical
extractor comparison below addresses codec sensitivity, with its
sampling limitations stated explicitly.

## VI Results and Analysis

### VI-A Primary Training-Disjoint Test

All three systems produce scores for every one of the 1,013 frozen
recordings. At its pre-established threshold of 0.225, ArtifactNet
yields 500 true positives, 34 false negatives, no false positives, and
479 true negatives. Its F1 is 0.9671, and its TPR is 93.63% (Wilson 95%
interval: 91.23–95.41%). The observed FPR is 0% (Wilson 95% interval:
0–0.80%). The secondary threshold of 0.5 reduces recall to 90.26% while
retaining zero observed false positives.

ArtifactNet’s AUROC and average precision are 0.9982 and 0.9988.
SpecTTTra produces 417 true positives, 117 false negatives, 131 false
positives, and 348 true negatives: F1=0.7708, TPR=78.09%, FPR=27.35%,
AUROC=0.8192, and average precision=0.8314. With one recording per head

positives, and 69 true negatives: F1=0.6898, TPR=93.07%, FPR=85.59%,

Exact paired tests on recording-level correctness compare the fixed
operating points. ArtifactNet is uniquely correct on 246 recordings
versus 32 for SpecTTTra among 278 discordant pairs
($`p=4.55\times 10^{-42}`$), and on 447 versus 34 for CLAM batch one
among 481 discordant pairs ($`p=5.54\times 10^{-93}`$). These
comparisons apply to the retained checkpoints and specified frontends.
Their unequal training data and operating-point conventions prevent
attributing the differences to one architectural component.

Errors are source-dependent. Of ArtifactNet’s 34 false negatives, 31
occur in the retained SONICS subset, whose sensitivity is 74.59%; the
remaining three occur in FakeMusicCaps and MoM DiffRhythm. Thus, the
aggregate result does not imply uniform behavior across generator
sources. Zero observed false positives likewise bounds rather than
proves a zero population FPR.

### VI-E From Full Separation to Compact Residual Extraction

The final inference architecture no longer computes the teacher’s source
estimates. It requires a 3.60M-parameter extractor and a 0.42M-parameter
classifier, making residual extraction the primary learned computation.
The frozen primary test and retrospective analyses show discriminative
scores at this parameter budget, within their stated evaluation scopes.
They do not isolate the accuracy difference between the student and a
teacher-based detector trained and evaluated under identical conditions.

The same distinction applies to staged training and the seven-channel
representation. Their roles are explicit in the architecture and
training procedure, but the present evidence does not isolate each
component’s causal contribution under fixed data and repeated training
seeds. The bounded-mask constraint has the mathematical effect described
in Section IV-A; a quantitative claim about its detection benefit
additionally requires a controlled experiment.

### VI-F Paired Sensitivity to Distribution Codecs

The paired study compares the Phase 2 and codec-aware extractors using
the same frozen MoM classifier. Before computing codec outcomes, a fixed
hash ordering selects 100 of the available 173 real recordings and 20
generated recordings from each of five SONICS source strata, excluding
known candidate matches and retaining distinct platform-generation
identifiers. The strata are three Suno versions (chirp-v2-xxl-alpha,
chirp-v3, and chirp-v3.5) and two Udio duration variants (30 and 120
seconds). This is a new availability-limited transcoding study, not a
reconstruction of the earlier unpaired codec experiment or an
independent detection test.

Each source is decoded to mono at 48 kHz. We take the middle six seconds
within its first 120 seconds, attenuating all variants by the same
factor only if the peak exceeds 0.95. From this common float32 WAV, we

decoded through the same path. Mean-centered cross-correlation estimates

resampled to 44.1 kHz for both extractors. Each variant is processed
separately to avoid coupling its log-mel floor to other variants. All
200 planned recordings succeed without replacement. Source and

retained.

### VI-G Model Size and Inference Cost

The implemented extractor and head total 4.03M parameters. The separator
is absent from inference, and no additional learned model is required
for HPSS. This establishes compactness in parameter count, not an
end-to-end speedup. Runtime depends on the spectral frontend, HPSS
implementation, excerpt count, hardware, and batching. We do not report
a new latency or throughput measurement in this study.

## VII Discussion and Limitations

### VII-A Interpretation of the Learned Residuals

ArtifactNet treats residual extraction as part of the detection problem.
The source-separation teacher supplies an initial representation, while
classifier-guided refinement and codec augmentation adapt that
representation to the task. This is the central distinction from
retaining a fixed separator or directly classifying musical content
without a residual intermediate.

The residual remains learned and may contain musical structure,
separation errors, recording effects, and synthesis-related components.
A useful discriminator does not establish which physical mechanism
causes its decisions. Accordingly, the phrase “forensic residual
physics” describes the signal-processing motivation for the
representation. The present results do not identify a pure RVQ error or
establish that all future generators will share a detectable residual.
Mechanistic attribution would also require controls for decoding,
clipping, and resampling, which can introduce spectral structure
independently of synthesis.

### VII-B Remaining Failure Cases and Generalization Limits

Conventional compression changes detector behavior, and the tested
codec-aware extractor trades fewer real false positives for reduced
generated-music sensitivity under some encodings. The Opus failures are
a concrete counterexample to uniform codec robustness. Aggressive
remastering, resampling, new generator families, and mixed human–AI
recordings require additional evaluation. The whole-recording
formulation also cannot determine which portion of a track was generated
or how much human authorship remains.

The primary test improves on the historical evaluations by tying every
score to a frozen recording list, an audio hash, and the retained
checkpoint lineage. Its strongest conservative step—excluding whole
February source families—is necessary because the old chunks do not
retain original file identities. It cannot rule out an undocumented
source or an unrecognized derivative relationship. Moreover, several
source families also occur in classifier training, although recording
identities are separated. The result therefore measures held-out
recordings within a related multi-source domain, not generalization to
wholly unseen generators.

Controlled component ablations, repeated training seeds, mixed human–AI
recordings, and a future collection acquired after model freeze are
still needed. Training-pipeline reproducibility is also limited: private
audio and commercial training infrastructure are not supplied with this
manuscript. The method-level description and retained evaluation
evidence permit score auditing, but do not establish independently
repeatable end-to-end retraining.

## VIII Conclusion

We presented ArtifactNet, a compact forensic detector developed from
source-separation residuals. A bounded-mask ArtifactUNet replaces the
full separator at inference, HPSS and temporal features form a
seven-channel representation, and a lightweight CNN performs detection.
Staged distillation and classifier-guided refinement connect the
separation-derived starting point to the discriminative task.

On the frozen 1,013-recording test, the 4.03M-parameter system obtains
F1=0.9671, AUROC=0.9982, and no observed false positives at its
pre-established operating threshold. The result is recording-disjoint
from the reconstructed training lineage, but it is not an
unseen-generator-family evaluation. Released-baseline comparisons favor
ArtifactNet at the specified operating points, while the CLAM diagnostic
shows that implementation semantics can materially change apparent
performance. In the paired transcoding study, the codec-aware extractor
reduces real-music score variation and false positives but loses
sensitivity to some generated audio, most notably Opus-encoded excerpts.
These findings support the compact residual-based design and its
measured tradeoffs, rather than universal codec robustness. Independent
future-collection evaluation and controlled component comparisons remain
necessary to establish broader generalization.
````
