# Ruby-OPD: Enhancing Pronunciation Controllability and Accuracy for Flow-Matching Text-to-Speech via On-Policy Distillation

Rui    Xiaolong    Zhenpeng

###### Abstract

Achieving both high pronunciation accuracy and fine-grained
controllability remains a central challenge in Japanese text-to-speech
(TTS), as Japanese is an unsegmented language mixing logographic Kanji
and phonographic Kana with extensive polyphony. Traditional cascaded
systems convert text into phoneme sequences via a front-end, ensuring
pronunciation control but discarding lexical semantics and prosodic cues
in surface orthography. Conversely, recent zero-shot TTS models directly
consume raw orthographic text to improve naturalness, yet suffer from
polyphone errors and lack explicit pronunciation control. To bridge this
gap, we propose a two-stage framework for flow-matching TTS. First, we
investigate Ruby text representations with orthography perturbation to
jointly preserve surface forms and target pronunciations for reliable
control. Second, we introduce On-Policy Distillation (OPD) on
large-scale text-only corpora to enhance pronunciation accuracy on raw
orthographic text without paired speech. On the Joyo-Kanji-Yomi
benchmark, our model achieves 97.13% Accuracy, 2.02% Target PER, 0.70%
Sentence PER, and 4.21% Sentence CER under raw text while retaining
strong controllability.

###### keywords

text-to-speech, on-policy distillation, pronunciation accuracy,
controllability, Japanese

## 1 Introduction

Traditional neural text-to-speech (TTS) systems \[1, 2, 3\] follow a
cascaded pipeline comprising a text front-end, an acoustic model, and a
vocoder, where the front-end extracts phonemes and prosodic features
from raw text. Recently, scaling model capacity and data has driven the
rise of zero-shot TTS across both autoregressive (AR) \[4, 5, 6, 7, 8\]
and non-autoregressive (NAR) \[9, 10, 11, 12\] paradigms. These models
bypass complex front-ends and directly consume raw orthographic text,
leveraging surface lexical semantics to synthesize natural speech.

In Japanese TTS, however, directly consuming orthographic text poses
severe challenges for pronunciation accuracy and controllability. As an
unsegmented language, Japanese combines logograms (Kanji) and phonograms
(Kana). While Kana maps almost deterministically to pronunciation, Kanji
pronunciations vary drastically with morphology and context. For
instance,

秋(autumn) takes the native pronunciation (Kun’yomi) aki standalone, the
Sino-Japanese pronunciation (On’yomi) shū in compounds such as

晩秋(late autumn), and the metaphorical pronunciation (Gikun) toki (

時, moment) in classical expressions such as

此れ誠に危急存亡の秋なり(this is truly a critical moment of survival).

Early Japanese TTS relied on morphological analyzers with
dictionaries \[13, 14, 15\] and pretrained language models for polyphone
disambiguation and accent prediction \[16, 17, 18, 19\]. Although
phonemic inputs offer direct pronunciation control, stripping away Kanji
orthography discards semantic and prosodic cues, limiting
naturalness \[20\]. Conversely, recent end-to-end Japanese TTS models
train directly on orthographic text. Irodori-TTS \[21\] models
continuous acoustic latents \[22\] via conditional flow matching with a
Diffusion Transformer (DiT) \[23\], while Sarashina2.2-TTS \[24\] uses
an AR architecture and mitigates Kanji mispronunciations via local Kana
substitution and offline speech synthesis over polyphones. Yet, this
still sacrifices orthographic semantics and incurs costly off-policy
synthesis.

In this paper, we propose a two-stage framework to enhance pronunciation
controllability and accuracy in flow-matching Japanese TTS. First, we
introduce Ruby text representations coupled with orthography
perturbation to jointly preserve logographic surface forms and target
pronunciations for precise pronunciation control. Second, we propose
cross-condition On-Policy Distillation (OPD) on large-scale text-only
corpora to improve raw-text pronunciation accuracy without paired
speech. Experiments on the Joyo-Kanji-Yomi benchmark show that our
method substantially improves both pronunciation controllability and
accuracy, achieving 97.13% Accuracy, 2.02% Target PER, 0.70% Sentence
PER, and 4.21% Sentence CER.

## 2 Method

### 2.1 Ruby text representation

Ruby is widely used in Japanese books, webpages, and subtitles to
annotate the pronunciations of proper nouns, polyphones, rare words, or
stylistically specialized terms. Visually, Ruby attaches small Kana
characters above or beside target Kanji, simultaneously conveying both
orthographic surface form and phonemic pronunciation within a single
visual unit.

When linearizing two-dimensional Ruby typography into a sequential text
input for a TTS text encoder, we explore two complementary
representations:

- •
  Implicit Ruby: Target pronunciations are appended directly after
  annotated Kanji words using parentheses. This aligns naturally with
  human users’ everyday writing and interactive editing habits when
  manually annotating occasional rare words or proper nouns on the fly;
- •
  Explicit Ruby: One limitation of implicit Ruby is the lack of explicit
  left boundaries for the target span, which can sometimes result in
  ambiguous annotations in unsegmented text. Explicit Ruby, however,
  employs explicit boundary markers for both the target span and its
  pronunciation, making it well suited for unambiguous and precise
  pronunciation annotation.

An example is illustrated in Table 1. The compound word

超電磁砲is canonically pronounced as

ちょうでんじほう(chōdenjihō). However, in the title

とある科学の超電磁砲(A Certain Scientific Railgun), it takes the
stylized pronunciation

レールガン(rērugan). Under implicit Ruby, because this non-standard
pronunciation has no morphological correspondence to individual Kanji
characters, the left boundary of the annotated span becomes ambiguous,
where it could be interpreted as annotating either

電磁砲or the entire

超電磁砲. In contrast, explicit Ruby eliminates such scope ambiguity by
introducing explicit boundary markers.

### 2.2 Training data construction

Constructing paired Ruby training data for supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
solely via Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) tools is prone to polyphone
disambiguation errors that may mismatch the actual pronunciations
uttered in the paired speech. To construct precisely aligned Ruby
training data, we employ a cross-modal verification approach that
leverages information from both text and audio modalities. Specifically,
we first use a phoneme recognition model to transcribe the input speech
into a pure Kana sequence, capturing the actual pronunciation of the
utterance. Then, for the corresponding orthographic text, we use
UniDic \[14\], a widely used morphological dictionary, to construct a
word lattice encompassing all valid morphological segmentations and
candidate pronunciations. Finally, we apply dynamic programming to
retrieve the alignment path through the lattice that exactly matches the
recognized Kana transcription, precisely pinpointing the character span
and true contextual pronunciation of every Kanji word unit in the
utterance.

In practical applications, users exhibit diverse annotation habits
across different granularities, ranging from short single words to
longer multi-word phrases, and may feed either completely unannotated
orthographic text or partially annotated Ruby text where only selected
words are annotated. To simulate these scenarios, rather than
enumerating fixed full-annotation paths after the alignment step, we
store the aligned Ruby units and adopt a dynamic multi-granularity
sampling strategy during training. First, at the utterance level, we
sample from a preset probability distribution to determine whether an
utterance is formatted as unannotated, partially annotated, or fully
annotated. Specifically, under the partially annotated mode, each Ruby
unit is converted into Ruby format with a certain probability and
otherwise reverts to raw orthographic text. Furthermore, adjacent Ruby
units that are simultaneously selected for annotation are merged with a
certain probability into a single contiguous Ruby unit. These data
augmentation strategies enable the model to seamlessly handle
unannotated raw text, sparse local annotations, and annotations of
varying granularities.

### 2.3 Orthography perturbation

In our preliminary experiments, we found that directly training the
model with Ruby text failed to make it properly interpret the Ruby
format or follow the pronunciation annotations at inference time.
Instead, the model often read aloud both the surface form and the
annotated pronunciation within Ruby units. We conjecture that because
the base model was pretrained on raw orthographic text, it can already
infer canonical pronunciations for most Kanji solely from their surface
glyphs, leaving little incentive to attend to the pronunciation cues
provided in the Ruby annotations.

To address this issue, we keep the target pronunciation annotation
intact while stochastically perturbing the surface form of each Ruby
unit during training via three complementary operations:

- •
  Kanji masking: replaces partial or full Kanji spans in the surface
  form with special mask tokens. Partial masking encourages the model to
  align unmasked characters with the target pronunciation to infer span
  boundaries in implicit Ruby, whereas full masking forces the model to
  rely on the pronunciation annotation when surface glyph cues are
  absent;
- •
  Random substitution: replaces the surface form in explicit Ruby with
  random characters or unrelated words of arbitrary length, breaking
  character-to-mora correspondence and compelling the model to follow
  the annotated pronunciation regardless of surface glyphs;
- •
  Semantic substitution: replaces the surface form in explicit Ruby with
  semantically similar Kanji words that have distinctly different
  canonical pronunciations, retrieved via text embeddings over UniDic.
  This design specifically simulates the aforementioned Gikun
  phenomenon, where the surface form is semantically aligned with both
  the annotated pronunciation and surrounding context yet phonemically
  unrelated to its canonical pronunciation. For example, the Kana word

  ワールド(world), pronounced as wārudo, may be assigned a semantically
  similar yet orthographically and phonemically distinct surface form

  世界, canonically pronounced as sekai. This strategy ensures that the
  model strictly adheres to the annotated pronunciation while leveraging
  surface orthography for semantic and prosodic cues.

### 2.4 On-policy distillation

After SFT with Ruby data, the model achieves high pronunciation accuracy
when provided with full Ruby annotations. However, as noted earlier,
real-world inputs are often unannotated orthographic text or only
partially annotated, leaving unannotated spans vulnerable to polyphone
disambiguation errors. Attaching an external G2P module at inference
time or synthesizing paired text-speech data over polyphones in an
off-policy manner \[24\] are viable workarounds, but they introduce
extra pipeline modules or costly offline synthesis steps. Instead, we
propose an On-Policy Distillation (OPD) approach for flow-matching TTS
that relies solely on paired
$`(\text{orthographic text},\text{Ruby text})`$ data without requiring
parallel audio, improving pronunciation accuracy when Ruby annotations
are absent.

In conditional flow matching \[25\], a neural network
$`v_{\theta}(x_{t},t,c)`$ learns a time-dependent velocity field that
transports standard Gaussian noise $`x_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)`$ at
$`t=1`$ to clean acoustic latents $`x_{0}`$ at $`t=0`$ conditioned on
text $`c`$. With paired speech $`x_{0}`$, noisy states are constructed
via linear interpolation $`x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}`$, and the model is
trained by minimizing:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,x_{0},x_{1}}\Bigl[\bigl\lVert v_{\theta}(x_{t},t,c)-(x_{1}-x_{0})\bigr\rVert_{2}^{2}\Bigr].
```

In our OPD framework, the SFT model serves as a frozen teacher
$`v_{\phi}`$ conditioned on full Ruby text $`c_{\text{ruby}}`$, while
the student $`v_{\theta}`$, initialized from $`v_{\phi}`$, is
conditioned on unannotated orthographic text $`c_{\text{raw}}`$ (other
conditioning is omitted for brevity). Because text-only samples lack
ground-truth speech latents $`x_{0}`$, we generate the intermediate
state $`x_{t}`$ online via student rollout \[26, 27\]. Starting from
pure Gaussian noise $`x_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)`$ with target sequence
length predicted by the teacher’s duration predictor, we integrate the
student’s ordinary differential equation (ODE) trajectory up to a
randomly sampled timestep $`t`$ without gradients to obtain the
on-policy state $`x_{t}`$. At $`(x_{t},t)`$, the student is trained to
match the teacher’s velocity field:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{OPD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,x_{t}}\Bigl[\bigl\lVert v_{\theta}(x_{t},t,c_{\text{raw}})-\text{sg}\bigl[v_{\phi}(x_{t},t,c_{\text{ruby}})\bigr]\bigr\rVert_{2}^{2}\Bigr],
```

where $`\text{sg}[\cdot]`$ denotes the stop-gradient operator, alongside
a Huber loss aligning the student’s duration predictor with the
teacher’s. Since the rollout starts from pure noise, $`x_{t}`$ contains
zero phonemic leakage from ground-truth audio, forcing the student to
disambiguate polyphones from context to match the teacher’s trajectory.
During training, we also interleave the paired speech data used in the
SFT stage to anchor acoustic naturalness and retain Ruby
controllability.

## 3 Experimental setup

### 3.1 Data

For the SFT stage, we use the Japanese subset of the open-source
multilingual speech dataset Emilia \[28\]. To ensure strict consistency
between pronunciation annotations and actual speech content, we
transcribe all utterances using kana-whisper¹¹ 1
[https://huggingface.co/sbintuitions/kana-whisper](https://huggingface.co/sbintuitions/kana-whisper)
and filter for utterances whose Kana transcriptions exactly match the
word lattice constructed via UniDic \[14\]. Combined with duration
filtering ($`0.5\sim 30\text{ s}`$), we obtain approximately
$`774\text{ hours}`$ of high-quality aligned data.

For subsequent text-only OPD, we follow \[19\] by using entries from
UniDic \[14\] as seed words and leveraging Gemini 3.1 Pro to synthesize
text-pronunciation pairs. For each monophonic and polyphonic entry, we
synthesize 5 and 10 contextual sentences, respectively. Specifically,
rather than asking the LLM to directly output Ruby annotations, we
prompt it to generate sentence-level Kana pronunciation sequences and
apply the same lattice alignment method used for Emilia. This yields
approximately $`680\text{k}`$ monophonic sentences and $`100\text{k}`$
polyphonic sentences.

### 3.2 Implementation details

We build upon the open-source Japanese flow-matching zero-shot TTS model
Irodori-TTS-v4.1-Small \[21\]²² 2
[https://huggingface.co/Aratako/Irodori-TTS-v4.1-Small](https://huggingface.co/Aratako/Irodori-TTS-v4.1-Small)
as our base model. It initializes its text encoder with a pretrained
bidirectional masked language model \[29\] and predicts the conditional
velocity field of $`25\text{ Hz}`$, $`32`$-dimensional continuous DACVAE
latents \[22\] via a DiT backbone.

In the SFT stage, utterance-level annotation modes are sampled with
probabilities of $`50\%`$ full Ruby, $`30\%`$ partial Ruby, in which
each Ruby unit is annotated with a probability of $`0.5`$, and $`20\%`$
unannotated raw text. Each annotated Ruby unit is formatted as implicit
or explicit Ruby with an equal probability of $`0.5`$, and adjacent
annotated Ruby units are merged with a probability of $`0.5`$, up to
$`3`$ merges and $`8`$ characters. For orthography perturbation, we use
Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B³³ 3
[https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B)
to retrieve semantic substitution candidates over UniDic \[14\], and
each annotated surface span undergoes full-span Kanji masking with a
probability of $`0.2`$, partial character-level masking with a
probability of $`0.25`$, semantic substitution with a probability of
$`0.2`$, and random substitution with a probability of $`0.15`$. The
model is trained for $`50\text{k}`$ steps with a global batch size of
$`256`$ using AdamW, with a learning rate of $`1\times 10^{-4}`$ for the
main model and $`1\times 10^{-5}`$ for the pretrained text encoder.

In the OPD stage, we freeze the SFT model as the teacher, which receives
$`100\%`$ full Ruby text, and train the student for $`50\text{k}`$ steps
with a global batch size of $`256`$, a learning rate of
$`5\times 10^{-5}`$, a text encoder learning rate of
$`2.5\times 10^{-5}`$, and $`8`$ rollout steps. Each training step
samples text-only data with an $`80\%`$ probability, equally weighted
between monophonic and polyphonic subsets with $`100\%`$ raw text input
for the student, and paired speech data with a $`20\%`$ probability,
where student inputs are sampled using $`80\%`$ raw text, $`10\%`$
partial Ruby, and $`10\%`$ full Ruby with the same orthography
perturbation settings as in the SFT stage.

### 3.3 Evaluation metrics

To evaluate pronunciation accuracy and controllability, we conduct
evaluations on a revised version of the Joyo-Kanji-Yomi benchmark
(JKYB) \[24\]⁴⁴ 4
[https://github.com/Parakeet-Inc/Joyo-Kanji-Yomi-Benchmark-Parakeet-Edition](https://github.com/Parakeet-Inc/Joyo-Kanji-Yomi-Benchmark-Parakeet-Edition).
It comprises $`13,536`$ sentences covering 2,136 Joyo Kanji (regular-use
Kanji) and their 4,512 official pronunciations specified by the Agency
for Cultural Affairs of Japan. JKYB provides the following metrics:

- •
  Accuracy: Percentage of evaluated Kanji pronunciations that exactly
  match the ground truth;
- •
  Target PER: Kana character error rate (Kana-CER) against the ground
  truth of the evaluated Kanji. Values are capped to 100%;
- •
  Sentence PER: Kana-CER for the full-sentence pronunciation. Values are
  capped to 100%;
- •
  Sentence CER: Standard CER between the ASR transcription of the
  utterance and the source text. Values are capped to 100%.

We also evaluate speech quality using UTMOSv2 \[30\] and DNSMOS \[31\],
and voice cloning speaker similarity using CAM++ \[32\] and
ERes2Net \[33\]. During inference, reference audio prompts are randomly
sampled from the JVS corpus \[34\], which consists of 100 speakers with
100 normal-style utterances per speaker.

## 4 Experimental results

### 4.1 Pronunciation controllability

We first investigate the effectiveness of Ruby representations for
pronunciation control. Using the reference text and ground-truth
pronunciations from JKYB, we construct oracle Ruby inputs at both the
target-word and full-sentence levels.

As shown in Table 3, for Irodori-Base, directly replacing the target
Kanji word with Kana (Target Kana) effectively improves target
pronunciation accuracy, whereas replacing the entire sentence with Kana
(Full Kana) causes a notable performance drop at both the target-word
and sentence levels, yielding an even higher Sentence PER than
unannotated raw text. This confirms that stripping away Kanji
orthography across an unsegmented sentence eliminates crucial lexical
boundary and semantic cues. In contrast, by jointly preserving surface
Kanji and phonemic annotations, both Irodori-Ruby-SFT and
Irodori-Ruby-OPD avoid this degradation: expanding annotations from the
target word (Target) to the full sentence (Full) maintains high target
accuracy while further reducing Sentence PER.

Moreover, explicit Ruby (Expl.) consistently outperforms implicit Ruby
(Impl.) across both SFT and OPD models under both target-word and
full-sentence settings. While implicit Ruby already provides strong
controllability for convenient user interaction, the explicit boundary
markers in explicit Ruby effectively eliminate left-boundary scope
ambiguity, particularly for multi-character words with non-compositional
pronunciations.

### 4.2 Main results

Table 2 compares our models (Irodori-Ruby-SFT and Irodori-Ruby-OPD)
against the base model (Irodori-Base) and public state-of-the-art (SOTA)
baselines across pronunciation accuracy, speech quality, and voice
cloning. At inference time, all models directly consume raw orthographic
text without any Ruby annotations. First, while general multilingual TTS
models, such as Qwen3-TTS, FireRedTTS3, OmniVoice, and FishAudio S2 Pro,
achieve leading performance in speech quality and voice cloning, they
still struggle with Japanese polyphonic Kanji pronunciation, exhibiting
relatively low Accuracy ($`72.10\%\text{--}89.90\%`$) and high Target
PER ($`8.27\%\text{--}23.68\%`$). In contrast, Japanese-specialized
models such as Sarashina2.2-TTS and Irodori-Base benefit from
large-scale Japanese pretraining and demonstrate a clear advantage in
pronunciation accuracy, with Irodori-Base achieving $`94.11\%`$ Accuracy
and the lowest Sentence CER ($`4.04\%`$).

Compared with Irodori-Base, Irodori-Ruby-SFT, fine-tuned solely on
Emilia Ruby data, exhibits a slight drop on raw text ($`92.32\%`$
Accuracy and $`6.05\%`$ Target PER) and minor shifts in acoustic and
speaker metrics, reflecting the limited scale and modest recording
quality of the SFT corpus as well as capacity allocated to learning Ruby
conditioning. After large-scale text-only OPD, however, Irodori-Ruby-OPD
achieves substantial gains on raw text without additional paired speech,
boosting Accuracy to $`97.13\%`$ ($`+4.81\%`$ over its teacher and
$`+3.02\%`$ over Irodori-Base), cutting Target PER by more than half vs.
Irodori-Base ($`4.68\%\rightarrow 2.02\%`$), and achieving the lowest
Sentence PER ($`0.70\%`$). Furthermore, Irodori-Ruby-OPD consistently
surpasses Irodori-Ruby-SFT across all four Ruby control modes (Table 3)
while maintaining speech quality and voice cloning on par with its
teacher. In particular, under target-only annotation (Target Impl. and
Target Expl.), Irodori-Ruby-OPD notably reduces Sentence PER compared
with Irodori-Ruby-SFT ($`0.56\%\text{--}0.57\%`$ vs.
$`0.72\%\text{--}0.75\%`$), as the remaining unannotated Kanji words in
the utterance simultaneously benefit from the enhanced raw-text
pronunciation capability distilled during OPD.

## 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a two-stage framework for flow-matching
Japanese TTS that first introduces implicit and explicit Ruby
representations coupled with orthography perturbation during SFT to
enable fine-grained pronunciation control while preserving surface Kanji
semantics. Building upon the Ruby-capable SFT teacher, we further
introduced cross-condition On-Policy Distillation (OPD) on large-scale
LLM-synthesized text-pronunciation pairs, transferring context-dependent
Kanji pronunciation knowledge into raw-text inference without requiring
paired speech. Experimental results on the Joyo-Kanji-Yomi benchmark
demonstrate that Irodori-Ruby-OPD achieves state-of-the-art
pronunciation accuracy on unannotated orthographic text while providing
flexible and reliable Ruby-based pronunciation control.
