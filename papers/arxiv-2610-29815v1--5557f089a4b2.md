###### Abstract

Driven by recent advancements in hearing assistive devices, target
speaker extraction (TSE) in multi-talker environments has drawn
significant attention. To address this, hearing technologies identify
attention using neural signals recorded via electroencephalography (EEG)
to guide TSE, enabling neuro-steered auditory attention decoding.
However, most existing techniques rely on time-frequency masking, which
suffers from low fidelity in spectro-temporally overlapping regions and
is often restricted to narrowband audio. In contrast, we introduce
NeuroCodec, which, to the best of our knowledge, is the first generative
EEG-guided TSE framework operating in the latent space of a neural audio
codec. Specifically, NeuroCodec models spatial EEG dependencies via
graph convolutions and fuses cross-modal EEG and speech representations
through attention mechanisms and Mamba-based temporal modeling. We
further introduce a six-phase training curriculum that progressively
incorporates latent reconstruction, multi-scale spectral, and
adversarial objectives to ensure stable, high-fidelity speech
generation. Experiments on the public Cocktail Party and KUL datasets
show NeuroCodec outperforms baselines, across several perceptual
metrics. A listening experiment with 20 participants validated that
NeuroCodec offers greater perceptual accuracy, achieving a mean score of
82.24 compared with 49.63 for M3ANet. Additionally, the proposed method
is seen to enable 44.1 kHz reconstruction with spectral content
extending up to 20 kHz, further supporting the perceptual results.
Finally, we extend NeuroCodec with a causal streaming formulation suited
for real-time processing, providing a basis for hearing assistive
devices.

## I Introduction

In complex, multi-talker acoustic environments, the human auditory
system exhibits a remarkable capacity to isolate a target speech stream
while suppressing interfering voices and background noise, an ability
commonly referred to as selective auditory attention in a “cocktail
party” scenario \[1, 2\]. However, this selective auditory attention is
severely compromised in listeners with hearing impairments \[3\],
leading to degraded speech intelligibility and elevated cognitive
load \[4, 5\]. While advanced hearing aid technologies and digital
acoustic prostheses have successfully restored broad auditory
accessibility for millions of individuals, enabling these devices to
operate effectively in dynamic, multi-speaker scenes remains a critical
hurdle. For modern speech enhancement systems, one fundamental challenge
lies not merely in isolating overlapping speech audio, but in decoding
the listener’s attention to determine which speaker to extract from the
acoustic mixture.

Target Speaker Extraction (TSE) aims to address this problem by
isolating a desired speaker from a speech mixture using auxiliary
information about the target speaker \[6\]. Recent approaches exploit a
variety of cues, including acoustic embeddings, directional or binaural
spatial cues \[7, 8\], and visual signals \[9, 10\], to guide the
extraction process and improve robustness in challenging acoustic
environments. Although these approaches have achieved strong extraction
performance, they rely on external reference information that may be
unavailable or unreliable in dynamic real-world environments.

An alternative approach is to decode the attentional target from the
listener’s brain activity, which has been shown to be strongly
correlated with the attended speech signal. Several studies in the field
of auditory neuroscience have demonstrated that the
electroencephalography (EEG) signal tracks the attended speech, which is
also called neural speech tracking \[11, 12\]. Consequently, EEG serves
as a direct, non-invasive, and robust indicator of attention, enabling
TSE without relying on restrictive external cues. While early
computational approaches leveraged this neural-acoustic correlation
solely to detect the attended speaker \[13\] without speech extraction,
a task known as Auditory Attention Decoding (AAD), recent advances have
established integrated neuro-steered TSE frameworks \[14, 15, 16, 17\].
By utilizing the continuous EEG signal as the reference cue, these
systems directly isolate and reconstruct the attended speech waveform
from the multi-talker acoustic mixture \[18\].

Neuro-steered TSE can be categorized into two main processing paradigms:
offline and online extraction. Offline neuro-steered TSE processes
complete audio-EEG sequence before producing the target speech, allowing
the model to exploit full temporal context during extraction. Early
work, such as brain-informed speech separation (BISS) \[18\], used an
EEG-decoded speech envelope to guide TSE. The brain-assisted speech
enhancement network (BASEN) \[19\] later introduced convolutional
cross-attention for direct EEG-guided speech enhancement, while
NeuroHeed \[15\] employed self-attention-based EEG encoding and an
end-to-end extraction framework. NeuroHeed+ \[17\] extended this
approach through joint auditory attention detection and extraction.
NeuroSpex+ \[16\] further improves extraction through dual-task training
on speech-envelope and waveform objectives and is evaluated under a
subject-independent (SI) setting, where test subjects are unseen during
training. Although SI evaluation is more challenging than
subject-dependent (SD) testing, it provides a rigorous measure of
cross-subject generalization for practical deployment. More recently,
M3ANet \[14\] incorporated graph-based EEG modeling and GroupMamba
blocks.

Despite these architectural advances, the inherent reliance on offline
processing renders these models unsuitable for real-time deployment in
hearing assistive devices that require continuous, low-latency
operation. To bridge this gap, online processing paradigms handle the
input audio stream using short, overlapping causal windows.
NeuroHeed \[15\] introduced an online formulation for causal
neuro-steered TSE, while CA-NeuroSpex \[20\] more recently explored a
related causal setting. However, causal neuro-steered extraction remains
highly challenging, with existing online systems generally showing lower
performance than their offline counterparts \[15\].

Taken together, prior work leaves two main gaps. First, existing
EEG-guided TSE systems still rely on discriminative mixture-domain mask
prediction \[14, 15\]. When target and interfering speech overlap, an
inaccurate mask may retain components of the interfering speaker,
resulting in speaker leakage. Second, because prior systems have been
evaluated at limited audio sampling rates, at a maximum of 8 kHz or
14.7 kHz \[14, 15\], their ability to reconstruct wider-band attended
speech has not been established. This narrowband constraint yields an
output with a muffled, robotic acoustic quality that fails to meet the
perceptual naturalness required for real-world applications.

Generative approaches have recently shown promising performance in
speech enhancement and for TSE conditioned on acoustic or visual cues
\[21, 22, 23\]. However, these models are computationally intensive,
rely on slow iterative generation, and typically benefit from
large-scale training data. These requirements are particularly
challenging for EEG-guided TSE, where paired EEG-speech datasets are
limited and low-latency processing is desirable. To address these
constraints, neural audio codecs such as SoundStream \[24\], EnCodec
\[25\], and the Descript Audio Codec (DAC) \[26\], offer an alternative
by using Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) to compress high-rate audio
into compact, low-frame-rate representations that can be reconstructed
by a learned decoder. Predicting these target speech latents
fundamentally transforms the extraction task from discriminative
mixture-domain mask estimation to direct, generative latent synthesis.

In this paper, we propose NeuroCodec, a generative neuro-steered TSE
framework that addresses the limitations of time-frequency mask
estimation using a generative neural audio codec. Specifically, the
framework predicts target speech representations within the latent space
of a frozen, pretrained DAC \[26\]. This generative formulation aims to
reduce masking-related artifacts such as spectral holes and residual
speaker leakage, while enabling substantially wider-band reconstruction.
Unlike prior neuro-steered systems limited to an output bandwidth of
approximately $`4`$-$`7`$ kHz, NeuroCodec reconstructs spectral content
up to $`20`$ kHz. Cross-modal attention and Mamba-based temporal
modeling fuse EEG and mixture audio representations at the compact codec
frame rate. To the best of our knowledge, there has not been prior work
on generative EEG-guided TSE frameworks. Moreover, we present
OnlineNeuroCodec, a causal streaming extension tailored for real-time
hearing assistive devices.

Our main contributions are as follows:

- •
  A generative EEG-guided target speech extraction framework,
  NeuroCodec, that predicts target speech in the latent space of a
  neural audio codec, featuring a six-phase training curriculum to
  stabilize latent, spectral, and adversarial objectives while
  mitigating traditional time-frequency masking artifacts.
- •
  A causal streaming extension, OnlineNeuroCodec, incorporating previous
  target-latent estimates through an auditory attractor to enable
  real-time, neuro-steered extraction.
- •
  A comprehensive experimental evaluation under the SI setting on the
  Cocktail Party \[27\] and KUL \[13\] datasets, featuring objective
  evaluations, ablation studies, streaming benchmarks, spectrogram
  analysis, and a perceptual listening study demonstrating
  reconstruction of spectral content up to approximately 20 kHz.

## II NeuroCodec Framework

### II-A Model Overview

We formulate the TSE problem as a generative mapping from a
multi-speaker mixture and multi-channel EEG signals to the target
speaker’s latent representations, which are subsequently decoded into a
high-fidelity speech waveform. By predicting these latents within the
structured space of a frozen pretrained DAC \[26\], the framework shifts
from mixture-domain interference suppression to cross-modal latent
alignment. This enables the model to leverage pretrained perceptual
priors for speech extraction while focusing its learning capacity on
EEG-guided attention detection. The codec’s decoder is utilized for
final waveform synthesis. As shown in Fig. 1 the architecture consists
of four stages: (i) audio encoder, (ii) EEG encoder, (iii) cross-modal
selective state space block (CM-S3), and (iv) codec latent generator and
decoder. The complete offline training algorithm is provided in the
supplementary materials.

### II-B Audio Encoder

A pretrained and frozen DAC \[26\] encoder $`\mathcal{E}_{c}`$ maps the
mixture waveform $`\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times 1\times T}`$ to a
compact latent representation
$`\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{mix}}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times D\times T_{c}}`$,
where $`B`$ is the batch size, $`T`$ is the number of waveform samples,
$`T_{c}`$ is the number of latent samples, and $`D=1024`$ is the codec
latent dimension size. The compact codec representation substantially
reduces the temporal resolution relative to the raw audio, enabling
efficient subsequent EEG-audio fusion. The DAC encoder remains frozen
throughout training to preserve its pretrained acoustic representation.

### II-C EEG Encoder

Adapted from M3ANet \[14\], the EEG encoder applies third order
Chebyshev graph convolutions \[28\] to model spatial dependencies across
electrodes. Unlike prior approaches that upsample EEG toward the audio
rate \[15, 14, 16\], NeuroCodec operates at the compact codec latent
rate (50 or 86 Hz), close to the native 128 Hz EEG resolution, thereby
avoiding the initial upsampling and substantially reducing interpolation
and computational overhead. The graph features are then processed by
parallel 1-D convolutions with kernel sizes 3, 7, and 11, followed by a
temporal encoder stack of 1-D convolutions to capture deeper sequential
dependencies, that yields a latent output
$`\bar{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times H\times T^{\prime}_{e}}`$,
where $`H`$ is the EEG embedding dimension and $`T^{\prime}_{e}`$ is the
number of latent samples.

### II-D Cross Modal Selective State Space Block

Positional encodings are then injected into both modalities and the
temporal sequences are aligned via multi-head cross-attention \[29\],
defined as

```math
\mathbf{Z}_{1}=\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{mix}}+\mathrm{Attn}(\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{mix}}),\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{\bar{E}}),\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{\bar{E}}))\vskip-4.26773pt \tag{1}
```

where $`\text{LN}(\cdot)`$ denotes layer normalization and
$`\text{Attn}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)`$ denotes cross-modal attention with
inputs of queries, keys and values. The acoustic tokens act as queries,
while the normalized EEG features serve as keys and values to steer the
extraction. Long-range temporal dependencies are subsequently modeled
using a Mamba block \[30\], defined as

```math
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{tgt}}=\mathbf{Z}_{1}+\mathrm{Mamba}(\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{Z}_{1}))\vskip-4.26773pt \tag{2}
```

By replacing self-attention with selective state-space modeling, Mamba
provides efficient linear-time temporal modeling of codec-latent
sequences.

### II-E Codec Latent Generator and Decoder

The output of the four stacked CM-S3 blocks is projected back to the
codec latent dimension to yield the continuous target latents
$`\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\mathrm{tgt}}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times D\times T_{c}}`$.
These predicted latents are then quantized using the residual vector
quantization (RVQ) \[26\] codebooks ($`Q_{1}`$-$`Q_{9}`$) of the frozen
DAC, yielding the discrete latent representation defined as
$`\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{\mathrm{tgt}}=\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\mathrm{tgt}})`$,
where $`\mathcal{Q}`$ denotes the quantization operation. Finally, a
pretrained and frozen DAC decoder $`\mathcal{D}_{c}`$ generates the
target speech waveform from these quantized latents, providing the
target reconstruction defined as
$`\hat{\mathbf{s}}=\mathcal{D}_{c}(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{\mathrm{tgt}})`$.

### II-F Loss Functions

Unlike conventional models that optimize scale-invariant
signal-to-distortion ratio (SI-SDR) loss directly on the decoded audio
\[18, 15, 14, 16\], Neurocodec is trained using a composite objective
function for accurate latent alignment and high-fidelity acoustic
reconstruction.

Reconstruction loss, $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}`$, is computed as
the mean squared error between the predicted and ground-truth target
continuous latents and is used to guide the model to reconstruct the
target speaker representation in the codec latent space.

However, reconstruction loss alone can yield perceptual artifacts.
Spectral loss, $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}`$ \[26, 31\], improves
spectral fidelity by minimizing the $`L_{1}`$ distance between log-Mel
spectrograms at multiple resolutions. We use seven scales up to 20 kHz
for the Cocktail Party \[27\] dataset and four scales up to 4 kHz for
KUL dataset \[13\] to capture both transient and steady-state spectral
characteristics across their respective bandwidths.

To further improve perceptual realism, we use an adversarial ensemble of
Multi-Period Discriminators (MPD) \[32\] to model waveform periodicity
and a Multi-band Multi-scale Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT)
Discriminator (MBMSD) \[26\] to enforce multi-band spectral consistency.
The generator is optimized using hinge adversarial loss
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}`$ and $`L_{1}`$ feature-matching loss
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fm}}`$ on intermediate discriminator features,
while the discriminators are trained with the standard hinge objective.

### II-G Multi-phase Training Curriculum

The overall generator objective is given by,

```math
\mathcal{L}_{G}=\lambda_{\mathrm{recon}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{mel}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{adv}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{fm}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fm}}, \tag{3}
```

where $`\lambda_{\mathrm{recon}}`$, $`\lambda_{\mathrm{mel}}`$,
$`\lambda_{\mathrm{adv}}`$, and $`\lambda_{\mathrm{fm}}`$ are weighting
coefficients balancing their respective loss terms throughout training.
We set $`\lambda_{\mathrm{recon}}=1`$ to be fixed throughout training.

To preserve GAN stability and mitigate mode collapse during training, we
employ a six-phase curriculum that progressively introduces spectral and
adversarial objectives. In Phase 1, the model optimizes exclusively
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}`$ until the validation latent mean
squared error (MSE) falls below 0.87, establishing a stable latent
mapping prior to multi-modal supervision. Phase 2 gradually introduces
the Mel loss ($`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}`$), ramping
$`\lambda_{\mathrm{mel}}`$ from 2 to 15 to progressively incorporate
spectral supervision without destabilizing the learned latent
representation. Upon reaching $`\lambda_{\mathrm{mel}}=15`$, Phase 3
maintains joint latent and spectral optimization until the validation
loss decreases for at least one epoch. In Phase 4, the generator
parameters are frozen for 2 epochs to allow warm-up pre-training of the
discriminators, providing stable adversarial supervision. Phase 5 then
jointly optimizes the generator and discriminators, progressively
increasing $`(\lambda_{\mathrm{adv}},\lambda_{\mathrm{fm}})`$ in steps
of $`(0.25,0.5)`$ until the target values of $`(1.0,2.0)`$ are reached.
Finally, Phase 6 conducts full adversarial training until validation
performance plateaus, employing a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler with
patience of 3 epochs and a reduction factor of 0.5.

In the following section, we extend this framework into a causal
streaming formulation, OnlineNeuroCodec, which integrates an auditory
attractor mechanism to enable real-time target speech extraction.

## III Causal streaming extension of NeuroCodec

We extend NeuroCodec to a causal formulation, termed OnlineNeuroCodec.
The causal model preserves the latent-domain processing of the offline
formulation while restricting each prediction to the current input
window and the information accumulated from preceding windows.

Inference uses a sliding window comprising buffered context $`w_{b}`$
and a new input hop $`w_{c}`$, following NeuroHeed \[15\]. At each hop,
only the latent frames corresponding to $`w_{c}`$ are generated, while
the preceding $`w_{b}`$ frames provide causal context. The complete
streaming procedure, including the cold start and the running speaker
state introduced below, is given in Algorithm 1.

$`\mathcal{D}_{c}`$; EMA rate $`\alpha`$

1:  $`\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{past}}\leftarrow\emptyset`$;
$`\hat{\mathbf{s}}\leftarrow\emptyset`$

2:  for each hop $`h=1,\dots,N_{\mathrm{hops}}`$ do

3:   $`\mathbf{x}^{(h)},\mathbf{R}^{(h)}\leftarrow`$ window of length
$`w`$ ending at hop $`h`$

4:

$`\bar{\mathbf{E}}^{(h)}\leftarrow\mathrm{EEGEnc}(\mathbf{R}^{(h)})`$

5:   if $`h=1`$ then

### III-A Auditory Attractor via Running Speaker State

OnlineNeuroCodec preserves attended-speaker identity across hops by
maintaining a running latent representation of previous target
estimates, $`\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{past}}`$. From the second hop onward,
it is updated using an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) \[33\]:

```math
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{past}}\leftarrow\alpha\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{past}}+(1-\alpha)\hat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\,h}_{\mathrm{tgt}},\vskip-4.26773pt \tag{4}
```

where $`\hat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\,h}_{\mathrm{tgt}}`$ denotes the
target-latent estimate of the current hop and $`\alpha`$ is the EMA
smoothing coefficient, set to 0.7 based on validation performance. The
EMA combines accumulated speaker information with the latest estimate,
providing a stable speaker memory for subsequent extraction.

OnlineNeuroCodec therefore uses two complementary cues: a _neuronal
attractor_, in which EEG features provide the keys and values for
cross-attention, and an _auditory attractor_ derived from
$`\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{past}}`$. The latter is mean-pooled and linearly
projected to FiLM \[34\] scale and shift parameters $`(\gamma,\beta)`$,
which modulate the current mixture-latent query
$`\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{mix}}`$ before EEG cross-attention:

```math
[\gamma,\beta]=\mathrm{Linear}\!\left(\mathrm{Mean}(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{past}})\right), \tag{5}
```

```math
\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{\mathrm{mix}}=(1+\gamma)\odot\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{mix}})+\beta.\vskip-2.84544pt \tag{6}
```

Thus, previous target estimates provide speaker-dependent conditioning
for the current hop. Similar to the autoregressive attractor in
NeuroHeed \[15\], this mechanism self-enrolls the attended speaker over
time. However, NeuroHeed re-encodes previous waveform outputs using a
dedicated LSTM speaker encoder, whereas OnlineNeuroCodec updates the
speaker state directly in codec latent space using a parameter-free EMA,
avoiding an additional recurrent speaker module.

### III-B Hopwise Training Strategy

At the first hop, no preceding prediction is available. Therefore,
$`\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{past}}`$ is initialized from an EEG-conditioned
forward pass without auditory-history conditioning. During training,
speaker dropout with probability $`p=0.2`$ is applied so that the model
remains functional when the historical context is unavailable \[35\].

Training follows the streaming setting by applying the reconstruction
and spectral objectives of Section II-F to each output hop. For hop
$`h`$, $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}^{(h)}`$ operates in the latent
domain, whereas $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}^{(h)}`$ is evaluated on the
corresponding decoded speech segment. The overall online objective is

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{online}}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{hops}}}\sum_{h=1}^{N_{\mathrm{hops}}}\left(\lambda_{\mathrm{recon}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}^{(h)}+\lambda_{\mathrm{mel}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}^{(h)}\right),\vskip-2.84544pt \tag{7}
```

where $`N_{\mathrm{hops}}`$ is the number of output hops, and
$`\lambda_{\mathrm{recon}}`$ and $`\lambda_{\mathrm{mel}}`$ weight the
reconstruction and spectral terms, respectively. Averaging across hops
keeps the objective scale independent of sequence length.

During training, gradients are stopped at $`\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{past}}`$
between consecutive hops, truncating backpropagation across hop
boundaries while maintaining the state for causal conditioning.

## IV Datasets and Experiments

In this section, we detail the experimental setup and the datasets used
to evaluate the proposed method.

### IV-A Datasets

To evaluate our proposed method, we used two publicly available
multi-speaker EEG-audio datasets: Cocktail Party and KUL. The Cocktail
Party dataset \[27\] includes EEG recordings from 33 subjects (128
channels, 512 Hz) who completed 30 dichotic listening trials (60 s
each), attending to one of two simultaneous narrative speech streams
presented to opposite ears. The KUL data set \[13\] consists of 16
subjects with normal-hearing with 20 trials each; in our experiments, we
used the first eight trials (similar to prior work for fair
comparison \[15, 16\]), such that subjects do not attend to the same
speech stimulus repeatedly, where participants selectively attended one
of two concurrent Dutch narrative stories presented dichotically.

### IV-B Implementation Details

EEG signals are sampled at 128 Hz and were band-pass filtered between
0.1-45 Hz, followed by ICA-based artifact removal \[36\]. All
experiments use subject independent (SI) evaluation, where test subjects
are unseen during training, providing a more stringent assessment of
cross-subject generalization.

For the Cocktail Party dataset, EEG is additionally re-referenced to the
average mastoids. Subjects are randomly split with a fixed seed into
23/5/5, training/validation/test subjects, and the same SI split is used
for all compared models. For fair comparison with M3ANet \[14\],
BASEN \[19\], and MSFNet \[37\], trials are segmented into 2s
non-overlapping windows. The native 44.1 kHz audio is processed using
the pretrained 44.1 kHz DAC encoder \[26\], which has a downsampling
factor of 512 and yields an approximately 86 Hz latent frame rate.

For KUL, we use the preprocessed data released with NeuroHeed \[15\] and
perform 16-fold SI cross-validation. For fair comparison with the SI
NeuroSpex+ setting \[16\], recordings are segmented into 4s windows with
a 1-s hop. The original 8 kHz audio is upsampled to 16 kHz and processed
using the pretrained 16 kHz DAC encoder \[26\], which has a downsampling
factor of 320 and yields a 50 Hz latent frame rate. Note that the
difference in window sizes between the two datasets is to ensure fair
comparison with the prior work on each dataset. Additional
implementation details and hyperparameters are provided in the
supplementary material. The source code will be released upon
acceptance.

### IV-C Evaluation Metrics

Performance is evaluated using standard objective speech enhancement
metrics: Short-Time Objective Intelligibility (STOI) \[38\], Extended
Short-Time Objective Intelligibility (ESTOI) \[39\], and Perceptual
Evaluation of Speech Quality (PESQ) \[40\]. To ensure fair comparison
with existing baselines that only operate at 8 kHz sample rate, all
outputs are resampled to 8 kHz before objective evaluation. This
prevents the wider output bandwidth of NeuroCodec from providing an
inherent advantage in the reported objective metrics.

## V Results and discussion

This section details the experimental evaluation of the proposed
framework across two public datasets. We present (i) objective speech
quality metrics, (ii) an ablation study validating our core
architectural choices, (iii) a subjective listening test, and (iv) a
performance evaluation of the causal extension.

### V-A Experimental Results

Table I compares NeuroCodec with previous EEG-guided target speaker
extraction systems on the Cocktail Party and KUL datasets.

On the Cocktail Party dataset, BASEN \[19\] and M3ANet \[14\] are
reproduced using the authors’ publicly available implementations to
ensure a fair comparison by evaluating under the same SI split used for

directly from the Neuro-Spex+ paper \[16\], which reports the identical

performance compared to all other models, as it employs a
subject-dependent linear decoder for envelope reconstruction that is
trained exclusively on single-talker data. In contrast, when compared

### V-B Ablation Study

To understand the contribution of each architectural component, we
conduct controlled ablation experiments on the KUL dataset. As
summarized in Table II, we evaluate the proposed architecture across
three core dimensions: the fusion block backbone, the internal EEG
encoder architecture, and the training objectives.

#### V-B1 Temporal modeling architecture

We first investigate the temporal modeling architecture by comparing
three sequence modeling strategies: (i) a standard Transformer \[29\],
(ii) a Transformer with Snake activations \[42\], and (iii) the proposed
Causal Mamba. The baseline Transformer achieves lower scores, while the
Snake-enhanced Transformer improves all three metrics over the standard
Transformer. However, the Causal Mamba achieves the highest scores
across all three metrics.

#### V-B2 EEG encoder guidance and components

Next, we validate the necessity and design of the EEG encoder. To assess
the model’s dependence on EEG guidance, we replace the EEG input with
Gaussian noise. The substantial performance drop supports the conclusion
that the model relies on neural guidance for target extraction.
Furthermore, ablating specific internal components of the EEG encoder,
namely the GCN spatial mapping and the Multi-Scale 1D Convolutions
(MS-Conv1D), confirmed that capturing both cross-channel spatial
topologies and multi-scale temporal receptive fields is crucial to
extract better EEG features.

#### V-B3 Progressive training objectives

Finally, we compare the progressive addition of training objectives.
Using solely the latent-domain reconstruction loss
($`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}`$) serves as our baseline. Integrating
the multi-scale log-Mel spectrogram loss
($`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}`$) drastically improves perceptual
quality, while the further addition of the adversarial feature-matching
loss ($`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv/fm}}`$) leads to even more improvement.
The combined objective
($`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv/fm}}`$)
achieves the highest overall performance, reaching a PESQ of 2.5623 and
an ESTOI of 0.7544.

### V-C Perceptual Evaluation

To verify the perceptual quality of the generated output, we conducted a
controlled multi-stimulus listening test with 20 participants. The study
was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the University of
Moratuwa (No EDN/2025/012) and all participants provided written
informed consent. We used web Multiple Stimulus with Hidden Reference
and Anchor (MUSHRA)  \[43\] to compare NeuroCodec’s native 44.1kHz
output with the native output of the strongest reproducible baseline,
M3ANet \[14\]. The Cocktail Party dataset was used; KUL was excluded
because NeuroSpex+ \[16\] has no public implementation. Eight 2s samples
were randomly selected from the held-out test set.

![](arxiv-2610-29815v1--5557f089a4b2.figures/figure-3.webp)

Fig. 3: MUSHRA listening-test results on the Cocktail Party dataset.
Bandwidth-matched NeuroCodec outperforms M3ANet, while native NeuroCodec

Each trial presented five signals under blinded and randomized
conditions: the hidden clean reference, mixture, M3ANet,
bandwidth-matched NeuroCodec, and NeuroCodec under blinded and
randomized presentation. As shown in Fig. 3, the hidden clean reference
achieved a mean score of 94.76 (95% CI: 94.41-95.14) while NeuroCodec
achieved 82.24 (CI: 78.86-85.39).Bandwidth-matched NeuroCodec, obtained
by low-pass filtering the NeuroCodec output at 7.296 kHz to match the
effective bandwidth of M3ANet, achieved 58.49 (CI: 55.06-62.31),
compared with 49.63 (CI: 45.42-53.71) for M3ANet. This improvement under
matched bandwidth indicates that NeuroCodec’s perceptual advantage is
not solely attributable to its wider output bandwidth and is consistent
with benefits from the proposed extraction architecture. The
substantially higher score of the original NeuroCodec output further
demonstrates the perceptual benefit of wider-band speech reconstruction.
The mixture obtained a mean score of 22.41 (CI: 20.24-24.80). The
NeuroCodec-M3ANet difference was significant under a paired permutation
test ($`p=3.0\times 10^{-5}`$).

Enabling the auditory attractor improves OnlineNeuroCodec from 0.742 to

## VI Conclusion

In this paper we introduce NeuroCodec, a generative EEG-guided target
speaker extraction framework that predicts latent representations of a
frozen high-fidelity audio codec instead of estimating time-frequency
masks. By combining graph-based EEG modeling, cross-modal attention, and
Mamba-based temporal modeling, NeuroCodec achieves effective EEG-audio
fusion in a compact latent space. Experiments on two datasets
demonstrate improved speech intelligibility and perceptual quality over
existing approaches, as measured by both objective metrics and a
subjective listening study. NeuroCodec also enables 44.1kHz
reconstruction with spectral content extending to approximately 20 kHz
with minimal speaker interference, unlike prior work with limited output
bandwidth and speaker leakage. We extend the model to a causal streaming
version, OnlineNeuroCodec, which enables streaming TSE using a causal
auditory attractor derived from previous target estimates. This model
achieves vastly superior computational speed while retaining competitive
extraction quality. These results demonstrate the potential of
generative latent modeling for neuro-steered speech extraction and
future assistive hearing systems. However, OnlineNeuroCodec, like most
other contemporary models, remains far from deployment on embedded
hearing-aid hardware. Future work will explore autoregressive
codec-token prediction for fully causal generation with reduced
buffering requirements, potentially lowering algorithmic latency,
alongside model compression and low-power implementation.
