# Can Prosodic Style Be Inferred from Text Alone? Evidence from Unsupervised Acoustic Clusters

Abdul Rehman    Jian-Jun Zhang    Xiaosong Yang ^(†)^(†)thanks: The
authors are with the National Centre for Computer Animation, Bournemouth
University, U.K.

###### Abstract

Much of expressive text-to-speech research rests on an untested
assumption that written text carries enough information to select an
appropriate prosodic style for its delivery. Text-predicted style models
improve listener preference, and expressive-appropriateness evaluation
presupposes that context constrains style, yet neither measures the
assumption itself. This paper tests it as a falsifiable hypothesis
against style labels derived from acoustics alone. For each of six
speakers in a 1,200-hour conversational corpus, utterances are clustered
in the spaces of five speech models, including a prosody-only control,
and the cluster of held-out utterances is predicted from twelve text
embedding models. Three controls are applied: utterance length is erased
from the speech embeddings; accuracy is scored against the
majority-class floor of unbalanced clusters rather than uniform chance;
and a bag-of-words baseline measures word identity alone. Text predicts
the cluster above that floor for all six speakers ($`+0.111`$ top-3
accuracy), but bag-of-words achieves three quarters of this. Sentence
embeddings add only $`+0.026`$, largest for encoders _not_ trained for
sentence semantics and reversed by tree-based probes for all others.
Acoustic clusters are not compact in text embedding space in any of 360
configurations. The prosody-only space weakens the association for five
speakers, but not for the speaker showing it most strongly. Text thus
informs these delivery clusters mainly through word choice, whether as a
cue to prosody or as a marker of topic and recording situation, and
reference-free style selection cannot assume more.

###### Index Terms: 

Prosody, speech synthesis, sentence embeddings, style selection,
text-to-speech.

## I Introduction

Human speech communicates through two coupled channels: the linguistic
content (what is said) and its paralinguistic realization (how it is
said). Modern text-to-speech (TTS) systems handle the first channel well
but rely on external conditioning for the second: to obtain contextually
appropriate prosody, many systems require reference audio, explicit
style labels, or manually selected style tokens \[1, 2, 3, 4, 5\]. A
system asked to read an apology, a sponsor announcement, and a casual
aside should be able to infer from the text that each calls for a
different delivery, yet without explicit style conditioning it typically
cannot.

A substantial body of work suggests that this bottleneck is solvable
from the text side. A style representation predicted from text improves
listener preference over an unconditioned baseline \[6\], and a growing
family of successors predicts style or prosody from progressively richer
text-side inputs: cross-sentence context \[7, 8\], hierarchical emotion
distributions deduced solely from text \[9\], contrastively learned
text–prosody alignments \[10\], and diffusion models that sample prosody
conditioned on text \[11, 12\]. Work on expressive appropriateness
likewise shows that human judgments of whether a rendering is suitable
depend on discourse context rather than on the isolated waveform \[13\].
All of these results, however, presuppose rather than measure the
underlying premise: the prediction targets are latent style embeddings
or prosodic features co-trained with the synthesis model itself, and the
outcome measures are synthesis quality and listener preference. None of
this work quantifies how strongly text constrains prosodic style against
an explicit null, separates a text–prosody association from the two
trivial explanations that any such measurement admits, utterance length
and unbalanced label priors, or measures how much of it is carried by
word identity alone. More broadly, much of TTS research implicitly
expects style selection from text to emerge given enough model scale and
data \[14, 15\]; that expectation has, to our knowledge, never been
tested directly. This paper provides that test.

The question addressed is: can prosodic style be inferred from text
alone? Concretely, if the prosodic styles a speaker actually uses are
identified from acoustics without any text supervision, can those styles
be predicted from a text-only representation of what was said? The
defensible form of this hypothesis is probabilistic. A deterministic
mapping from text to prosody is arguably impossible: the same sentence
admits multiple contextually valid renderings depending on discourse
context and speaker intent, which are not recoverable from the text
itself \[16\]. What is tractable is the weaker claim that textual
content is not prosodically _neutral_, consistent with long-standing
evidence that intonation and speaking style reflect discourse structure
and communicative intent \[17, 18\]. The hypothesis under test and its
null are:

- $`H_{1}`$:
  Within an individual speaker’s natural speech, textual content
  statistically constrains prosodic style: a text-only representation of
  an utterance predicts its acoustically derived prosodic style category
  better than utterance length and label frequency do, and those
  categories occupy distinguishable regions of text embedding space.
- $`H_{0}`$:
  Prosodic style, as identified by unsupervised clustering of acoustics,
  is independent of text beyond utterance length and label frequency:
  text representations do not beat those references, and the style
  categories are geometrically indistinguishable in text embedding
  space.

Word identity is treated as a reference level rather than as a confound.
Individual words can themselves cue delivery, as interrogatives,
intensifiers, and discourse markers do, so an association carried by
word identity would still be usable by a TTS system; but the same
association would also arise if a speaker’s vocabulary merely tracks
topic and recording situation. A bag-of-words arm is therefore reported
alongside the sentence embeddings, and the question whether text is
informative at all is kept separate from the question whether
sentence-level representations add anything beyond the words they
contain.

The hypothesis is operationalized as a cross-modal cluster prediction
task (Fig. 1). Each speaker’s utterances are embedded by a speech
representation model and clustered; the cluster index serves as an
acoustically derived prosodic style label. Utterance embeddings are
mean-pooled over segments of up to 20 s, so these labels describe
utterance-level delivery, the register in which a stretch of speech is
produced, which is also the level at which reference encoders and style
tokens condition a TTS system \[3\]; local intonation contours such as a
final question rise are outside the scope of the design. The
transcriptions of the same utterances are embedded by a sentence
transformer, and $`H_{1}`$ is confronted with three falsifiable
predictions: (P1) text predicts the acoustic cluster of held-out
utterances above the majority-class floor and the length-only baseline,
with sentence embeddings adding to what bag-of-words achieves; (P2) the
acoustic clusters occupy distinguishable, even if overlapping, regions
of the text embedding space; and (P3) the alignment does not arise from
lexical content leaking through the speech representation, and therefore
persists when that representation contains no lexical channel. Each
prediction can fail independently. P3 addresses the most serious
confound of the design: general-purpose speech representations encode
phonetic, and hence lexical, content, so an apparent text–speech
alignment can be lexical leakage rather than prosodic coupling. The
speech-model pool therefore includes, alongside four self-supervised
models, a deliberately minimal prosody-only model (TIMBRESPACE,
Section III) that discards phonetic detail by construction.

The main contributions are:

1.  1.  To our knowledge, the first test of the hypothesis that prosodic
        style is inferable from text against independently derived
        (unsupervised, acoustics-only) style labels rather than co-trained
        latents. The assumption exploited, implicitly or by construction, in
        prior work \[6, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 8, 13\] is stated with an explicit
        null and evaluated across 5 speech representation models, 12 text
        embedding models, 6 classifier families, and 3 encoder depths, under
        a strict within-speaker protocol on six speakers. The speech
        encoders behind the labels are separately validated on labeled
        speech (Section VII), where the same frozen representations decode
        four-way emotion at 0.53 to 0.59 weighted $`F_{1}`$ against a 0.197
        majority floor.
2.  2.  A measurement protocol with the three controls such a test requires,
        and evidence that they are not optional: length-conditional erasure
        of the speech embeddings before clustering, which brings the
        length-only baseline down to the level of a constant predictor;
        scoring against the majority-class floor, which absorbs more than
        half of the apparent margin over uniform chance; and a bag-of-words
        baseline, which absorbs three quarters of what remains.
3.  3.  The resulting bounded result. P1 survives in a weak form: text
        predicts its own utterance’s acoustic cluster above the floor for
        all six speakers ($`+0.111`$ top-3), mostly through word identity,
        and sentence embeddings add only $`+0.026`$ over bag-of-words,
        positive for every speaker on average. P2 fails: acoustic clusters
        are not compact in text embedding space in any of 360
        configurations.
4.  4.  Two diagnostics on the character of the association. On the text
        side, roberta-base and distilbert-base-uncased, which are not
        trained for sentence similarity, give the largest increments and are
        the only encoders whose advantage over bag-of-words survives
        tree-based probes, while the 0.6B–1.5B instruction-tuned embedders
        rank last, which argues against a semantic reading of the increment.
        On the speech side, P3 holds only in part: the prosody-only
        representation retains less than half of the waveform encoders’
        association for five of six speakers, consistent with lexical
        leakage, yet most of it for the speaker with the strongest
        association, which lexical leakage cannot explain.

## II Background

### II-A Self-Supervised Speech Representations

Self-supervised (SSL) speech models learn representations from raw
waveforms that transfer well to supervised downstream tasks \[19\]. The
three dominant families differ in their pretraining targets: wav2vec 2.0
uses contrastive targets over masked frames \[20\], HuBERT predicts
iteratively refined K-means cluster assignments \[21\], and WavLM adds
speech denoising and augmentation to masked prediction \[22\]. The
ProsAudit benchmark \[23\] shows that SSL models encode structural
prosodic knowledge above chance, which supports treating their embedding
spaces as a source of prosodic structure. Downstream benchmark success,
however, does not imply that these models organize speech into coherent
_unsupervised_ structure: benchmark rankings are sensitive to
probing-architecture choices \[24\], speaker and content information
occupy largely orthogonal subspaces \[25, 26\], and an analysis of ten
pretrained SSL models by Rehman *et al.* \[27\] found that strong
classification performance frequently coexists with weak clustering
coherence for the same attribute. The present study responds with a
within-speaker clustering protocol, which controls for the dominant
speaker-identity structure in SSL embedding spaces, and with a
prosody-specific control representation alongside the general-purpose
waveform encoders.

### II-B Prosody and Style in TTS

Expressive TTS systems condition synthesis on style information obtained
from reference audio encodings \[1, 2, 3\], explicit pitch and duration
targets \[28, 29\], adversarial style discriminators built on SSL
representations \[4\], large-scale speech-token language modeling \[5\],
prosody-focused self-supervised objectives \[30, 31\], or discrete
latent style spaces \[32, 33\]. All of these mechanisms answer _how_ to
impose a chosen style; none answers _which_ style should be chosen for a
given input text.

A second line of work predicts the style from the text itself.
Text-predicted style models \[6\] train a predictor from text to the
latent style embedding of a GST-Tacotron; hierarchical context-aware
predictors extend the text-side input to neighboring sentences and
previously realized styles \[7\]; emotion-distribution predictors deduce
multi-level emotion intensities solely from text \[9\]; and
diffusion-based predictors replace deterministic regression with
sampling, motivated by the one-to-many character of the text-to-prosody
mapping \[11, 12\]. Closest of all are contrastive frameworks that align
text with prosody or style directly: CLAPSpeech \[10\] contrasts the
prosody of the same spoken token across different text contexts, and
TACA \[8\] learns an utterance-level text-aware style space under the
supervision of a speech-derived one. Related threads predict style
embeddings from natural-language style prompts \[34\], which supply the
style description explicitly, and model prosody from joint acoustic and
textual input, where the decoder is observed to rely chiefly on the
textual stream \[35\]. The consistent success of this family establishes
that _some_ usable signal exists in text, but the evidence is indirect:
the prediction targets are latent variables co-trained with the
synthesis model or the alignment objective, the outcome measure is
synthesis quality or subjective preference, and, with the partial
exception of CLAPSpeech’s same-token contrast, nothing in these designs
excludes lexical, length, or channel information as the carrier of the
learned association. From the evaluation side, Wang *et al.* \[13\]
formalize _expressive appropriateness_, whether given speech fits its
discourse context, and show that context-free judgments consistently
underperform context-aware ones; there, the text-to-style link is the
premise of the framework, not its object of measurement. None of this
work measures whether, and how strongly, text suffices to _choose_ the
style, which is the hypothesis test carried out here.

### II-C Sentence Embeddings

Sentence transformers \[36\] map text to dense vectors whose distances
reflect semantic similarity, with variants trained on natural language
inference \[37\], paraphrase pairs, and general-purpose contrastive
objectives \[38, 39, 40\]. Large language models and retrieval-augmented
generation \[41, 42\] would likely host an eventual deployment of
text-driven style selection. This study deliberately uses frozen text
embeddings with shallow classifiers instead, isolating the question of
whether the information is present and accessible from the engineering
question of how best to exploit it, and it includes plain
masked-language-model encoders alongside similarity-trained ones so that
“semantic” and “surface” explanations of any effect can be told apart.

## III A Minimal Prosody-Only Representation

Testing whether text--speech alignment is carried by prosody requires a
speech representation with ‘‘just enough’’ information about prosody and
timbre and as little as possible about lexical content. None of the SSL
baselines satisfies this: their waveform input retains full phonetic
detail. TIMBRESPACE is a simple transformer model built for this
purpose; code and checkpoints are publicly available¹¹ 1
[https://anonymous.4open.science/r/Timbrespace/](https://anonymous.4open.science/r/Timbrespace/),
and further implementation details are given in the supplementary
material.

TIMBRESPACE operates on six frame-level acoustic features extracted at
62.5 frames per second (50 ms analysis frames, 16 ms hop): fundamental
frequency (F0), F0 bandwidth, first and second formant frequencies (F1,
F2), RMS power, and a formant-to-remainder signal-to-noise ratio (SNR),
each normalized to $`[0,1]`$ over fixed, perceptually motivated ranges.
The set covers the primary acoustic correlates of prosody and voice
quality while excluding phoneme-discriminative representations such as
MFCCs. F1 and F2 carry some vowel information, but the feature set as a
whole is drastically less lexically informative than spectrogram or
waveform input, which is what makes the model useful as a
lexical-leakage control. The frames are quantized into a vocabulary of
120 prosodic tokens by weighted K-means, and an 8-layer, 8-head,
768-dimensional transformer encoder is trained with BERT-style masked
language modeling over the token sequences, using span masking (1 to 10
tokens) and a curriculum masking probability increasing from 1% to
20% \[44, 21\]. Training uses LibriSpeech \[45\] in two phases
(train-clean-360, then train-other-500). Utterance-level embeddings are
obtained by mean pooling frame representations from a chosen layer,
identically to the SSL baselines.

Two properties matter here, both measured directly in Section VII.
First, TIMBRESPACE embeddings cluster along paralinguistic dimensions
despite, or because of, their restricted input. Second, speaker identity
is the dominant axis in the space, as it is in SSL embedding
spaces \[27\] and in TTS style-embedding spaces, which cluster first by
speaker timbre and only then by style attributes \[34\]. Any
cross-speaker clustering would therefore produce speaker partitions
rather than style partitions, which motivates the strict within-speaker
protocol used throughout this paper.

## IV Methodology

### IV-A Task Definition

For each speaker, let $`\{(a_{i},x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}`$ denote utterances
with audio $`a_{i}`$ and transcription $`x_{i}`$. A speech
representation model $`S`$ produces utterance embeddings $`S(a_{i})`$
(mean-pooled over the utterance’s real frames from a fixed layer), which
are partitioned into $`K=30`$ clusters by K-means; the cluster index
$`y_{i}\in\{1,\dots,K\}`$ is the utterance’s acoustically derived
prosodic style label. A text embedding model $`T`$ produces sentence
embeddings $`T(x_{i})`$. $`H_{1}`$ is tested by (i) training classifiers
to predict $`y_{i}`$ from $`T(x_{i})`$ on a train split and evaluating
on held-out utterances, against the same classifiers trained on two
reference feature sets (prediction P1), and (ii) measuring the geometry
of the label partition $`\{y_{i}\}`$ directly in the text embedding
space (prediction P2). Clustering is fitted once per (speaker, speech
model, layer) and shared by all text models, so text-model comparisons
are free of clustering noise.

### IV-B Speech Representation Models

Table I lists the five models. The four SSL baselines span the three
major self-supervised families and were the top performers of their
families in the ten-model analysis of Rehman *et al.* \[27\]; pretrained
weights are taken from the torchaudio pipelines. wav2vec2 XLSR-53 \[43\]
additionally provides a multilingual reference, and TIMBRESPACE the
prosody-only control. All models are used frozen. Layers are indexed
proportionally on a 1–7 scale, index $`i`$ denoting the layer at $`i/7`$
of each model’s depth, so that a given index is the same relative depth
in every model and index 7 is the final layer. The information carried
by SSL layers varies strongly with depth \[46\], so the index is a
consequential choice. All main results use the final layer. Indices 1
and 3 were also run, and Section VIII-F reports the full comparison
across the three depths.

### IV-C Text Embedding Models

Twelve text embedding models are used: similarity-trained sentence
transformers (all-MiniLM-L6-v2 \[39\], all-mpnet-base-v2 \[38\],
paraphrase-distilroberta-base-v2 \[36\],
paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2); general-purpose dense retrievers
(nomic-embed-text-v1.5 \[40\], bge-large-en-v1.5, e5-large-v2); an
NLI-tuned encoder (bart-large-mnli \[37\]); large instruction-tuned
embedders (gte-Qwen2-1.5B-instruct, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B); and two plain
masked-language-model (MLM) encoders that are _not_ trained for sentence
similarity, roberta-base \[47\] and distilbert-base-uncased \[48\]. The
last pair functions as a weak-encoder control: if the measured effect is
semantic, models trained to represent sentence meaning should beat
models that merely pool token embeddings.

### IV-D Classifiers and Reference Baselines

Six scikit-learn classifiers, Logistic Regression, Random Forest,
K-Nearest Neighbors, Ridge Classifier, Linear Discriminant Analysis
(LDA), and Extra Trees, represent complementary geometric assumptions
and guard against the known sensitivity of representation evaluation to
the probing architecture \[24\]. Features are standardized with training
statistics and the training set is subsampled to 10,000 utterances; test
sets are never subsampled. All reported numbers use the
_best-performing_ classifier per configuration by weighted $`F_{1}`$.

This selection gives each arm its most suitable probe: LDA wins 359 of
360 text-embedding cells, whereas the TF-IDF arm is won by tree
ensembles in 228 cells and the length arm by Random Forest in 264.
Because it is made on the held-out split, it is a maximum over six
correlated estimates, and the selection gap (best minus mean over the
six classifiers) of 0.030, 0.022, and 0.014 weighted $`F_{1}`$
respectively gives the scale of the resulting optimism in each arm’s
absolute level. Every arm comparison is therefore also repeated with a
single classifier held fixed across arms (Section VIII-B), which removes
the selection step from the comparison.

Two reference arms run through the identical classifier suite on the
same labels and the same held-out utterances:

- •
  Length only: three features, the word count, character count, and
  heuristic syllable count of the text.
- •
  TF-IDF: bag-of-words vectors, up to 2,000 features with sublinear term
  frequency, over scikit-learn’s default word tokens (punctuation and
  single-character words excluded). This arm captures “this is the
  cluster where the speaker says _concrete_” without any sentence-level
  semantics or surface form.

The length arm is the floor that any text representation must clear. The
bag-of-words arm measures what word identity alone achieves; exceeding
it is not required for text to be informative about delivery.

### IV-E Confound Controls

Three properties of the pipeline could create text-predictable clusters
with no prosodic style involved. Each is controlled, and the controls
change the result materially.

#### IV-E1 Utterance Length

Mean-pooled speech embeddings encode input length almost perfectly: a
ridge predictor recovers log utterance length with $`R^{2}\geq 0.97`$
for every model tested. This code is structural, not prosodic. A
predictor trained on natural utterances also recovers the duration of
arbitrary mid-utterance crops, which contain no phrase boundary,
declination arc, or final lengthening, with a predicted-versus-true
slope of 1.01 to 1.12; excluding edge frames or subtracting per-position
profiles does not remove it. Without intervention, K-means therefore
partly returns duration bins. Two measures address this: embeddings are
L2-normalized before clustering, and the length-conditional mean is
removed, that is, clustering operates on
$`X-\mathrm{E}[X\mid\log\ell]`$, with the conditional mean fitted as a
cubic spline on the training split only and applied unchanged to test
data. Erasure cuts the length-only baseline’s lift by 40 to 70%. A
residual association with length remains ($`\eta^{2}=0.19`$ to
$`0.52`$), so the length arm is retained as a diagnostic; at the final
layer that residual is concentrated in the two HuBERT models
(Section VIII-A).

#### IV-E2 Sequence Length Beyond the Trained Range

TIMBRESPACE was trained on sequences of up to 622 tokens
($`\approx`$10 s), while 43.5% of analysis sequences are longer, and
whole-sequence inference on those shifted embeddings further than the
distance between two unrelated utterances (median cosine 0.757 for the
longest third). Long utterances are therefore encoded in overlapping
622-token windows with stride 311 and averaged per token, which leaves
short sequences bit-identical and brings the model’s length sensitivity
down to WavLM’s level. The waveform models were verified to be stable
over the same range (cosine $`\geq 0.96`$).

#### IV-E3 Clustering Instability

K-means at $`K=30`$ is sensitive to training-row order: shuffling rows
alone produced partitions with adjusted Rand index (ARI) 0.47 to 0.85
against each other. Runs therefore sort rows canonically, use float64
with ten initializations, seed all generators, and cache the fitted
clustering so that all twelve text models of a (speaker, speech model)
pair score the same partition. This makes every result reproducible, and
every comparison between text models or arms a comparison on one fixed
partition.

## V Datasets

The analysis uses a corpus of Creative-Commons-licensed YouTube speech:
approximately 1,200 hours from six speakers (three male, three female),
subsampled to 200 hours per speaker to equalize speaker effects,
spanning diverse topics, registers, and spontaneous conversational
contexts. Per-channel attribution, hours per speaker, and the data-use
statement are given in the supplementary material.

Each source video is denoised before any further processing, with a
pretrained speech-enhancement model \[49\] applied in 300 s windows with
10 s overlap and peak-normalized, and is then transcribed with a
Whisper-large-v3-turbo model using word-level timestamp
alignment \[50\]. Denoising suppresses background noise before any
embedding is computed, which narrows the recording-condition account
discussed in Section IX.

Audio is segmented along transcript boundaries, merging adjacent
segments separated by at most 1 s up to a 20 s maximum, and discarding
segments shorter than a minimum duration, longer than the encoder’s
maximum input, or carrying fewer than 5 word tokens per second, which
removes near-silent and sparsely transcribed spans; the transcriber’s
own silence handling means segments do not contain long pauses. This
yields 22,929 to 66,619 training and 5,840 to 15,488 test segments per
speaker. The text attached to a segment is its own transcript; when that
is shorter than 200 characters, neighboring segments’ text is prepended
and, if still short, appended, so that every text input carries at least
that much context.

Train–test partitioning is at the level of source videos, drawn randomly
but deterministically in an 80/20 ratio (426 to 1,270 training and 107
to 318 test videos per speaker), so no video contributes utterances to
both sides and classifiers cannot exploit within-video shortcuts. Random
rather than chronological selection also removes any systematic era
difference between training and test data; it does not remove topic
structure, since a channel’s recurring settings and subjects appear on
both sides of the split.

All training and evaluation is performed on _individual speakers_:
acoustic clusters are fitted independently on each speaker’s data, and
text-to-cluster prediction is trained and evaluated within that same
speaker’s utterances. The measured association therefore reflects how an
individual varies delivery with content, not differences between
speakers. The six speakers are accordingly treated as blocks, each a
complete replication of the experiment, rather than as a sample of a
population.

## VI Evaluation Metrics

### VI-A Classification Metrics and the Chance Level

Classifiers trained on text features predict acoustic cluster
assignments on held-out utterances, scored by top-3 accuracy (the
proportion of utterances whose true cluster is among the three
highest-scoring predictions; the relevant metric for style selection,
where proposing a small candidate set suffices) and weighted $`F_{1}`$,
reported as a robustness check.

With $`K=30`$ clusters, uniform chance for top-3 is $`3/K=0.10`$, but
K-means on speech embeddings does not produce balanced clusters (balance
scores 0.10 to 0.57 here, and near zero for one degenerate encoder). The
appropriate floor is therefore the majority-top-3 rate: the combined
share of a speaker’s three largest clusters, which is the accuracy of a
constant predictor that ignores the text entirely. This floor averages
0.255 across configurations and reaches 0.448 for the most unbalanced
one, so an apparently large margin over uniform chance can be worth
nothing. All claims below are stated against this floor.

### VI-B Geometric Coherence Metrics

Independently of any classifier, the acoustic-cluster partition is
evaluated directly in the text embedding space by the cosine silhouette
of the acoustic clusters computed on text embeddings (positive values
indicate that acoustically defined clusters are also semantically
compact) and by the separation ratio of mean inter-cluster to mean
intra-cluster distance. Raw coherence values are not comparable across
text models, whose cosine similarity scales differ, so only within-model
comparisons are made.

### VI-C Statistical Testing

The unit of replication is the speaker. Cells are averaged within a
speaker first, so that speakers contribute equally, and one-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests are then applied across the six speakers;
with $`n=6`$ the smallest attainable one-sided $`p`$ is $`.016`$ (all
six speakers agreeing) and five of six gives $`.031`$. Factor effects
(choice of text or speech embedding model) use Friedman tests with
speakers as blocks and Kendall’s $`W`$ as effect size; layer comparisons
use the same cells at each depth, each scored against its own floor,
with Friedman tests over depths and two-sided Wilcoxon tests between
them. Per-cell binomial tests against the floor, and per-cell
two-proportion tests between arms, are also reported, but they treat
held-out utterances as independent Bernoulli trials; since consecutive
utterances from the same video are correlated, these $`p`$-values are
_nominal_ and are read as descriptive indicators of effect strength.
Alongside the mean over configurations, the worst configuration per
speaker is reported: the mean describes the typical configuration, and
the worst cell bounds it from below.

## VII Validation on Labeled Speech

The style labels this paper predicts are unsupervised by design. To
verify that the embeddings behind them encode paralinguistic information
that listeners label consistently, we repeat the encoding and clustering
stages on IEMOCAP \[51\], which supplies per-utterance emotion and
speaker labels, using the identical pipeline: same encoders, same
pooling, same length erasure, same K-means.

We use the corpus-provided speaker-disjoint split (Sessions 1–4 for
training, Session 5 for test): 3,289 training and 1,176 test segments
over the four standard emotion classes, with seven distinct speakers
surviving filtering in training and two entirely unseen speakers in
test, so every probe below is speaker-independent. Embeddings are taken
at the first and the final encoder layer. Because the test split
contains one male and one female speaker, speaker identity and gender
induce the same binary partition of the test set; we report it as a
single _identity_ axis. Supervised probes are the classifier set of
Section IV-D, with the majority-class predictor as the floor.

### VII-C The Non-Lexical Control Behaves as Required

TIMBRESPACE is the weakest encoder under supervised probing, which is
expected: 768 dimensions derived from six acoustic streams with no
phonetic channel cannot compete with 1024- to 1280-dimensional waveform
encoders at recovering categorical labels. What matters for its role is
whether its space is _organized_ along paralinguistic axes, and under
unsupervised measurement it is the most strongly organized of the five.
At the final layer it leads every label-free metric on both axes: mean
$`\eta^{2}`$ at 69 times the null, three times the strongest SSL encoder
and 4.7 times the weakest, maximum $`\eta^{2}`$ of 0.248, cluster-label
ARI and adjusted mutual information, and cluster–emotion
$`\chi^{2}=506`$ ($`p<10^{-40}`$), while separating the two unseen
speakers at $`F_{1}=0.83`$ from acoustic features alone. Because the
pipeline consumes these embeddings without supervision, this is the
property it actually depends on.

### VII-D Layer Choice Changes Geometry More Than Decodability

Between the first and final layer, SSL probe performance is essentially
flat (within $`0.05`$ $`F_{1}`$), but the underlying geometry is not:
identity $`\bar{\eta}^{2}`$ falls by 6 to 25 times and the
cluster–identity ARI by 4 to 20 times, as the top layer specializes for
the masked-prediction objective. TIMBRESPACE is stable or improving
across layers, and its emotion $`\bar{\eta}^{2}`$ doubles at the final
layer. A supervised probe is therefore a poor instrument for selecting a
layer for a clustering pipeline, and the unsupervised side does not
settle the choice either: emotion $`\bar{\eta}^{2}`$ is equal or
slightly higher at the first layer for the four SSL encoders, whereas at
the final layer their geometry is far less dominated by voice identity
and the prosody-only control is at its most emotion-aligned. One encoder
is degenerate at the final layer: ten principal components carry 91.8%
of XLSR-53’s variance, against 33 to 54% for the others, so its tight
clusters (silhouette $`+0.18`$) sit in a nearly low-rank space. The same
collapse recurs on the main corpus. Because labeled speech does not
decide between depths, the choice for the main experiments is made on
the main corpus itself (Section VIII-F).

## VIII Results

The experiment comprises 360 configurations, 6 speakers $`\times`$ 5

degenerate partition (Section VIII-D). It is kept in every mean, since
the majority floor scores its imbalance correctly, and results without

### VIII-C Does Better Semantics Help?

If the residual beyond word identity were semantic, encoders trained to
represent sentence meaning should extract more of it than encoders that
merely pool token embeddings. The opposite holds (Fig. 3). roberta-base
($`+0.063`$) and distilbert-base-uncased ($`+0.051`$) lead every other
encoder and rank first and second for each of the six speakers
individually; roberta-base beats every non-MLM encoder in at least 29 of
the 30 speaker–speech blocks. The seven similarity-trained sentence
encoders fall between $`+0.014`$ and $`+0.029`$ and the NLI-tuned
bart-large-mnli at $`+0.032`$, while the 0.6B and 1.5B instruction-tuned
embedders rank last ($`+0.008`$ and $`+0.004`$). The text-model effect
is itself significant (Friedman over the twelve encoders,
$`\chi^{2}(11)=48.5`$, $`p=1.2\times 10^{-6}`$, $`W=0.74`$ for top-3;
$`\chi^{2}(11)=48.9`$, $`p=1.0\times 10^{-6}`$, $`W=0.74`$ for weighted
$`F_{1}`$), so this ordering is not noise.

The natural reading is that the residual lives in _surface form_, such
as word order, function words, punctuation, disfluency, and sub-word
composition, which raw MLM token embeddings retain and
similarity-trained encoders deliberately normalize away; it shrinks
rather than grows with model scale, and Table V shows that only the MLM
encoders keep their advantage under probes that can split on single
words. Part of that surface form may be prosodically meaningful in its
own right. The bag-of-words arm discards punctuation (Section IV-D), so
a question mark that the MLM encoders see is invisible to it; their
advantage therefore argues against a _semantic_ reading of the
increment, not against a prosodic one.

Prediction P3 states that the association should persist for a speech
representation with no lexical channel. It persists, but weakened
(Figs. 4 and 5). The three working SSL waveform encoders reach 0.365 to
0.419 top-3, a lift of $`+0.157`$ to $`+0.174`$ over their floors, while
TIMBRESPACE reaches 0.274, a lift of $`+0.070`$ that is significant in
50 of its 72 configurations but below each of the three waveform
encoders for every one of the six speakers (Friedman over the four
encoders, $`\chi^{2}(3)=11.6`$, $`p=.009`$). The increment over TF-IDF
is also smallest for TIMBRESPACE ($`+0.010`$, against $`+0.026`$ to
$`+0.035`$ for the waveform encoders) and negative for two speakers,
although at this depth the increments do not differ significantly across
speech models (Friedman $`p=.50`$).

XLSR-53 is a separate case. Its final layer is nearly low-rank: ten
principal components carry 92% of its variance, against 43 to 57% for
the other encoders, and K-means returns a few large, tightly separated
clusters (acoustic silhouette $`+0.29`$, against $`-0.08`$ to $`-0.05`$
elsewhere; balance 0.01), the three largest holding 40% of held-out
utterances. No text arm beats that floor on average (embedding-arm lift
$`-0.015`$), although the embedding arm still exceeds TF-IDF
($`+0.026`$). The same collapse appears at the final layer on IEMOCAP
(Section VII-D) and is absent at the two earlier depths
(Section VIII-F), so it is a property of this checkpoint’s final layer
rather than of the corpus or the pipeline. Its well-separated but
text-unpredictable clusters also show that cluster compactness and text
predictability are distinct properties.

The weakening is not uniform across speakers. For five of the six,
TIMBRESPACE retains between none and about a third of the three waveform
encoders’ lift, the pattern expected if lexical content leaks from the
speech embeddings into the cluster labels. The sixth, F3, whose
association is the largest in the corpus, does not fit this account. The
prosody-only representation retains 81% of the waveform encoders’ lift
for F3 ($`+0.273`$ against $`+0.338`$), bag-of-words alone predicts its
clusters at $`+0.262`$ above the floor, and the ratio is 0.69 to 0.78 at
every depth of Section VIII-F. Structure that text predicts in a
representation without a phonetic channel cannot be lexical leakage
through the speech encoder. It must be carried by pitch, formant,
energy, or SNR differences: either by delivery, or by recording
conditions, which the two channel-sensitive features, power and SNR,
would register (Section IX). As a lexical-leakage control, TIMBRESPACE
therefore gives a speaker-dependent answer: leakage can account for most
of the association in five speakers, but not in the speaker who
contributes most of it.

The gap between TIMBRESPACE and the waveform encoders also co-varies
with model capacity. Across the 30 speaker–speech pairs, the lift over
the floor correlates with the width of the speech embedding
($`\rho=0.38`$ speaker-centered, $`0.45`$ without XLSR-53, against
$`0.77`$ at the first layer), and TIMBRESPACE, the narrowest encoder
(768 dimensions against 1024 and 1280), also produces the least compact
clusters, with roughly half the Calinski–Harabasz score of the working
SSL models. Wider encoders yield more separable clusters, which are
easier to predict from any input, so the weaker average association of
the prosody-only representation may in part reflect that narrower, less
separable clusters are harder to predict. Pretraining domain does not
explain the gap: the two HuBERT models, which show among the strongest
associations, were pretrained on read audiobook speech, as TIMBRESPACE
was. The compactness account is not the whole story either: on labeled
speech the same encoder produces the least compact clusters and yet the
_best_ alignment with emotion and speaker (Section VII), so low cluster
quality does not by itself imply a representation with little
recoverable structure.

### VIII-E Cluster Geometry

Prediction P2 is not supported. The cosine silhouette of the acoustic
clusters measured in text embedding space is negative in _all_ 360
configurations (range $`-0.230`$ to $`-0.025`$), with a mean separation
ratio of 0.253, above one in only 6 of 360: utterances sharing an
acoustic cluster are no closer in text space than utterances from
different clusters. This holds even though the coherence metrics are
computed on the most favorable subsample available, the at most 100
utterances nearest each cluster centroid. A classifier can still extract
a weak signal from systematic differences in mean location, but the
clusters are not “about” distinct content or registers in any geometric
sense.

The geometry is not uninformative, though. While the silhouette is, if
anything, inversely related to predictability ($`\rho=-0.34`$ across the
30 speaker–speech pairs), the _separation ratio_ tracks it
($`\rho=0.61`$, and $`0.58`$ after centering within speakers). As a
reference level, the clusters are only weakly formed in the acoustic
space that defines them (mean silhouette $`-0.08`$ to $`-0.05`$ for the
four non-degenerate encoders), so compactness is a demanding criterion
for a 30-way partition in either modality.

### VIII-G Verdict on the Null

$`H_{0}`$ is rejected, and what replaces it is a small, lexically
carried association. P1 is satisfied in a weak form: all six speakers
clear the majority floor ($`p=.016`$), three quarters of that
association is available from word identity alone, and the embedding
arm’s advantage over bag-of-words, although positive for all six
speakers, holds for similarity-trained encoders only under linear
probes. The association varies more across speakers than across models.
Speaker identity accounts for 55% of the variance in top-3 accuracy
across the grid, against 25% for the speech model and 3% for the text
model; one speaker (F3) contributes a lift of $`+0.255`$ against
$`+0.082`$ averaged over the other five; and in each speaker’s worst
configuration the lift over the floor is negative for all six, in every
case an XLSR-53 configuration, and for two of six when that encoder is
set aside. P2 fails in every configuration at every depth, and P3 holds
only in part (Section VIII-D). The hypothesis that text alone identifies
prosodic style is therefore supported in a bounded form: text is
informative about these utterance-level delivery clusters, mostly
through which words are used, and sentence-level meaning adds little.

## IX Discussion

### IX-A A Lexically Mediated Association

The association that survives all three controls is real but narrow, and
its character is not what the TTS literature assumes: it is carried by
vocabulary. Two accounts of that vocabulary effect are consistent with
the results of Section VIII, and they differ in what a TTS system could
do with it. In the first, a speaker’s vocabulary co-varies with the
situation in which they are recorded: what someone talks about changes
with where, when, and how they are recorded, and the acoustic embedding
registers the accompanying differences in vocal effort, distance, room,
and microphone. The text side then predicts a cluster without any
prosodic mediation. Denoising eliminates the crudest version of this
channel story; microphone timbre, reverberation, distance, and vocal
effort survive it, and RMS power and SNR are two of the prosody-only
model’s six features. The dominant speaker fits this account: F3’s
clusters are almost as text-predictable in a space of pitch, formant,
energy, and SNR features as in the waveform encoders.

In the second account, words are genuine cues to delivery.
Interrogatives, intensifiers, discourse markers, and punctuation signal
the register in which a stretch of speech is produced, so an association
carried by word identity would be prosodic coupling through the lexicon
rather than a confound, and would be usable by a TTS system as it
stands. The accounts make different predictions about which words carry
the association, content words, which track topic, or function words,
discourse markers, and punctuation, which cue delivery, and about how
strongly each speaker’s clusters align with source video; both are the
natural next tests within this protocol. The results do not establish
that prosody is unpredictable from text. They establish that at the
granularity of this design, unsupervised 30-cluster partitions of
mean-pooled within-speaker utterance embeddings, and with trivial
explanations controlled, text informs delivery mostly through which
words are used.

### IX-B The Controls Are the Finding

Without the controls of Section IV-E, the same data would have supported
a strong positive result, and each control removes a specific share of
it. Pooled speech embeddings encode input length structurally, so
clustering them partly returns duration bins that text length predicts
trivially; after erasure the length arm falls to the level of a constant
predictor. The control is conservative: duration is a genuine
paralinguistic cue as well as a confound, so erasing it biases every arm
towards the null. The majority floor absorbs more than half of the
apparent margin over uniform chance: the collapsed final layer of
XLSR-53 reaches 0.384 top-3, nearly four times uniform chance, without
beating a constant predictor. The bag-of-words arm separates “text is
associated with delivery” from “sentence meaning is associated with
delivery”: without it, the 0.111 lift over the floor would be attributed
to semantics, when three quarters of it is word identity. We recommend
all three as minimum practice for claims of this type, whatever their
sign.

### IX-C Implications for Reference-Free TTS

A retrieval mechanism that selects prosodic parameters by matching input
text against the transcriptions of each style cluster would, in this
corpus, operate at roughly 0.37 top-3 against a 0.26 constant-predictor
floor, with most of that margin carried by vocabulary. To the extent
that vocabulary reflects topic rather than delivery, such a mechanism
would retrieve the styles a speaker uses when discussing similar
subjects, which is not the same as the style the sentence calls for.
This bounds, rather than contradicts, the systems that exploit the
text-to-style link inside a synthesis pipeline \[10, 8, 6\]: they
co-train the style representation with the objective, which may recover
structure that unsupervised acoustic clustering does not expose, and
their gains are measured by listener preference, which a weak but
non-zero signal can still improve. What the result does undercut is the
expectation that scale and data alone will deliver style selection from
text \[14, 15\]: the largest text encoders extracted the least beyond
bag-of-words.

### IX-D Limitations

The claims above are bounded by the design in the following ways.
_Labels._ The style labels are unsupervised acoustic partitions, not
perceptually validated speaking styles. Section VII establishes that the
embeddings being clustered carry decodable paralinguistic information
and that the prosody-only space is organized along it, while categorical
emotion accounts for little of a 30-way partition (cluster–emotion ARI
at most 0.024). What the clusters index beyond that, delivery or
recording context, is the question of Section IX; per-cluster acoustic
profiling and listening tests would address it. _Clustering._ Each
(speaker, speech model) pair uses one clustering at $`K=30`$, shared by
all text models and arms, so comparisons among them are made on
identical labels, whereas absolute levels would vary across seeds (ARI
0.47 to 0.85 between reorderings). _Probes._ The classifier is selected
on the held-out split, so absolute levels are optimistic on the scale
given in Section IV-D, and the central comparison is reported under
fixed probes for that reason. _Speech models._ The speech-model
comparison is not matched for embedding width or cluster compactness
(Section VIII-D). _Corpus._ Each speaker is one YouTube channel, which
the within-speaker design holds fixed, but recording situation varies
within a channel and recurring topics and settings appear on both sides
of the split. Transcriptions are ASR-generated, so every text arm
follows the transcriber’s punctuation and disfluency conventions, ASR
error patterns co-vary with acoustic conditions, and the bag-of-words
arm differs from the embedding arms in access to punctuation as well as
to sentence-level representation. All data are English conversational
YouTube speech from six speakers, analyzed as blocks; read speech, other
languages, and population-level inference are outside the scope of this
study.

These bounds notwithstanding, the test is made under the conditions of
practical use. The corpus is spontaneous, in-the-wild speech spanning
many topics, settings, and recording situations, which is the material a
deployed reference-free system has to handle, and a text-to-style link
robust enough to be relied upon should be measurable under such
conditions and not only in controlled read speech.

## X Conclusion

This paper turned a foundational assumption of expressive TTS, that an
appropriate prosodic style can be inferred from text alone, into a
falsifiable hypothesis with an explicit null, and tested it against
independently derived, acoustics-only style labels under three controls:
length erasure before clustering, a majority-class floor in place of
uniform chance, and a bag-of-words reference arm. Across 360
configurations spanning six speakers, five speech representation models
including a prosody-only control, twelve text embedding models, and six
classifier families, with three encoder depths compared, the result is a
weak, lexically mediated association. Text predicts its own utterance’s
acoustic cluster above the majority floor for all six speakers
($`+0.111`$ top-3), but bag-of-words alone delivers about three quarters
of that lift. Sentence embeddings add $`+0.026`$, an increment that
changes sign with the probe for every similarity-trained encoder and is
largest for encoders not trained for sentence semantics. The association
is weaker in the prosody-only representation for five of six speakers,
but not for the speaker in whom it is strongest. Acoustic clusters are
not compact in text embedding space in any configuration, speaker
identity accounts for over half of the variance in the headline metric,
and these conclusions hold at each encoder depth at which they could be
tested.

Within this design, then, text informs utterance-level delivery mainly
through which words are used. Whether those words act as cues to
delivery or as markers of topic and recording situation is the question
this result opens, and reference-free style selection cannot assume more
than this. The protocol extends directly to the analyses that would
answer it: separating content words from delivery cues in the
bag-of-words vocabulary, relating clusters to source video, and
perceptual validation of the clusters. Further extensions are listed in
the supplementary material.
