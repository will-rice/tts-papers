###### Abstract

Large pretrained text-to-speech (TTS) models sound almost human for
well-resourced languages, but much worse for languages that are rare in
their training data. We study this quality gap for Khmer and Korean
using VoxCPM2, a 2.4B-parameter, tokenizer-free TTS model that joins a
MiniCPM-4 language-model backbone with a flow-matching diffusion
decoder. We build one shared, language-tagged corpus of 25.5 hours after
cleaning and adapt VoxCPM2 with a single Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
adapter, trained on both languages at once and added to both the
language model and the decoder. The adapter is zero-initialized, so
training starts exactly at the original zero-shot model. In
native-speaker listening tests, the Khmer Mean Opinion Score (MOS) rises
from 3.85 to 4.23 with the best adapter, rank 64. This gain is highly
significant under a paired Wilcoxon test with $`p<0.001`$, and it is
achieved while training only 0.19 to 3.03 percent of the parameters. Two
findings stand out. First, the training loss and human ratings disagree
on the best rank. The loss is lowest at rank 128, but MOS peaks at rank 64. Second, the same adapter gives no significant gain for Korean, which
the base model already covers well, and a high rank even hurts quality.
This shows that adaptation helps mainly where the base model is truly
weak.

## I Introduction

Neural text-to-speech (TTS) has improved quickly. Early systems
predicted acoustic features, such as Tacotron 2 \[1\] and
FastSpeech 2 \[2\]. Newer end-to-end and large generative systems \[3,
4\] now sound close to human for well-resourced languages such as
English and Mandarin. The main reason is large-scale pretraining.
Foundation models such as VoxCPM \[5\] and its successor VoxCPM2 \[6\]
are trained on millions of hours of multilingual speech and synthesize
highly natural, context-aware speech.

Even so, this speech is not yet equal to a real human speaker. The base
model is already useful, and its zero-shot output is reasonably good,
but it still mispronounces some words, has unnatural prosody, and sounds
less natural than a native speaker. This shortfall is larger for
under-resourced languages that appear rarely in pretraining \[7, 8\],
and our goal is to close this remaining gap between the model and
natural human speech. We study Khmer, the official language of Cambodia
and a genuinely low-resource language; unlike English or Korean, Khmer
is written without spaces between words \[9\]. To test whether
adaptation helps every language or only those with a real deficit, we
pair Khmer with Korean, a language that the base model already covers
well.

The usual fix is full fine-tuning, which updates every parameter. It can
restore quality, but it is costly because it needs a lot of compute and
storage, a separate copy of a multi-billion-parameter model for each
language, and it can make the model forget what it already knew.
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) avoids this. In particular,
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) \[10\] keeps the pretrained weights frozen
and trains only small low-rank matrices, so only a tiny fraction of the
parameters change. LoRA is well proven for large language models \[10,
11\]. For speech, two questions are still open. The first is how well it
closes the quality gap for low-resource TTS, and the second is whether a
single shared adapter can serve several very different languages at the
same time.

This paper investigates whether a small LoRA adapter on VoxCPM2 can
close this gap, using Khmer and Korean, two languages that the base
model covers to different degrees. Our contributions are:

- •
  One shared adapter for two languages and two modules. We train a
  single LoRA adapter on Khmer and Korean together, and we add it to
  both the MiniCPM-4 language model and the flow-matching decoder. One
  small adapter, which trains only $`0.19`$ to $`3.03\%`$ of the
  parameters, then serves both scripts, with no separate model per
  language. As far as we know, this is the first parameter-efficient
  adaptation of a foundation TTS model for Khmer.
- •
  Adaptation helps only where the base model is weak. We measure
  native-speaker MOS and test for significance. The same adapter gives a
  large, highly significant gain for Khmer, which the base model covers
  poorly, as overall MOS rises from 3.85 to 4.23, an improvement of 0.38
  points with $`p<0.001`$. It gives no significant gain for Korean,
  which the base model already covers well, since the best rank is only
  0.11 points higher, with $`p=0.49`$. A high rank even makes Korean
  worse. So the adapter fills a real gap, rather than helping every
  language equally.
- •
  Training loss does not predict the best rank. We test ranks 8, 16, 32,
  64, and 128. The validation loss is lowest at rank 128, but Khmer MOS
  is highest at rank 64 for naturalness, prosody, and pronunciation, and
  then drops. So the loss overstates the value of extra capacity. The
  rank should be chosen by listening tests, and the small rank-8 adapter
  already recovers most of the gain.
- •
  Adaptation as a simple probe of what the model already knows. Because
  the adapter starts at the exact zero-shot model, how much it helps,
  and whether it helps at all, shows how much of a language the base
  model already learned. The useful rank grows with this gap. This gives
  clear advice to use rank 64 for Khmer, to avoid fine-tuning Korean for
  overall quality, and to avoid rank 64 or higher for Korean. It also
  shows that one global rank is wrong when languages differ.

## II Related Work

### II-A Neural Text-to-Speech

Modern TTS began with two-stage neural pipelines. Tacotron 2 \[1\]
predicts mel-spectrograms autoregressively and pairs them with a neural
vocoder, while FastSpeech 2 \[2\] introduced non-autoregressive
synthesis with explicit duration, pitch, and energy modeling. Fully
end-to-end systems such as VITS \[3\] combine variational inference with
adversarial training and normalizing flows. Large generative models then
recast TTS as a conditional language-modeling or diffusion problem, and
VALL-E \[4\] frames zero-shot TTS as neural-codec language modeling.
VoxCPM \[5\] departs from discrete-codec approaches with a
tokenizer-free design that models continuous acoustic representations,
and VoxCPM2 \[6\] scales this to a 2.4B-parameter model that pairs a
MiniCPM-4 \[12\] backbone with a flow-matching \[13\] diffusion decoder.
We adopt VoxCPM2 as our base model.

### II-B Multilingual and Low-Resource TTS

Extending speech technology to under-resourced languages is a
long-standing challenge. Massively multilingual efforts such as the
Massively Multilingual Speech project \[7\] and the Common Voice
corpus \[14\] expand language coverage. For zero-shot and cross-lingual
TTS, YourTTS \[15\] and XTTS \[16\] demonstrate transfer to new speakers
and languages from limited data. Nevertheless, languages such as Khmer
remain data-scarce, and quality for nominally supported low-resource
languages typically lags that of high-resource languages, motivating
targeted adaptation.

### II-C TTS Adaptation

A body of work adapts pretrained TTS models to new speakers, styles, or
languages from limited data \[17\]. AdaSpeech \[18\], for example,
adapts a model while updating only a small set of parameters,
foreshadowing parameter-efficient approaches. These methods establish
that high-quality adaptation need not retrain the full model. Our work
extends this line to lightweight, jointly multilingual adaptation of a
2.4B-parameter foundation TTS model, where a single shared low-rank
adapter serves two typologically distinct, under-resourced languages at
once.

### II-D Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning adapts large pretrained models while
updating only a small subset of parameters. LoRA \[10\] injects
trainable low-rank matrices into frozen weight matrices, and
QLoRA \[11\] further reduces memory via quantized backbones. PEFT is
standard for adapting large language models and is increasingly applied
to speech \[19\]. Its use for closing the quality gap in under-resourced
TTS, and whether a single shared adapter can jointly serve multiple
typologically distinct languages, has received limited attention; we
address this directly.

## III Methodology

Fig. 1: Proposed shared-LoRA fine-tuning pipeline for VoxCPM2.

### III-A Model

Figure 1 gives an overview of our pipeline. We build on VoxCPM2 \[6\], a
tokenizer-free TTS model with about $`2.39\times 10^{9}`$ parameters.
The input is a text prompt, which is normalized, segmented, and
BPE-tokenized before entering the model. VoxCPM2 has two parts. The
first is a MiniCPM-4 \[12\] language-model backbone with hidden size
2048, 28 transformer layers plus 8 residual layers, 16 attention heads
with 2 key/value heads, and a vocabulary of 73,440. In this backbone, a
text-semantic stage (TSLM), a finite scalar quantization stage (FSQ),
and a residual-acoustic stage (RALM) turn the tokens into an acoustic
representation. The second is a flow-matching \[13\] diffusion
transformer (DiT) decoder, shown as a local DiT (LocDiT) followed by the
AudioVAE V2 vocoder. It produces continuous acoustic features with
feature dimension 64 and patch size 4, and renders 48 kHz audio. Unlike
systems that use discrete codec tokens \[4\], VoxCPM2 predicts
continuous features directly, which avoids codec quantization artifacts.

### III-B Corpus

We build one corpus for Khmer (km) and Korean (ko) from public and
in-house sources, summarized in Table I. These are a Khmer corpus
provided by the Institute of Digital Research and Innovation (IDRI),
Cambodia; the Korean Single Speaker (KSS) corpus \[20\]; and Korean
Common Voice/FLEURS \[14, 8\]. We prepare the data in four steps. In
Step 1, aggregation, we pair each clip with its transcript and measure
its duration. In Step 2, cleaning, we drop clips shorter than $`0.5`$ s
or longer than $`20`$ s and check that audio and text match. In Step 3,
tokenization, we add a language tag, \[km\] or \[ko\], to the front of
each transcript, encode it with the VoxCPM2 tokenizer, whose vocabulary
has 73,440 entries, and drop clips with more than 256 text tokens. Steps
2 and 3 together remove $`7.1\%`$ of the Khmer clips and $`0.7\%`$ of
the Korean clips. In Step 4, manifest construction, we split each
language 90/10 into train and validation, then upsample, that is,
repeat, the Khmer training clips $`2.81\times`$ until Khmer is $`40\%`$
of the training mix, to make up for its scarcity. We keep the validation
split at its natural ratio, so the loss is measured fairly. Table I
reports utterances and hours at every stage. Its rows follow the four
steps above. Raw is the aggregated corpus, and Cleaned is what survives
Steps 2 and 3. Train, unique and Validation are the two halves of the
90/10 split, and Train, upsampled is the manifest we actually train on,
which counts each repeated Khmer clip once per repetition. Because
upsampling repeats existing audio rather than adding new material, that
manifest spans $`28.31`$ h of rows drawn from only $`22.95`$ h of unique
audio; we report both, and all corpus sizes quoted elsewhere in this
paper refer to unique audio after cleaning.

### III-C Joint Multilingual LoRA Adaptation

Rather than fine-tuning all parameters, we attach a single shared
LoRA \[10\] adapter to the frozen backbone. For a pretrained weight
matrix $`W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}`$, LoRA constrains the update to
a low-rank product:

```math
W=W_{0}+\Delta W=W_{0}+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA,
```

Here $`A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}`$ uses the Kaiming-uniform
initialization and $`B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}`$ is set to zero. So
$`\Delta W=0`$ at the start, and training begins exactly at the original
zero-shot model. We add the adapter to the query, key, value, and output
projections of attention in both the language model, covering its base
and residual layers, and the DiT decoder. The feed-forward linears and
the audio VAE stay frozen. We set $`\alpha=2r`$ and try ranks 8, 16, 32,
64, and 128. This is $`4.5`$ to $`72.4`$ million trainable parameters,
or $`0.19`$ to $`3.03`$ percent of the base model.

Importantly, we train one adapter on Khmer and Korean together, using
the language-tagged data. So a single set of low-rank matrices learns
both scripts. The language tag tells the model which language it is
reading, so the adapter can share capacity but still keep the scripts
apart. This means we do not need a separate model or adapter for each
language.

### III-D Training Configuration

We train every adapter with the AdamW optimizer with
$`\beta_{1}{=}0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}{=}0.999`$, and weight decay $`0.01`$.
The peak learning rate is $`1\times 10^{-4}`$, with a 200-step linear
warmup and then cosine decay to zero. The effective batch size is 16,
from a micro-batch of 4 with gradient accumulation of 4. We clip
gradients at $`1.0`$ and use bfloat16 mixed-precision training; the
audio VAE stays in float32. Each run is 10,000 steps, validated every
500 steps. We use one NVIDIA H200 GPU, and each rank takes about 2.6
hours at around 1.07 steps per second.

### III-E Evaluation Metrics

Our main automatic metric is the validation flow-matching loss, the
diffusion objective measured on the held-out validation split. A lower
value means a better fit to the target speech. We also track the
stop-token loss. Because the adapter starts at zero, the loss at the
start of training equals that of the zero-shot base model, so the drop
during training shows how much of the gap the adapter closes. We also
synthesize speech. For each rank and for the base, we generate the same
Khmer and Korean sentences at 48 kHz. Finally, we run MOS listening
tests for both languages, reported in Tables III and IV. For each
language, five male and female native speakers rate every system on a
5-point scale along three axes, namely naturalness, prosody, and
pronunciation, over 20 sentences. Let $`r_{s,a,i}`$ be the score that
rater $`i`$ gives system $`s`$ on axis
$`a\in\{\mathrm{nat},\mathrm{pros},\mathrm{pron}\}`$, and let
$`\bar{m}_{s,a}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}r_{s,a,i}`$ be its mean. The overall
MOS is the mean of the three axes:

```math
\mathrm{MOS}_{s}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{a}\bar{m}_{s,a}.
```

We compare each system with the zero-shot base using a paired Wilcoxon
signed-rank test. We also give 95% confidence intervals from a bootstrap
with 10,000 resamples and report inter-rater agreement with
Krippendorff’s $`\alpha`$. The two languages used different rater
panels, so we compare systems only within a language, never across
languages.

## IV Results and Discussion

### IV-A Rank Sweep and Perceptual Results

Table II shows the trainable fraction, adapter size, and validation loss
at 10,000 steps for the base and each rank. Tables III and IV show the
native-speaker MOS, by axis, for Korean and Khmer.

Automatic loss. The validation flow-matching loss drops from about
$`0.83`$ for the zero-shot base to between $`0.71`$ and $`0.73`$ for
every rank. The loss is lowest at rank 128, with a value of $`0.7094`$.
Rank 8 has the second-lowest loss of $`0.7243`$, yet its adapter is only
$`18`$ MB, about $`15`$ times smaller than the $`277`$ MB rank-128
adapter.

Khmer quality. LoRA gives a large gain that grows with rank and then
peaks. Overall MOS rises from $`3.847`$ for the zero-shot base to
$`4.227`$ at rank 64, a gain of 0.38 points that is highly significant
with $`p<0.001`$. Ranks 32 and 128 are also significant, both with
$`p=0.001`$, while ranks 8 and 16 are not, with $`p=0.19`$ and
$`p=0.07`$ respectively. The gain shows up on all three axes, and the
biggest single jump is in prosody, which rises from $`3.76`$ to
$`4.36`$, the strongest effect in the study. This suggests the base
model’s main Khmer weakness was rhythm and intonation. Quality rises up
to rank 64 and then falls to $`4.083`$ at rank 128, so capacity beyond
rank 64 hurts perceived quality even though the loss keeps dropping. In
short, the loss and human ratings agree that adaptation helps but
disagree on the best rank. Rank 64 is best for people, and even rank 8,
with an MOS of $`3.910`$, already recovers most of the gain.

Korean quality. The result is very different. No adapter significantly
improves overall Korean MOS. The best mean is $`3.757`$ at rank 32,
compared with $`3.650`$ for the base, and this difference is not
significant with $`p=0.49`$. In addition, the confidence intervals for
ranks 8 to 32 all overlap the baseline. The only significant overall
change is negative. Rank 64 scores $`3.480`$, which is significantly
below the base with $`p=0.02`$, as naturalness falls from $`3.67`$ to
$`3.23`$. The only local improvement is pronunciation, which reaches
$`4.03`$ at rank 32 while naturalness and prosody stay flat. Korean is
already well covered by the base model’s pretraining, so there is little
gap to close. At a high rank the extra capacity overfits the small
training set and damages skills the model already had. The stop-token
loss converges below $`0.05`$ for all ranks.

### IV-B Qualitative Synthesis

For each rank and for the base, we synthesize the same Khmer and Korean
sentences at 48 kHz. By ear, the adapted models pronounce Khmer
subscript consonant clusters and Korean sound boundaries more reliably
than the base, and their rhythm is steadier. This matches the lower
validation loss. These samples come with the released artifacts.

### IV-C Discussion

We draw three lessons. First, one shared adapter, trained on both
languages with language tags and Khmer upsampling, adapts VoxCPM2 to two
very different scripts at once, with no per-language model. Because it
starts at zero, it begins at the zero-shot base and lowers the
validation loss for every rank. Second, the loss is not a perfect guide
to perceived quality. The loss is lowest at rank 128, but Khmer MOS is
highest at rank 64, reaching $`4.227`$, and drops at rank 128. So rank
64 is best for listeners, and the tiny rank-8 adapter already recovers
much of the gain with an MOS of $`3.910`$, which is handy for deployment
and for swapping adapters at run time. Third, and most important, the
benefit depends on the language. The same adapter gives a large,
significant gain for Khmer, an improvement of 0.38 points with
$`p<0.001`$, but no significant gain for Korean, where a high rank even
hurts. The simplest explanation is that the useful adapter size grows
with the gap between the language and what the base model already knows.
When the gap is large, as for Khmer, the model can use more capacity, up
to rank 64. When the gap is small, as for Korean, extra capacity has
little to add and a high rank overfits. So adaptation should target
languages where the base model is truly weak, and a single global rank
does not fit all languages.

### IV-D Limitations

Our tests have some limits. We now report MOS for both Khmer and Korean
in Tables III and IV, but a full fine-tuning upper bound is left for
future work. We also have not yet isolated the value of sharing. To show
that one joint adapter beats separate per-language adapters, we would
need a comparison at the same parameter budget, which we leave for
future work. The ratings are also noisy. Inter-rater agreement is low,
with Krippendorff’s $`\alpha`$ of $`0.31`$ for Khmer and $`0.26`$ for
Korean, so the Korean null result is especially uncertain. Each panel
has only five raters and 20 sentences per system, and the two languages
used different panels, so only within-language trends are valid, not
absolute cross-language scores. The corpus is small. It has $`25.5`$ h
after cleaning, of which only $`22.95`$ h of unique audio reach
training, and the KSS Korean data comes from a single speaker. In
addition, the Khmer gains come partly from upsampling, not from more
unique data, since the Khmer training set spans just $`2.96`$ h of
unique speech. Finally, the two languages differ not only in pretraining
coverage but also in fine-tuning data source. Korean uses open public
corpora, namely KSS and Common Voice/FLEURS, while Khmer uses a private
in-house corpus. The two sets may differ in recording quality, speaker
variety, and amount. So the Khmer-vs-Korean difference mixes two things,
namely how far each language is from what the base model knows, and the
fine-tuning data itself. We do not fully separate these. This is another
reason our claims hold within a language, not as a controlled comparison
across languages.

## V Conclusion

We studied the quality gap that large pretrained TTS models show for
low-resource languages, using Khmer and Korean with VoxCPM2. We built
one language-tagged corpus from several sources and trained a single
shared LoRA adapter on both languages, adding low-rank updates to both
the language model and the decoder. This lowered the validation loss
from about $`0.83`$ for the zero-shot base to as low as $`0.709`$. It
also raised Khmer MOS from $`3.85`$ to $`4.23`$, an improvement of 0.38
points with $`p<0.001`$, while training only $`0.19`$ to $`3.03`$
percent of the parameters. The rank sweep shows that the loss is lowest
at rank 128, but Khmer MOS is highest at rank 64, and even rank 8
recovers much of the gain. For Korean, which the base model already
covers well, no adapter improves overall quality and a high rank makes
it worse. This difference between languages shows that small LoRA
adapters help most where the base model is truly weak. Future work will
add a full fine-tuning comparison, use larger rater panels, and cover
more languages.
