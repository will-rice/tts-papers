# Beyond Token Revision: Investigating Mask-and-Replace Diffusion for Zero-Shot Text-to-Speech

Hounsu Kim    Joonyong Park    Yuki Saito    Satoru Fukayama    Juhan
Nam ^(†)^(†)thanks: ^(†)Co-corresponding authors.

###### Abstract

Unlike autoregressive models, discrete diffusion–based models for
zero-shot text-to-speech generate speech tokens in parallel and can
revisit earlier predictions. Mask-and-replace training extends mask-only
training by randomly replacing some tokens, and its gains are commonly
attributed to self-correction, the ability to revise previously
generated tokens. However, exposure to randomly perturbed context during
training may itself improve generation, raising the question of whether
these gains require inference-time token revision. To investigate this
question, we use DeMaR, which combines mask-and-replace training with
confidence-ranked mask-only sampling while preserving the total training
corruption probability. Trained from scratch on LibriTTS, DeMaR achieves
lower word error rates (WER) than autoregressive and mask-only diffusion
baselines using the same speech tokenizer. This advantage persists when
each token remains unchanged after first being unmasked. Matched
training conditions on two heterogeneous speech tokenizers show that
both noisy-context augmentation and replacement supervision improve WER
under this restriction. These findings demonstrate training-side
benefits of replacement beyond enabling inference-time token revision.

zero-shot text-to-speech, discrete diffusion, speech language models,

## 1 Introduction

Zero-shot text-to-speech (TTS) generates speech from text using
reference speech to specify the speaker’s voice, without
speaker-specific fine-tuning \[5, 21\]. Speech language models
(SpeechLMs) perform this task by generating discrete speech tokens that
a waveform decoder converts to audio \[4\]. Autoregressive language
models (AR-LMs) generate tokens sequentially, whereas _discrete
diffusion language models_ (DLMs) recover clean speech-token sequences
from corrupted inputs through repeated parallel predictions \[17\]. With
linguistic content supplied by the input text, this non-autoregressive
generation offers a way to reduce reliance on preceding token
predictions and potentially limit error accumulation \[14\].

In the forward process, mask-only training masks some target tokens,
whereas mask-and-replace training also replaces some with randomly
sampled alternatives \[8, 19\]. Both approaches train the model to
reconstruct the original sequence. Reconstruction loss at replaced
positions (_replacement supervision_) trains the model to recover the
original tokens from randomly substituted ones, potentially supporting
self-correction at inference. Beyond serving as reconstruction targets,
these replaced positions also provide noisy context for predicting
masked tokens. We hypothesize that this _noisy-context augmentation_ can
improve generation even without replacement supervision or
inference-time revision.

To test this hypothesis, we use _DeMaR_ (_decoupled mask-and-replace_),
which combines mask-and-replace training with confidence-ranked
mask-only sampling for conditional speech-token generation. Following
D3RM \[11\], DeMaR uses replacement only during training. At inference,
masks alone carry the combined training probability of masking and
replacement. This mask-only sampling procedure allows a _commit-only_
variant in which each token remains unchanged once it is first unmasked.
Keeping the trained model and masking schedule fixed, we use this
variant to test whether the gains persist without inference-time
revision.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

- $`\bullet`$
  Benefits beyond inference-time revision. DeMaR retains its WER
  advantage over mask-only DLMs (Mo-DLMs) under commit-only sampling. We
  also distinguish the effects of direct token revision from those of
  confidence-based remasking.
- $`\bullet`$
  Training-side contributions. Matched training conditions show that
  noisy-context augmentation improves WER without replacement
  supervision or inference-time revision, while replacement supervision
  provides further gains under commit-only sampling.

Our comparison covers two heterogeneous tokenizers: CosyVoice3’s
fixed-rate tokens \[6\] and FlexiCodec’s variable-rate content and span
sequences \[12\].

## 2 Related Work

Inference-time token revision. Standard masked decoders keep a token
once it is unmasked: MDLM copies visible tokens unchanged \[17\], and
confidence-ranked generators such as MaskGCT, MaskGIT, and SoundStorm
never remask them \[21, 2, 1\]. Token-to-mask (T2M) remasking returns a
visible token to mask; ReMDM and _Mask, Sample, Revise_ apply it without
retraining \[20, 7\], while PALLE uses it in a separate refinement
stage \[24\]. Token-to-token (T2T) overwriting changes a visible token
directly; GIDD and SCOPE pair this operation with training on incorrect
visible tokens \[19, 26\]. _Remask, Don’t Replace_ compares the two and
finds T2M more effective, mainly for correction-trained models \[25\].

Mask-and-replace training. Mask-and-replace training exposes the
denoiser to incorrect visible tokens, both as reconstruction targets and
as context for predicting masked tokens. VQ-Diffusion and Diffsound
adopt it partly to avoid accumulating errors, and InstructTTS applies it
to TTS \[8, 23, 22\]. GIDD applies it to text and suggests that
self-correction can occur during denoising \[19\], CDLM explicitly
supervises corrupted visible tokens to support targeted
refinement \[28\], and SCOPE instead trains on self-generated
predictions matched to its sampler \[26\].

Decoupling training and sampling corruption. D3RM trains with masks and
random replacements but samples with masks only for piano
transcription \[11\]. It assigns the combined corruption probability to
masks. This improves accuracy over mask-and-replace sampling, and under
this sampling, mask-and-replace training still outperforms mask-only
training. DeMaR adapts this training–sampling separation to speech
tokens, using confidence-ranked masking rather than D3RM’s posterior
sampler. We test whether DeMaR’s gains persist without inference-time
revision and whether noisy-context augmentation and replacement
supervision each contribute.

## 3 DeMaR and Matched Comparisons

We formulate zero-shot TTS as text-conditioned speech-token
continuation, following the prefix-conditioning setups of MaskGCT and
VALL-E \[21, 4\]. The conditioning sequence $`c`$ consists of the
concatenated prompt and target text tokens, followed by the uncorrupted
prompt speech tokens. Let
$`\mathbf{x}_{0}=(x_{0,1},\ldots,x_{0,L})\in\mathcal{V}^{L}`$ be a clean
target sequence of $`L`$ speech tokens from vocabulary $`\mathcal{V}`$.
Given a corrupted sequence $`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$, conditioning $`c`$, and
diffusion time $`t\in[0,1]`$, the denoiser with parameters $`\theta`$
predicts a categorical distribution at each target position, assigning
probability $`p_{\theta}(x_{0,i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},c,t)`$ to the
ground-truth token.

### 3.1 Training corruption and objective

At a shared diffusion time $`t`$, each target token $`x_{0}`$ is
independently corrupted according to

```math
q(x_{t}\mid x_{0})=a_{t}\delta_{x_{0}}+m_{t}\delta_{\textsc{mask}}+r_{t}\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{V}), \tag{1}
```

where $`\delta_{x}`$ is a point mass, $`\textsc{mask}\notin\mathcal{V}`$
is the mask symbol, and $`\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{V})`$ samples each
vocabulary symbol with probability $`1/|\mathcal{V}|`$, including the
original token. Here $`a_{t}`$, $`m_{t}`$, and $`r_{t}`$ are the
probabilities of retaining, masking, and randomly replacing a token,
respectively. Following GIDD \[19\], we set
$`h(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{t(1-t)}`$ and use

```math
(a_{t},m_{t},r_{t})=\frac{(1-t,t,h(t))}{1+h(t)}. \tag{2}
```

These probabilities sum to one, with clean inputs at $`t=0`$ and fully
masked inputs at $`t=1`$. The matched Mo-DLM baseline masks tokens with
probability $`\rho_{t}=m_{t}+r_{t}`$ and retains them otherwise. The two
models therefore select positions for corruption with the same
probability but apply different corruption operations.

For each training example, we uniformly sample one of $`T`$ diffusion
timesteps evenly spaced in $`[0,1]`$ and corrupt the target input at
that time. DeMaR and Mo-DLM reconstruct the original tokens using the
same weighted cross-entropy over target positions
$`\mathcal{I}=\{1,\ldots,L\}`$:

```math
\mathcal{L}=-\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}w_{i}\log p_{\theta}(x_{0,i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},c,t)}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}w_{i}}. \tag{3}
```

The loss weight $`w_{i}`$ is 1 at positions selected for masking or
replacement and 0.1 otherwise.

For CosyVoice3, this formulation applies to a single sequence of
fixed-rate speech tokens. FlexiCodec instead uses paired sequences of
content and span tokens, with each span indicating the number of
consecutive pre-merge tokens combined into the corresponding content
token. The two streams are corrupted independently at the same sampled
diffusion time. We apply Eq. 3 to each stream and sum the normalized
losses.

### 3.2 Confidence-based sampling

DeMaR and Mo-DLM share a confidence-based token selection and masking
procedure. For Mo-DLM, the sampling mask budget follows its mask-only
training probability $`\rho_{t}`$. DeMaR instead trains with both
masking and replacement but allocates their combined probability
$`\rho_{t}=m_{t}+r_{t}`$ entirely to masking during sampling. We call
this change of corruption process at inference _decoupled sampling
(DS)_.

Generation starts from a fully masked target sequence of length $`L`$.
Let $`N`$ be the number of denoiser calls, i.e., function evaluations
(NFE), indexed by $`j=0,\ldots,N-1`$. At call $`j`$, we use training
timestep $`s_{j}`$ and its corresponding diffusion time $`t_{j}`$:

```math
t_{j}=\frac{s_{j}-1}{T-1},\qquad s_{j}=\left\lceil\frac{T(N-j)}{N}\right\rceil. \tag{4}
```

At denoiser call $`j`$, we select a token proposal $`\hat{x}_{0,i}`$ at
position $`i`$ from the sampling distribution
$`\pi_{j,i}\in[0,1]^{|\mathcal{V}|}`$, where
$`\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{j,i}(v)=1`$. We assign confidence
$`u_{j,i}\in\mathbb{R}`$ using either the proposal’s probability under
$`\pi_{j,i}`$ or the negative entropy of $`\pi_{j,i}`$ \[13\]. For both
models, the number of masked positions in the next input follows the
corruption probability $`\rho_{t_{j+1}}`$:

```math
k_{j}=\operatorname{round}\!\left[L\rho_{t_{j+1}}\right],\qquad j=0,\ldots,N-2. \tag{5}
```

For the final call, we set $`k_{N-1}=0`$. Under the default revisiting
policy, the denoiser proposes tokens at every target position. We select
the $`k_{j}`$ lowest-confidence positions as
$`R_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{I}`$. For $`j<N-1`$, the next input is
constructed from the predicted clean sequence by

```math
x_{t_{j+1},i}=\begin{cases}\textsc{mask},&i\in R_{j},\\
\hat{x}_{0,i},&i\notin R_{j}.\end{cases} \tag{6}
```

This revisiting policy permits two operations (Fig. 1(c)). A
_token-to-token (T2T) revision_ changes a previously visible token to a
different vocabulary token that remains unmasked in the next state.
*Token-to-mask (T2M) demotion* \[25\] remasks a visible position
selected by the low-confidence ranking. When that position is later
unmasked, its token may be the same as or different from the token
before masking. Under revisiting, the schedule determines how many
positions are masked, while confidence rankings determine which
positions are masked at each call.

### 3.3 Controlling inference-time revision

To test whether DeMaR’s advantage over Mo-DLM depends on inference-time
revision, we replace revisiting with _commit-only_ sampling (Fig. 1(c)).
Both policies run the denoiser on the full sequence, but commit-only
uses new proposals and confidence scores only at currently masked target
positions, $`M_{j}=\{i\in\mathcal{I}:x_{t_{j},i}=\textsc{mask}\}`$. At
call $`j`$, it fills the $`|M_{j}|-k_{j}`$ highest-confidence positions
in $`M_{j}`$ with their proposed tokens and leaves the remaining
$`k_{j}`$ positions masked. Commit-only therefore disables both T2T
revision and T2M demotion.

To test whether either revision pathway improves generation beyond
commit-only sampling, we evaluate them separately. _T2T-only_ allows
visible tokens to change directly but selects masks only within
$`M_{j}`$. _T2M-only_ selects masks across all target positions but
preserves the current values of visible tokens not selected for masking.
All policies use the same mask count $`k_{j}`$.

### 3.4 Separating augmentation from replacement supervision

To separate noisy-context augmentation from direct replacement
supervision, we compare three training conditions. Let
$`\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{I}`$ and
$`\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{M}`$ denote the
masking and replacement-candidate positions sampled under Eq. 1. The
remaining positions are
$`\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{I}\setminus(\mathcal{M}\cup\mathcal{R})`$. In _no
replacement_, tokens at $`\mathcal{R}`$ remain unchanged and receive
zero loss weight. _Replacement, no loss_ replaces tokens at
$`\mathcal{R}`$ with uniformly sampled tokens but assigns no loss to
these positions. _Replacement + loss_ (DeMaR) uses the same corrupted
inputs and adds unit-weight reconstruction loss at $`\mathcal{R}`$.

All three conditions use loss weights 1 on $`\mathcal{M}`$ and 0.1 on
$`\mathcal{O}`$, replacing the denominator in Eq. 3 with

```math
Z=|\mathcal{M}|+|\mathcal{R}|+0.1|\mathcal{O}|. \tag{7}
```

Including $`|\mathcal{R}|`$ keeps the denominator unchanged when
replacement supervision is added. Unlike the no-replacement condition,
Mo-DLM masks both $`\mathcal{M}`$ and $`\mathcal{R}`$ and supervises
them with unit weight.

All three conditions use the same sampling mask budget, determined by
$`\rho_{t}=m_{t}+r_{t}`$, as in Table 5. Comparing them under revisiting
and commit-only sampling tests whether the benefits of augmentation and
replacement supervision require inference-time revision.

## 4 Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Data and model training

We train speech-token generators from scratch on 585 hours of
LibriTTS \[27\] using the frozen tokenizers and waveform decoders of
CosyVoice3 \[6\] and FlexiCodec \[12\]. All models share a backbone with
12 layers, a hidden dimension of 768, and 12 attention heads, totaling
84.95M parameters excluding vocabulary-dependent embeddings. They share
text preprocessing and tokenization and are trained with AdamW at a
learning rate of $`4\times 10^{-4}`$ on two NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs per
run.

AR-LMs use causal attention and teacher forcing to predict speech tokens
and EOS, whereas DLMs use bidirectional attention to reconstruct
corrupted tokens; both condition on the full transcript. During DLM
training, we corrupt tokens after a clean prefix covering 10–40% of the
sequence, or the entire sequence with probability 0.15. We select DLM
checkpoints by dev-clean WER using oracle target-token counts. Each
condition in Section 3.4 is trained with three seeds; the other tables
report seed 1.

### 4.2 Decoding hyperparameter tuning

For the system comparison, we tune each model’s decoding hyperparameters
by dev-clean WER using Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization with
expected improvement (GP-EI) \[18\]. We search on 1,000 dev-clean
utterances and rerank the top four candidates on 3,000 separate
dev-clean utterances. We search temperatures 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6; top-$`p`$
0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 1.0; and repetition penalties \[10\] from 1.0 to 1.6 in
steps of 0.1. For AR-LMs, we also consider repetition-aware sampling
(RAS), either disabled or using (window, threshold) pairs $`(5,0.2)`$,
$`(20,0.1)`$, or $`(20,0.2)`$. We stop after three consecutive GP checks
in which every unevaluated candidate has an expected WER reduction below
0.02 percentage points and less than a 10% probability of improving the
best observed WER by at least 0.1 percentage points.

DLMs use 32 denoiser calls with constant temperature; we compare the two
confidence rules in Section 3.2. Repetition penalty, temperature, and
top-$`p`$ filtering modify $`\pi_{j,i}`$, the distribution used for both
token proposals and confidence scores. In Table 5, commit-only uses the
same checkpoint and decoding settings as revisiting. For each tokenizer
and seed in Table 2, all three training conditions use the
no-replacement model’s selected decoding settings.

### 4.3 Evaluation protocol and metrics

We evaluate all 4,835 LibriTTS test-clean utterances. Within each
tokenizer, all systems share the evaluation pairs, text conditioning,
full reference speech-token prefix, and frozen waveform decoder. AR-LMs
terminate at EOS, whereas DLMs use target-token counts predicted by
OmniVoice’s prompt-based length rule \[15\]. We measure intelligibility
using utterance-averaged WER between HuBERT-Large CTC \[9\] transcripts
of generated speech and normalized written references.
UTMOS22-strong \[16\] estimates naturalness, while WavLM-Large \[3\]
measures speaker similarity to the reference speech (SIM).

DeMaR outperforms both AR-LM and Mo-DLM in WER (Table 5), reducing
Mo-DLM’s WER from 6.05 to 4.95% for CosyVoice3 and from 5.39 to 4.89%
for FlexiCodec. Both DLMs also outperform AR-LM in UTMOS and SIM. For
CosyVoice3, DeMaR raises UTMOS from 4.127 to 4.276 and SIM from 0.701 to
0.717 relative to AR-LM. Differences between the two DLMs are more
pronounced in WER than in UTMOS or SIM; for FlexiCodec, both achieve
approximately 3.88 UTMOS and 0.694 SIM. We next examine whether DeMaR’s
WER gains require inference-time revision and how noisy-context
augmentation and replacement supervision contribute.

### 5.1 Contribution of inference-time revision

Switching from revisiting to commit-only raises DeMaR’s WER by only 0.29
percentage points for CosyVoice3 and 0.14 for FlexiCodec (Table 5).
Under commit-only sampling, DeMaR still outperforms Mo-DLM: 5.23 versus
5.97% for CosyVoice3 and 5.03 versus 5.38% for FlexiCodec. Mo-DLM’s WER
decreases slightly under the same switch, from 6.05 to 5.97% and from
5.39 to 5.38%, respectively. Thus, DeMaR’s advantage does not require
inference-time revision, motivating the following analysis of its
training components.

### 5.2 Noisy-context augmentation and replacement supervision

Table 2 focuses on WER, as UTMOS and SIM vary by at most 0.08 and 0.006,
respectively, within each tokenizer and seed. Unlike Mo-DLM, the
no-replacement model leaves replacement-candidate positions unchanged
and unsupervised during training (Section 3.4). Under commit-only
sampling, adding random replacements without supervising them reduces
seed-1 WER from 6.87 to 5.73% for CosyVoice3 and from 5.61 to 5.27% for
FlexiCodec.

Adding replacement supervision further lowers seed-1 commit-only WER to
5.07% for CosyVoice3 and 5.03% for FlexiCodec. The two
replacement-trained models share the same training-input distribution,
so this comparison measures the effect of supervising replaced
positions. This comparison does not separate the benefit of
reconstructing replaced tokens from that of increasing the number of
supervised positions. Augmentation lowers WER in all three seeds for
both tokenizers under both sampling policies. With revisiting, mean WER
decreases from $`6.91\pm 0.24`$ to $`5.61\pm 0.58`$% for CosyVoice3 and
from $`5.75\pm 0.31`$ to $`5.50\pm 0.31`$% for FlexiCodec. Replacement
supervision further reduces the means to $`5.02\pm 0.41`$% and
$`4.98\pm 0.30`$%, respectively, improving WER in two of three
CosyVoice3 seeds and all three FlexiCodec seeds under both sampling
policies. The exception is CosyVoice3 seed 2, where WER increases by
0.06 percentage points with revisiting and 0.24 with commit-only. Thus,
augmentation consistently helps across the tested seeds, while
replacement supervision provides additional but less uniform gains.

We next examine whether learning to reconstruct replaced tokens
translates into additional gains from T2T revision or T2M demotion at
inference (Table 5.2, CosyVoice3). Relative to commit-only, T2T-only
raises WER from 6.87 to 7.20% without replacement and from 5.72 to 6.27%
with replacement but no loss. With replacement supervision, this penalty
disappears: T2T-only yields 5.02% WER versus 5.07% for commit-only.
T2M-only differs from commit-only by at most 0.04 percentage points for
all three models. With replacement supervision, WER spans only
5.02–5.08% across all four policies, with neither revision pathway
substantially improving on commit-only sampling. Together with the
commit-only improvements in Table 2, this suggests that learning to
reconstruct replaced tokens benefits generation beyond performing token
revision at inference.

## 6 Conclusion

We tested whether the gains from mask-and-replace training depend on
inference-time token revision. When each token remains unchanged after
first being unmasked, DeMaR retains its WER advantage over mask-only
diffusion, with WER increasing by only 0.29 percentage points for
CosyVoice3 and 0.14 for FlexiCodec. Noisy-context augmentation improves
WER without replacement supervision in all three seeds for both
tokenizers, under both revisiting and commit-only sampling. Replacement
supervision provides further gains in two of three CosyVoice3 seeds and
all three FlexiCodec seeds under both policies. These findings show that
noisy-context augmentation and replacement supervision can improve
generation even when generated tokens are never revised.
