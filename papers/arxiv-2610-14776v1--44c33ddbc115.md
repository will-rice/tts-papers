# Training a Compact, Strong, and Realistic Text-to-Speech Model

Runyuan Cai    Yu Lin    Yiming Wang    Xiaodong Zeng
Affiliation: AutoArk

October 10, 2026

###### Abstract

Text-to-speech (TTS) systems are conventionally optimized for
intelligibility and speaker similarity. However, neither objective
captures the prosodic detail that distinguishes human performances from
synthetic speech, leaving a realism gap that larger models and
higher-quality data can narrow but not close. In the absence of an
objective that directly targets this gap, realism remains a persistent
challenge in TTS.

We present Audio8-TTS, a compact 0.6B-parameter model for zero-shot
voice cloning that achieves state-of-the-art performance in
intelligibility and speaker similarity, with particularly strong results
in perceptual naturalness. To align the training signal with human
perception rather than signal-level fidelity, we construct a quality
ladder from widely used open-source TTS systems and train a realism
critic to assess generated speech against matched human recordings of
the same sentences. An identity-erasure objective discourages the critic
from relying on generator fingerprints. We train the model in three
stages: multilingual supervised fine-tuning; group relative policy
optimization (GRPO) with word error rate (WER) and speaker similarity
(SIM) rewards; and GRPO guided by the realism critic. The final stage
improves perceived prosodic naturalness and speaker similarity while
preserving WER performance.

## 1 Introduction

Autoregressive language models over discrete audio tokens have become a
leading paradigm for speech generation. Neural audio codecs encode
waveforms as compact sequences of discrete codes \[36, 8\], enabling
language models trained on these codes to synthesize speech at a quality
once attainable only with dedicated acoustic pipelines \[1\]. This
representation also supports unified modeling of recognition, synthesis,
and conversion within a single model \[5\], and subsequent work has
extended this line of research to decoupled continuous representations
\[31\]. These advances show that compact speech models can perform
demanding audio tasks when trained with suitable objectives.
Accordingly, directly optimizing for perceptual realism applies this
principle to a quality axis absent from conventional fidelity
objectives, enabling compact models to better exploit their existing
capacity. How to design a realism oriented training phase remains a key
challenge.

TTS systems are commonly evaluated and optimized for two properties:
lexical intelligibility, measured by word error rate (WER), and speaker
similarity (SIM), which measures how closely the synthesized voice
matches a reference. These metrics may appear to cover the main
requirements of TTS, but each is a narrow proxy. WER measures whether
the intended words are recognized, not how naturally they are delivered.
SIM measures speaker identity in a compact representation, leaving much
of the utterance’s expressive realization unconstrained. Prosodic
details such as delivery style, rhythm, and timing therefore remain
largely outside the score. A model can achieve low WER and high SIM
while still sounding synthetic. Optimizing only these metrics is like
judging athletic ability by bodybuilding standards: it rewards
muscularity and definition, but not necessarily athletic performance.
More data and greater model capacity may narrow this realism gap, but
neither directly optimizes perceptual naturalness. Reinforcement
learning, often used to enhance model expressiveness, inherits the same
limitation when guided only by WER and SIM: it can improve measured
fidelity without necessarily making speech sound more natural.

To address this gap, we present Audio8-TTS, a compact 0.6B-parameter
model for zero-shot voice cloning. Its decoder follows the fast–slow
dual-autoregressive formulation used in recent codec language models
\[32, 20\]. The slow branch uses Qwen2.5 0.5B as its backbone, with its
vocabulary extended to include audio tokens. A shallow fast branch
predicts the remaining codebooks. Together with our training framework,
this architecture enables Audio8-TTS to achieve state-of-the-art
performance in intelligibility, speaker similarity, and perceptual
naturalness, despite having far fewer parameters than substantially
larger models.

Training proceeds in three stages: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on
multilingual speech; group relative policy optimization (GRPO) \[30\]
with WER and SIM rewards to stabilize content and speaker identity; and
a final GRPO stage guided by extra realism critic to enhance perceptual
naturalness. We train the critic on a quality ladder of outputs from
widely used open-source TTS systems, ranked through a private blind
listening study, with matched human recordings as the top rung. Unlike
public arenas, where each system speaks in its own voice, our study
holds speaker identity and sentence content constant: every system
clones the same reference voice and reads the same target sentence. The
critic scores each TTS candidate against the matched human recording of
that sentence. System-specific fingerprints can reveal which generator
produced a sample without revealing how natural it sounds. We therefore
introduce an identity-erasure strategy to keep the critic’s ratings
focused on prosodic realism.

Another challenge is constraining the output duration within a plausible
range. Since progressive pause compression is part of our ladder
construction strategy, candidates with less pronounced pauses is
designed to receive lower realism scores. So optimization can favor
longer outputs regardless of the naturalness of speaking rate, providing
another potential shorcut. Since the WER and SIM are largely insensitive
to duration, an explicit duration constraint is further introduced as
the solution.

In this technical report, we introduce a training framework for compact
zero-shot voice cloning. The framework uses a learned realism critic as
a reward model, complementing WER and SIM rewards with a signal for
perceptual naturalness. Applied to Audio8-TTS, a 0.6B-parameter model,
this framework achieves state-of-the-art results in intelligibility,
speaker similarity, and perceptual naturalness.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Neural Text-to-Speech and Discrete Audio Tokens

Early end-to-end neural text-to-speech systems mapped text to
intermediate acoustic features \[34\]; later systems generated waveforms
directly \[16\]. Neural audio codecs introduced another way to represent
speech, encoding waveforms as short sequences of discrete codes \[36,
8\]. Self-supervised speech models also provide discrete units for
autoregressive modeling \[14, 17\]. Representing speech as a token
sequence makes synthesis a language-modeling task, as in recent
zero-shot systems \[1\]. The same approach also allows a single
autoregressive model to handle recognition, synthesis, and conversion
through instruction-driven task induction. We adopt this discrete-token
approach and focus on a compact model for synthesis. Its main design
challenges are efficiently decoding codebook residuals and maintaining
perceptual audio quality.

### 2.2 Preference Optimization for Generative Models

Reinforcement learning from human feedback uses preference signals to
align generative models with human judgments \[7, 26\]. Proximal policy
optimization (PPO) \[29\] is widely used for this purpose, but requires
an auxiliary value model. Group relative policy optimization (GRPO)
avoids that requirement by normalizing rewards across a group of
responses to the same prompt \[30\]; it is now commonly used to
post-train language models. Applying these methods to speech adds a
practical challenge: each rollout must first be synthesized and then
scored. The reward must also reliably distinguish samples that differ
mainly in prosody.

### 2.3 Reward Models and Listening Tests for Speech

Objective quality predictors estimate an absolute mean opinion score
(MOS) from a single utterance \[11\]. They can rank degraded samples,
but their training domains are narrow, and they do not directly compare
a candidate with a human performance. Listening arenas compare systems
head-to-head, but each system speaks in its own voice, even when the
text is held fixed. Their rankings can therefore reflect speaker
preference as well as synthesis quality. Our reward model instead scores
each candidate against a matched human recording of the same sentence,
holding content and speaker identity constant. It combines a frozen
self-supervised frontend \[6\] with explicit prosodic features, rather
than predicting an absolute quality score.

## 3 A Compact Dual-Autoregressive Model

Our decoder adopts the fast–slow dual-autoregressive design used in
recent codec language models \[32, 20\]. Figure Figure 1 shows the full
pipeline. The code, model weights, and implementation and deployment
details are released in the
[Audio8-TTS](https://github.com/Edge0-AI/Audio8_TTS) repository \[2\].
We describe the two branches and their computational cost below.

Figure 1: Overview of Audio8-TTS. The slow autoregressive branch
predicts text tokens and the first audio codebook at the frame rate,
while a shallow fast branch predicts the remaining nine codebooks in
parallel. The audio decoder remains frozen.

### 3.1 Discrete Audio Tokens

A fixed audio tokenizer maps each waveform to $`T`$ frames at
$`21.5\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}`$. Each frame comprises ten discrete codes,
one drawn from each of ten codebooks with size $`4096`$. Speech
synthesis is therefore reduced to generating a $`10\times T`$ matrix of
discrete tokens, after which a frozen audio decoder reconstructs the
waveform. Both the tokenizer and the decoder remain fixed throughout
language-model training.

### 3.2 Slow and Fast Branches

Generation is factorized along the frame and codebook axes. The slow
branch operates autoregressively at the frame rate, predicting the text
tokens together with the first audio codebook. It is based on a Qwen2.5
0.5B language model \[27\] augmented with audio-token embeddings, with a
hidden size of 896 and 24 layers. Conditioned on the hidden state
produced by this branch, a shallow fast branch with four layers predicts
the remaining nine codebooks of each frame in parallel. Because the slow
branch operates only at $`21.5\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}`$, the autoregressive
sequence remains short, while codebook-level detail is handled by a
branch roughly an order of magnitude shallower.

### 3.3 Parameter Budget and Inference

The autoregressive decoder has 601M parameters, excluding the frozen
codec. The slow branch has 24 layers, and the fast branch has 4 layers;
both have a width of 896. They use grouped-query attention with 14 query
heads and two key–value heads. The decoder also includes ten codebook
embedding tables, each with 4096 entries.

At inference, each frame passes through the slow branch once and the
fast branch once. The fast branch predicts nine codebooks in parallel,
adding no autoregressive steps. Thus, sequential computation occurs only
at the frame level.

For CPU inference, the released ONNX implementation uses the same
schedule and applies weight-only INT4 quantization to both
autoregressive branches. Activations, the KV cache, and the codec remain
in FP16. In the reported Apple M2 configuration, memory use is
approximately 1.0 GiB after loading and peaks at 1.1–1.2 GiB. The online
model files total about 572 MiB.

## 4 Training for Realism

Training has three stages: multilingual supervised fine-tuning, GRPO
with WER and SIM rewards, and a final GRPO stage guided by the realism
critic as well as auxiliary WER and SIM reward components.

### 4.1 Supervised Fine-tuning

For supervised fine-tuning, each example pairs reference and target
recordings from the same speaker and includes a transcript for each. The
training set combines cleaned Emilia \[13\] and YODAS2 \[18\] with a
private collection covering speakers and recording conditions
underrepresented in public datasets. We exclude low-quality recordings,
compare transcripts against automatic speech recognition (ASR) output,
and filter samples by language, duration, and ASR error rate. The target
waveform is encoded in the discrete token space defined in Sec. 3; the
reference audio and transcript use the inference-time conditioning
format.

We train both the two branches with cross-entropy: the slow branch
predicts text and the first codebook at the frame rate, while the fast
branch predicts the remaining 9 codebooks in parallel. Training begins
with the slow branch alone, proceeds to the fast branch with the slow
branch frozen, and ends with joint fine-tuning.

This SFT stage yields intelligible, speaker-consistent speech across the
languages in the training corpus. The preference-optimization stages
then preserve and enhance these strengths, while addressing the
perceptual unnaturalness that SFT leaves unresolved (see Sec. 1).

### 4.2 Learning a Reference-Based Realism Critic

After SFT, the first GRPO stage optimizes WER and SIM; a second adds a
reference-based reward for perceptual realism. We first describe the
method for constructing the critic that provides this reward.

#### Quality-tier construction.

A naive human-versus-synthetic classifier cannot rank synthetic speech
by quality. When its synthetic examples come from a single generator,
source identity is confounded with the class label: the classifier can
exploit generator-specific cues rather than learn to assess speech
quality.

To provide graded supervision, we construct a quality ladder from
outputs of six open-source systems: Fish-Speech 1.5 \[20\], IndexTTS-2
\[38\], Qwen3-TTS \[15\], Higgs Audio v3 \[4\], Fish Audio S2 \[21\],
and Spark-TTS \[33\]. We rank the six TTS systems in a private blind
listening study. In each pairwise comparison, listeners hear outputs for
the same sentence and reference voice, without being told which systems
produced them. Holding content and speaker identity fixed is essential:
otherwise, as in public arenas where systems speak in their own voices,
speaker preference can influence the quality ranking. We aggregate the
pairwise judgments into an Elo-style ranking and place the matched human
recording at the top. This ordered set forms the quality ladder used in
our experiments.

All six systems contribute to the listening study and appear on the
resulting quality ladder, but only four provide critic-training data. We
reserve Fish Audio S2\[21\] and Spark-TTS\[33\] for evaluation, testing
whether the critic can score unseen systems in a way that preserves
their listening-study ranking and places their scores within the ranges
expected for their respective ladder tiers.

#### Critic design.

The critic scores each synthesized utterance against the matched human
recording of the same sentence. It is trained to rank utterances within
sentence-specific groups, with the human recording anchoring the top of
the quality ladder; its scores do not represent absolute quality.

We pass both the synthesized utterance and its human reference through a
frozen WavLM-large encoder \[6\], extract layer 8, and pool the frames
to 25 Hz. We also provide explicit pitch, energy, and voicing
trajectories for both. After monotonic-band alignment, multiscale
residual features are mapped to a learned distance $`d`$. The critic
returns the bounded score

```math
s=-B\tanh(d/B),\qquad B=30, \tag{1}
```

where $`s\in(-30,0]`$ and larger values indicate speech closer to the
human reference. The score is not calibrated as a mean opinion score
(MOS). Since policy optimization normalizes rewards within each group,
only relative scores within a group affect the update.

Only the scoring head is trained. It has roughly 3M parameters; the
316M-parameter WavLM encoder remains frozen. This keeps scoring
inexpensive relative to generating the rollout it evaluates.

We use the same feature-extraction pipeline during training and
inference, including the WavLM layer, temporal pooling, prosodic
features, and waveform preprocessing. Because the critic’s scores depend
on this representation, we store a hash of the pipeline configuration
with the critic and verify it at load time. Scoring is disabled if the
active configuration differs.

#### Critic training.

Each training group is indexed by a sentence and a reference voice. It
contains the matched human recording $`y`$ and, for each of the $`K`$
systems used to train the critic, synthesized utterances
$`\{x_{i,\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{L}`$ of the same sentence in the same voice,
at degradation levels $`\ell=0,\ldots,L`$. The reference voice
conditions each system and fixes speaker identity across systems; the
human recording serves as the perceptual anchor for scoring. For
brevity, we write $`s(x_{i,\ell})`$ for the reference-conditioned score
$`s(x_{i,\ell},y)`$.

_Constructing the degradation levels._ We supplement the system-quality
ordering with a second comparison axis: progressively weakened internal
rhythm. Here, rhythm refers to the timing and scale of auditory pauses.
We detect them, scale their durations by a factor, and resynthesize each
utterance using Praat’s duration manipulation, based on
pitch-synchronous overlap-add \[23\]. Other features including pitch,
spectral envelope, and lexical content are preserved during this
operation. Let $`d_{j}`$ denote the original duration of detected pause
$`j`$. At level $`\ell`$, its duration is

```math
d_{j,\ell}=a_{\ell}d_{j},\qquad 1=a_{0}\geq a_{1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{L}=0.
```

Thus, the pause pattern is unchanged at level $`0`$ and all detected
pauses are removed at level $`L`$, forming a chain of rhythm
degradation. To check that the effect is not specific to Praat, we
repeat the construction with WORLD-based resynthesis \[22\]. Same score
trend indicates that the critic’s response is not specific to Praat’s
resynthesis procedure.

_Monotonicity within a system._ As pauses are progressively compressed,
the score for each system should decrease by at least a margin $`m`$:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mono}}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\max\!\left(0,\,s(x_{i,\ell+1})-s(x_{i,\ell})+m\right). \tag{2}
```

The hinge loss vanishes once the score drop reaches $`m`$. This margin
sets a minimum score gap between adjacent degradation levels, giving
pause-related changes a local scale that the cross-system ranking loss
does not provide.

_Ranking across systems._ We denote by $`\mathcal{P}`$ the set of system
pairs whose relative quality is reliably established by the listening
study. For each pair in this set, we train the critic to preserve the
observed ordering at every degradation level:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rank}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{BCE}\!\left(\sigma\!\left(\frac{s(x_{j,\ell})-s(x_{i,\ell})}{T}\right),\,p_{ij,\ell}\right), \tag{3}
```

where $`p_{ij,\ell}`$ is a soft target derived from the listening-study
quality tiers. As rhythm flattening increases, the target moves toward
$`1/2`$, allowing the score gap between systems to narrow without
reversing their order. The ranking loss preserves the listening-study
order; the monotonicity loss separately requires scores to fall as each
system’s rhythm is flattened.

_Erasure of identity._ At level $`\ell=L`$, all detected pauses have
been removed. We treat any remaining score differences across systems as
possible generator-identity leakage and penalize them directly:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{era}}=\sum_{i<j}\left|s(x_{i,L})-s(x_{j,L})\right|. \tag{4}
```

The ranking loss may still preserve some score separation at this
endpoint, as specified by $`p_{ij,L}`$, while
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{era}}`$ pulls the scores together. At level
$`\ell=0`$, the ranking target preserves the listening-study order. In
practice, the score spread among fully flattened variants is a small
fraction of that among the original outputs.

The full objective is

```math
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rank}}+\lambda_{m}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mono}}+\lambda_{e}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{era}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{nuis}},
```

where $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{nuis}}`$ penalizes scores predictable from
duration or energy.

#### Validation.

We evaluate the critic on 5,600 held-out sentence–voice groups. Each
group contains one utterance from each of the four training systems at
each of four degradation levels, for a total of 89,600 synthesized
utterances, in addition to the matched human references.

_Systems used for training._ On these held-out groups, the score
distributions follow the quality tiers established by the listening
study: the human references occupy the top of the range, followed by the
four systems in ladder order. The critic thus recovers the
listening-study ranking on sentence–voice groups excluded from training.

_Systems excluded from training._ Fish Audio S2 and Spark-TTS are not
used to train the critic. Their score distributions nevertheless align
with the tiers assigned to them in the listening study, rather than
collapsing into the tiers of the training systems. The critic therefore
preserves the study’s quality distinctions for generators it has not
seen during training.

The scoring path is deterministic and performs no resynthesis, so
repeated scoring of the same utterance returns the same value. This
consistency is required during policy optimization, which scores every
rollout.

### 4.3 Optimizing the Policy with GRPO

The final two training stages use group relative policy optimization
(GRPO) \[30\]. For each prompt, we sample a group of $`N`$ rollouts from
the current policy, decode them to audio, and score them. The
stage-specific reward axes are normalized and combined into a scalar
$`q_{i}`$, as described below. GRPO then centers and scales this scalar
within the group:

```math
A_{i}=\frac{q_{i}-\bar{q}}{\sigma_{q}+\epsilon},\qquad\bar{q}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{j},\qquad\epsilon=10^{-6}, \tag{5}
```

where $`\sigma_{q}`$ is the standard deviation of the combined scores.
The additive $`\epsilon`$ stabilizes the normalization when the scores
have little variation.

We then subtract the duration cost to obtain the coefficient used in the
policy update:

```math
\tilde{A}_{i}=A_{i}-\lambda C_{i}, \tag{6}
```

where $`C_{i}`$ is the normalized duration cost defined in Sec. 4.4. The
policy update uses $`\tilde{A}_{i}`$ to weight the log-probabilities of
rollout $`i`$. We recompute these log-probabilities with a forward pass
over the sampled sequence rather than reading cached generation-time
values.

The scoring models remain frozen, and no gradient is propagated through
them. During training, scoring runs in a separate, persistent service
that transcribes and embeds decoded audio while the policy samples and
updates.

_Fidelity stage._ The first GRPO stage optimizes lexical accuracy and
speaker similarity. We compute WER with Whisper-large-v3 \[28\] for
non-Chinese input and an in-house recognizer for Chinese \[10\]. We map
WER to reward with a clipped linear function, so lower WER yields higher
reward:

```math
r_{i}^{\mathrm{ASR}}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{WER}_{i}}{2.0},\,0,\,1\right), \tag{7}
```

where WER is represented as a decimal fraction (e.g., $`2\%=0.02`$). We
compute SIM as the cosine similarity between candidate and reference
embeddings from a WavLM-based speaker encoder \[6\], then rescale it to
$`[0,1]`$:

```math
r_{i}^{\mathrm{SIM}}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{SIM}_{i}}{0.85},\,0,\,1\right). \tag{8}
```

The fidelity score combines the two rewards before group-level
standardization:

```math
s_{i}^{\mathrm{fid}}=0.7\,r_{i}^{\mathrm{ASR}}+0.3\,r_{i}^{\mathrm{SIM}}. \tag{9}
```

_Realism stage._ The final GRPO stage combines the fidelity reward with
the realism critic’s score. For rollout $`i`$ in group $`g`$, let
$`s_{i}^{\mathrm{fid}}`$ denote its fidelity score and
$`s_{i}^{\mathrm{pros}}`$ its critic score. We center each score by its
mean across the group and scale it by the larger of the group standard
deviation and an axis-specific minimum:

```math
\displaystyle z_{i}^{\mathrm{fid}} \displaystyle=\frac{s_{i}^{\mathrm{fid}}-\bar{s}_{g}^{\mathrm{fid}}}{\max(\sigma_{g}^{\mathrm{fid}},\,0.005)}, \tag{10}
```

```math
\displaystyle z_{i}^{\mathrm{pros}} \displaystyle=\frac{s_{i}^{\mathrm{pros}}-\bar{s}_{g}^{\mathrm{pros}}}{\max(\sigma_{g}^{\mathrm{pros}},\,0.3)},
```

```math
\displaystyle q_{i} \displaystyle=z_{i}^{\mathrm{fid}}+w\,z_{i}^{\mathrm{pros}},\qquad w=1,
```

where $`\bar{s}_{g}`$ and $`\sigma_{g}`$ are the mean and standard
deviation within group $`g`$. The floors prevent small group variances
from amplifying either score. The combined scalar $`q_{i}`$ is the
reward passed to the GRPO normalization. With $`w=1`$, the two axes have
equal coefficients, although the floors can make their effective
magnitudes differ.

We combine the axes before computing the GRPO advantage. Computing a
separate advantage for each would normalize each axis independently,
potentially giving a low-variance signal disproportionate influence.
Standardizing the critic score also prevents its prompt-dependent raw
spread from determining its contribution to the combined reward.

### 4.4 Bounding Temporal-Scale Drift

The pause-compression ladder metioned in Sec. 4.2 provides one
controlled cue in critic training: within each system, progressively
compressed pauses receive lower scores. This teaches the critic to treat
pause structure as one aspect of prosodic realism but can also encourage
a favor for longer total duration, since preserving pauses increase the
length of utterance. Hence we constrain temporal scale separately with a
one-sided log penalty beyond a 35% margin over the matched reference.

Let $`\rho_{i}`$ be the ratio of rollout duration to reference duration,
with threshold $`k=1.35`$. The one-sided log penalty is

```math
u_{i}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\log\frac{\rho_{i}}{k},\,0,\,c\right),\qquad C_{i}=\frac{u_{i}-\bar{u}_{g}}{\max(\sigma_{g}^{u},\,0.1)}, \tag{11}
```

where $`c`$ caps the penalty, and $`\bar{u}_{g}`$ and $`\sigma_{g}^{u}`$
are the mean and standard deviation of $`u_{i}`$ within rollout group
$`g`$. Thus, $`u_{i}=0`$ below the duration threshold and increases
logarithmically above it. If all rollouts in a group satisfy the
threshold, the centered cost is zero for the entire group. Dual ascent
adjusts $`\lambda`$ toward a 15% rate of rollouts exceeding the
threshold. The cost enters the policy update as defined in Sec. 4.3.

On a fixed evaluation set, duration stays within 6% of its initial
value, with fewer than 5% of utterances exceeding the reference by more
than 50%. Critic scores and speaker similarity nevertheless improve,
supporting gains in delivery rather than duration.

## 5 Analysis

We report results on the nine languages in the CV3 benchmark
\[9\]—Chinese, English, Japanese, Korean, German, French, Spanish,
Italian, and Russian—and on thirteen additional languages: Arabic,
Finnish, Hindi, Indonesian, Malay, Dutch, Norwegian, Polish, Portuguese,
Swedish, Thai, Turkish, and Vietnamese. Per-language results are given
in Appendix A.

### 5.1 Data Mixture

Chinese and English each make up about 30% of the supervised fine-tuning
data; each other language contributes at most 3%. In the GRPO stage
optimized with WER and speaker-similarity rewards, about two-thirds of
the prompts are in the nine CV3 languages, with roughly 7% from each.
The remaining prompts are in thirteen additional languages, with roughly
3% from each language except Thai, which accounts for about 1%. For the
realism stage, the prompts corpus uses Chinese and English in roughly
equal proportions.

### 5.2 Stage-wise Results

On the nine CV3 languages, mean error rate falls from 5.41% after SFT to
5.01% after WER/SIM optimization and 4.86% after realism optimization.
The WER/SIM stage lowers error in every language. Speaker similarity
also improves across stages, with most of the increase occurring during
realism optimization.

On the thirteen additional languages, WER/SIM optimization yields no
aggregate improvement: error rate rises slightly from 22.36% to 22.53%,
while SIM changes from 0.638 to 0.637. The aggregate changes are small
because improvements in some languages are offset by regressions in
others. By contrast, realism optimization improves both aggregate
metrics, with lower error rates and higher SIM in all thirteen
languages.

### 5.3 Blind Human Evaluation

We conducted a blind listening study to compare outputs from the SFT,
WER/SIM, and realism stages. The realism-stage model received a
substantially higher Elo rating than either of the other two models,
while the SFT and WER/SIM models received similar ratings.

## 6 Limitations and Conclusion

_Limitations._ The realism critic requires a matched human reference for
each sentence. Its scores are relative: they rank candidates for the
same prompt according to the ordering established by the listening
study, but do not provide an absolute measure of naturalness or replace
a content check. Though the critic ranks overall synthesis quality
across systems, its controlled signal for separating prosodic cues from
generator identity comes from pause-flattened variants. These variants
isolate pause timing; they do not provide equally targeted supervision
for pitch, energy, or other prosodic cues.

The duration penalty reduces length drift but does not eliminate it.
Preference optimization has a different limit: it can make existing
prosodic patterns more or less likely, but cannot teach the model
patterns it never produces.

The 13 additional languages account for a smaller share of the training
data, and their recognition error remains substantially higher than that
on the nine CV3 languages. We do not evaluate audio artifacts such as
buzzing or vocoder noise and the metrics used in evaluation would not
capture them.

_Conclusion._ We presented Audio8-TTS, a 0.6B dual-autoregressive model
trained in three stages: supervised fine-tuning, GRPO with word error
rate and speaker similarity rewards, and GRPO with a reference-anchored
realism critic. The critic is trained against a quality ordering
established through a blind listening study, with an identity-erasure
objective to keep its score from tracking generator identity.

On the nine CV3 languages, error rate falls at each stage and mean
speaker similarity rises. On the 13 additional languages, the WER/SIM
stage leaves aggregate results nearly unchanged, while the realism stage
improves both metrics in every language, despite using only Chinese and
English prompts. In a separate blind listening study, the realism-stage
model receives a substantially higher Elo rating than the SFT and
WER/SIM models, whose ratings are similar. The duration constraint also
keeps output duration close to its initial level as critic scores and
speaker similarity improve.
