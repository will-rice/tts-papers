# Cross-Lingual TTS for Indian Languages: A Benchmark and Inference-Time Duration Estimation Strategies

Adarsh Arigala^(\*)    Arjun Gangwar^(\*)    Srinivasan Umesh

###### Abstract

Recent alignment-free Text-to-Speech (TTS) models achieve high synthesis
quality by removing explicit alignment modules and simplifying training.
However, they often struggle in cross-lingual scenarios due to
differences in character durations across languages, leading to duration
mismatch and degraded speech quality. Existing approaches typically
address this issue using explicit duration predictors, increasing model
complexity. Systematic evaluation of TTS performance for Indian
languages in cross-lingual settings remains under-explored due to the
lack of standardized evaluation benchmarks. In this work, we construct a
cross-lingual evaluation benchmark covering English and nine Indian
languages and propose a language-aware inference-time duration
estimation strategy for alignment-free TTS that incorporates
language-specific duration-per character statistics without retraining
or architectural modifications. We further develop heuristic
formulations that refine duration estimation based on language-dependent
duration variations. Experiments on bilingual and multilingual A-DMA
models demonstrate improved speech naturalness, intelligibility, and
speaker similarity while reducing duration mismatch. ¹¹ 1 The dataset
and code recipe are available at:

## 1 Introduction

Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have achieved significant progress with the
emergence of neural architectures, enabling natural and expressive
speech synthesis. Recent alignment-free models such as E2-TTS Eskimez et
al. (2024), F5-TTS Chen et al. (2025) and A-DMA Choi et al. (2025),
simplify training by removing explicit alignment modules while
maintaining high synthesis quality. Although these models perform well
when reference and target speech is in the same language, they often
struggle in cross-lingual scenarios where reference and target languages
differ. This happens due to differences in writing systems, orthographic
characteristics, and language-specific duration-per character variations
across languages. Moreover, in F5-TTS, duration estimation is based on
the number of UTF-8 bytes obtained by encoding the input text, rather
than the actual number of characters. Since characters from different
writing systems occupy different numbers of UTF-8 bytes and languages
exhibit different duration-per-character patterns, the estimated
duration can become inaccurate, leading to duration mismatch and
degraded synthesis quality.

To address these challenges, recent works have explored various
strategies to improve duration modeling and generation quality in
alignment-free TTS. Cross-Lingual F5-TTS Liu et al. (2026) removes the
dependency on reference transcripts in flow-matching TTS and enables
cross-lingual voice cloning through forced alignment during training.
However, it introduces additional training complexity by requiring
MMS-based alignment Pratap et al. (2024) preprocessing and separately
trained speaking-rate predictors, making the overall pipeline more
complex than standard alignment-free TTS systems.

Another work, PFluxTTS Pankov et al. (2026) proposes a hybrid
flow-matching framework that combines a duration-guided model and an
alignment-free model through inference-time fusion, improving the
trade-off between alignment stability and naturalness. However, it
relies on an explicit duration predictor, which reduces the benefits of
alignment-free TTS by reintroducing duration modeling.

Most people in India are bilingual and frequently switch between English
and their regional languages during everyday conversations.
Consequently, cross-lingual TTS between English and Indian languages has
become increasingly important. These languages exhibit substantial
differences in orthographic characteristics and character-to-speech
duration, making accurate duration estimation essential for stable
cross-lingual speech synthesis. Despite its practical importance,
systematic evaluation of TTS performance for Indian languages in
cross-lingual settings remains underexplored due to the lack of
standardized evaluation benchmarks and limited investigation of duration
adaptation strategies for alignment-free synthesis.

To address these limitations, we propose a cross-lingual evaluation
benchmark covering nine Indian languages and English and introduce
language-aware inference-time duration estimation strategies for
alignment-free TTS. Our approach incorporates language-specific
duration-per character statistics directly during inference without
requiring retraining or additional model components. Specifically, we
design heuristic formulations that adjust the estimated duration
according to language-specific duration-per-character statistics,
enabling effective cross-lingual duration adaptation.

## 2 Methodology

### 2.1 Baselines

We adopt A-DMA Choi et al. (2025) framework for our experiments. It is
based on the F5-TTS Chen et al. (2025) backbone and introduces a
training objective that combines conditional flow matching with
dual-modality text and speech representation alignment losses to
accelerate convergence. We utilize self-supervised representations (SSL)
from the 21st layer of data2vec-AQC Lodagala et al. (2023) to compute
the speech-level alignment loss. The model is trained using the
conditional flow matching loss from F5-TTS as the primary generative
objective. A Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) based text
alignment loss Graves et al. (2006) is applied at an intermediate layer
to for early character-level alignment, and a speech-level alignment
loss is used at a later layer to enforce alignment in the speech
representation space.

```math
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CFM}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{text}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{text}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{speech}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{speech}},
```

where $`\lambda_{\mathrm{text}}=0.1`$ and
$`\lambda_{\mathrm{speech}}=1.0`$. This combined objective improves
convergence and speech synthesis quality.

### 2.2 Cross-lingual Evaluation Dataset

To test the cross-lingual generalization capability of the A-DMA Choi et
al. (2025) model, we constructed a dedicated cross-lingual evaluation
benchmark covering nine Indian languages and English. In this setting,
the reference speech and the text being synthesized belong to different
languages.

For each Indian-language and English pair in the cross-lingual
evaluation setting, we constructed approximately two hours of evaluation
data, allocating one hour to (i) English Reference and Indic Generation
and one hour to (ii) Indic Reference and English Generation. The
Indian-language speech was selected from the Rasa Srinivasa Varadhan et
al. (2024) and IndicVoices-R Sankar et al. (2024) test splits, while the
English speech was sampled from the IndicTTS Consortium et al. (2023)
training split. ³³ 3 Indian English is available only in IndicTTS-v2, so
we take 500 utterances from its 250-hour training set as a held-out set.
Each language has only two speakers, and all speakers also recorded
Indian English.

We considered two cross-lingual evaluation scenarios: (i)
Indian-language reference speech with English generation text, and (ii)
English reference speech with Indian-language generation text. In both
scenarios, reference and target utterances were randomly sampled subject
to duration constraints of 4–7 seconds and 4–10 seconds, respectively.
Detailed statistics of the constructed benchmark, including the total
number of utterances for each language pair, are provided in Table 1.

### 2.3 Model Setup

We trained the original A-DMA Choi et al. (2025) model for Indian
languages in two settings: bilingual and multilingual. For the bilingual
setup, each model was trained on one Indian language combined with a
30-hour subset of Indian English data Consortium et al. (2023), while
the multilingual model was trained on 24 languages as described in
Section 3.1.

The bilingual model used the F5-TTS Small architecture with 18 DiT
layers Peebles and Xie (2023), 12 attention heads, and 768-dimensional
embeddings, together with a 4-layer ConvNeXtV2 Woo et al. (2023) module
with 512-dimensional embeddings, totaling 159M parameters. It was
trained for 1.2M steps with 20k warmup updates and an effective batch
size of 0.75 hours.

The multilingual model used F5-TTS Base with 22 DiT layers Peebles and
Xie (2023), 16 attention heads, and 1024-dimensional embeddings, along
with a 4-layer ConvNeXtV2 Woo et al. (2023) module with 512-dimensional
embeddings, totaling 335.8M parameters. It was trained for 300k updates
with 45k warmup updates and an effective batch size of 2.09 hours. For
both settings, 100-channel mel spectrograms were extracted at a 24 kHz
sampling rate with a hop size of 256 samples. Waveform synthesis was
performed using the pre-trained Vocos vocoder Siuzdak (2024), and
optimization was carried out using AdamW Loshchilov and Hutter (2019)
with a peak learning rate of $`7.5\times 10^{-5}`$.

### 2.4 Language-Aware Heuristic Duration Estimation

#### 2.4.1 Duration Estimation in Original F5-TTS

Alignment-free TTS models require predicting target speech duration
prior to synthesis. A-DMA uses the F5-TTS duration estimation
formulation defined as:

```math
D_{F5}=D_{ref}+\left\lfloor\frac{D_{ref}}{L_{ref}}\cdot\frac{L_{gen}}{s}\right\rfloor \tag{1}
```

where $`D_{ref}`$ denotes the reference speech duration, $`L_{ref}`$ and
$`L_{gen}`$ represent the number of characters in reference and
generation text, which are converted into UTF-8 byte encoding for
duration estimation, and $`s`$ denotes the local speed factor, which we
set to 1.

While the standard F5-TTS formulation performs well when reference and
generated speech share the same language, it computes text length using
UTF-8 byte-level encoding, which is not suitable for multilingual
settings. In cross-lingual generation, this becomes problematic because
Indian languages typically require multiple bytes per character, whereas
English characters generally require a single byte. Consequently,
byte-level length estimation leads to inconsistent scaling in duration
prediction across languages. Previous works have addressed this
limitation by incorporating explicit speaking-rate predictors Liu et al.
(2026) or duration predictors Pankov et al. (2026), which require
modifications to the original F5-TTS architecture and model training.

To overcome this issue, we reformulate Original F5-TTS in a
character-level space and report results using this variant, referred to
as Modified F5-TTS. Additionally, we propose two language-aware duration
modeling strategies, Heuristic-1 and Heuristic-2, which incorporate
duration-per character information into the F5-TTS formulation through
fusion and additive interaction mechanisms, respectively.

#### 2.4.2 Heuristic-1: Language-Aware Duration Model

The heuristic (H1) extends the original F5 duration estimation by
incorporating language-specific duration-per-character statistics.

```math
\displaystyle D_{H1}=D_{ref} \displaystyle+\alpha\left\lfloor\frac{D_{ref}}{L_{ref}}\cdot\frac{L_{gen}}{s}\right\rfloor \tag{2}
```

```math
\displaystyle+\beta(D_{ind}+D_{eng})
```

where $`\alpha=0.7`$ and $`\beta=0.3`$ in all experiments. The effect of
varying $`\alpha`$ and $`\beta`$ is analyzed in Section 5.2. $`D_{F5}`$
denotes the duration estimated by F5-TTS. Duration estimations for
Indian languages and English are computed as follows to capture
language-dependent duration variations:

```math
\begin{array}[]{c c}D_{ind}=\dfrac{d_{ind}^{c}\times C_{ind}}{s}&D_{eng}=\dfrac{d_{eng}^{c}\times C_{eng}}{s}\end{array} \tag{3}
```

where $`D_{ref}`$ denotes the reference speech duration,
$`d_{ind}^{c},d_{eng}^{c}`$ denote the duration-per character statistics
for Indic languages and English, respectively, $`C_{eng}`$ and
$`C_{ind}`$ denote the number of English and Indic characters in the
generated text, and $`s`$ denotes the local speed factor, which we set
to 1.

The language-specific duration-per character are estimated from
multilingual training corpora. For each utterance, the duration-per
character is computed as:

```math
d^{c}=\frac{L_{audio}}{C_{text}}=\frac{N_{samples}/H}{C_{text}} \tag{4}
```

where $`N_{samples}`$ denotes the waveform length, $`H`$ denotes the hop
length, and $`C_{text}`$ denotes the text length in characters.

To obtain robust language-specific statistics, duration-per-character
values are aggregated across all speakers within each language using the
corresponding training corpus (Table 2). For example, the Telugu
statistic is computed from the Telugu training corpus, and similarly for
the remaining languages. The language-specific average
duration-per-character is computed as:

```math
d^{c}_{lang}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{utt}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{utt}}}D^{c}_{i} \tag{5}
```

where $`N_{\text{utt}}`$ denotes the number of utterances per language.

Fig. 1 shows the utterance-level distribution of duration-per-character
values for all languages in the training corpora. Although each language
exhibits natural variability due to differences in speakers and speaking
styles, we use the average duration per character, $`d_{lang}^{c}`$, for
each language in the proposed heuristics. The resulting
language-specific average duration-per-character statistics are:

Indian English (9.6), Kannada (8.7), Telugu (8.4), Tamil (7.0), Hindi
(8.5), Malayalam (6.8), Punjabi (10.1), Bengali (8.1), Odia (8.7), and
Marathi (9.8). This formulation combines the original F5 duration
estimate with language-aware duration estimation to better handle
cross-lingual synthesis scenarios.

#### 2.4.3 Heuristic-2: Additive duration-per character Guided Scaling

The second heuristic extends the original character-based duration
estimation by incorporating relative duration-per character differences
between languages through an additive correction mechanism. The target
duration is computed as:

```math
D_{H2}=D_{ref}+\left(\frac{D_{ref}}{C_{ref}}+\frac{d^{c}_{gen}}{d^{c}_{ref}}\right)\frac{C_{gen}}{s} \tag{6}
```

where $`D_{ref}`$ denotes the reference speech duration, $`C_{ref}`$ and
$`C_{gen}`$ denote the number of characters in the reference and
generated text, and $`d^{c}_{ref}`$ and $`d^{c}_{gen}`$ denote the
language-specific duration-per character statistics corresponding to the
reference and target generation languages. $`\frac{D_{ref}}{C_{ref}}`$
captures the reference duration-per-character, while
$`\frac{d^{c}_{gen}}{d^{c}_{ref}}`$ provides a relative duration
adjustment that introduces a language-dependent correction to
per-character scaling across languages.

## 3 Experimental Setup

### 3.1 Training Dataset

We follow the original A-DMA Choi et al. (2025) training setup and adapt
it for Indian languages in order to evaluate its multilingual
performance. The model was trained using four multilingual speech
corpora: IndicVoices-R Sankar et al. (2024) containing 1,587.5 hours of
speech from 21 languages; IndicTTS V2 and V3 versions Consortium et al.
(2023) containing 547 hours and 363.3 hours of speech, respectively,
covering 14 languages in V2 and 20 languages in V3; and Rasa Srinivasa
Varadhan et al. (2024) containing 750.6 hours of speech across 16
languages. In total, the model was trained on 24 languages, comprising
approximately 3,248.5 hours of multilingual speech data. Detailed
per-language training hours are provided in Table 2. The trained model
are evaluated using the proposed cross-lingual benchmark described in
Section 2.2.

### 3.2 Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate synthesized speech using four objective metrics. Word Error
Rate (WER) and Character Error Rate (CER) are computed from ASR
transcriptions generated using Whisper-large-v3 Radford et al. (2023)
for English and IndicConformer-600M  AI4Bharat (2024) for Indian
languages. Speaker Similarity (SIM) evaluates speaker identity
preservation using WavLM-large-based speaker embeddings Chen et al. ().
UTMOS Saeki et al. (2022) measures speech naturalness via an objective
MOS prediction model.

## 4 Results

The trained bilingual and multilingual A-DMA models are evaluated using
the proposed benchmark under four duration strategies: Original F5-TTS,
Modified F5-TTS, Heuristic-1, and Heuristic-2. We evaluate performance
in two cross-lingual settings: (i) Indic Reference and English
Generation and (ii) English Reference and Indic Generation. Although
recent methods such as Cross-Lingual F5-TTS Liu et al. (2026) and
PFluxTTS Pankov et al. (2026) have proposed alternative duration
modeling strategies, their official implementations were not publicly
available at the time of our experiments. Consequently, we compare our
proposed methods against the publicly available A-DMA baseline.

### 4.1 English Reference and Indic Generation

Table 3 presents cross-lingual results for English reference and
Indian-language speech generation. The original F5-TTS exhibits severe
degradation in cross-lingual settings, primarily due to byte-level text
length estimation, which leads to incorrect duration scaling and high
WER/CER across all languages. For instance, in Kannada, the bilingual
model yields a WER/CER of 109.56/57.07, while the multilingual model
also exhibits degraded performance, with a WER/CER of 55.88/22.22.

The Modified F5-TTS substantially reduces these errors across all
languages by correcting text-length estimation, resulting in large
relative improvements in both WER and CER. In Kannada, the multilingual
model achieves relative reductions of 52.7%/81.2% in WER/CER compared to
the Original F5-TTS, indicating better linguistic alignment. Further,
Heuristic-1 and Heuristic-2 for the multilingual model, provide relative
WER/CER reductions of 0.9%/0.2% and 4.9%/6.2% over the Modified F5-TTS,
respectively, while UTMOS increases from 3.460 to 3.475 and 3.495.
Similar trends are observed across all languages.

### 4.2 Indic Reference and English Generation

Table 4 reports results for the reverse direction. The original F5-TTS
again shows high error rates. For Kannada, the bilingual model yields a
WER/CER of 57.01/52.16, while the multilingual model reduces these to
49.66/44.33, respectively.

The Modified F5-TTS significantly improves cross-lingual robustness
across all languages. In Kannada, the multilingual and bilingual models
achieves relative reductions of 93.2%/97.6% and 91.6%/96.1% in WER/CER
over the Original F5-TTS. Further, Heuristic-1 and Heuristic-2 for the
multilingual model, provide additional improvements over the Modified
F5-TTS, achieving WER/CER reductions of 5.4%/6.6% and 3.0%/4.7%,
respectively. SIM remains comparable, while UTMOS shows slight
improvement. Similar trends are observed across all languages. Overall,
all proposed duration strategies operate within a similar performance
range, showing stable improvements across Indian languages.

Compared to bilingual models, multilingual models generally achieve
lower WER and CER across most languages, indicating improved robustness
due to exposure to diverse scripts and duration-per character variations
during training.

## 5 Further Analysis

### 5.1 Qualitative Analysis

Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 show the predicted and ground-truth (GT) duration
distributions on our benchmark for two cross-lingual settings: (i)
English reference and Tamil speech generation and (ii) Tamil reference
and English speech generation. GT durations correspond to natural
multi-speaker utterances, while generated speech is conditioned on a
reference utterance. Since GT and generated samples are not
speaker-aligned, we compare duration distributions instead of absolute
differences. The original F5-TTS underestimates durations relative to GT
due to byte-level UTF-8 encoding. The modified F5-TTS alleviates this
issue by switching to character-level encoding; however, it still
underestimates durations, resulting in speech that sounds too fast in
setting (ii). Meanwhile, the proposed Heuristic-1 and Heuristic-2
methods produce durations that align better with the GT distribution in
both settings. This indicates that incorporating language-specific
duration-per character statistics during inference improves
cross-lingual duration modeling and yields better alignment with
ground-truth.

### 5.2 Effect of $`\alpha:\beta`$ ratio in Heuristic-1

To investigate the sensitivity of Heuristic-1 to the interpolation
coefficients $`\alpha`$ and $`\beta`$, we evaluate three ratio
configurations: 6:4, 7:3, and 8:2 using the multilingual model across
Telugu, Hindi, and Kannada in both cross-lingual settings. As shown in
Table 5, the performance remains similar across ratio configurations,
with minor improvements. For Telugu in the (i) English Reference and
Indic Generation setting, the 7:3 ratio achieves the best WER/CER of
16.40/1.93, with relative improvements of 2.1%/2.5% over 6:4 and
0.2%/5.9% over 8:2, while SIM remains nearly unchanged.

In the reverse setting, (ii) Indic Reference and English Generation, the
6:4 ratio performs slightly better in some cases. Overall, the optimal
ratio varies slightly with the language pair and direction, and we
recommend experimenting with different values. We adopt $`\alpha=0.7`$
and $`\beta=0.3`$ for all experiments to ensure fair comparisons.

## 6 Conclusion

We propose a cross-lingual evaluation benchmark and language-aware
duration strategies for alignment-free TTS. Our approach introduces
Modified F5 for improved duration estimation and two language-aware
duration prediction methods, Heuristic-1 and Heuristic-2, which
incorporate language-specific duration-per character statistics.
Extensive experiments on both bilingual and multilingual models,
evaluated across nine Indian languages and two cross-lingual directions,
show that the proposed heuristic methods consistently improve duration
alignment. Qualitative analysis further highlights the importance of
language-aware duration modeling for robust multilingual speech
synthesis.

## 7 Limitations

Our approach has a few limitations that we plan to address in future
work. The proposed duration estimation relies on language-level average
duration-per character rates and modifies the inference process without
retraining; therefore, it does not explicitly account for speaker- or
utterance-level variations in speaking style and prosody. In addition,
our evaluation covers nine Indian languages and English, focusing on
English to Indic and Indic to English cross-lingual directions. As
future work, we plan to extend the evaluation to Indic to Indic language
pairs to investigate broader cross-lingual generalization. Finally, the
proposed duration adjustments are heuristic rather than learned, and our
comparison is primarily with the publicly available alignment-free TTS
such as A-DMA/F5-TTS baselines, as implementations of some recent
cross-lingual TTS methods were unavailable during our experiments.
