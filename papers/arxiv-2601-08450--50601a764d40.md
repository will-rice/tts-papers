# Decoding Order Matters in Autoregressive Speech Synthesis

Minghui    Anton

###### Abstract

Autoregressive speech synthesis has traditionally followed a
left-to-right order, yet generation order is a modelling choice. This
paper investigates decoding order through a masked diffusion framework
that enables arbitrary decoding orders at inference time. To isolate the
effect of decoding order from inductive biases introduced by learned
discrete encoders, we operate on scalar quantised Mel-spectrograms. Our
results demonstrate that the left-to-right strategy is suboptimal for
capturing complex acoustic dependencies. The reverse, right-to-left,
order consistently outperforms the traditional approach, while the
adaptive confidence-based strategy top1 achieves the highest Mean
Opinion Score (MOS) among all model outputs. Analysis reveals that the
most effective orders maintain local clusters of consecutive frames to
balance long-range temporal dependencies with local coherence. These
results establish decoding order as a critical parameter for improving
synthesis quality.

###### keywords

speech synthesis, masked diffusion models, order-agnostic decoding

## 1 Introduction

Autoregressive (AR) generation has long been central to speech
synthesis, from earlier systems such as Wavenet \[1\] and Tacotron 2
\[2\] to recent approaches that represent speech as discrete tokens such
as \[3, 4, 5\]. In these systems, speech is generated sequentially,
always following a left-to-right order that mirrors the natural
progression of speech.

From a modelling perspective, left-to-right generation is not
necessarily optimal. Speech dependencies extend beyond a simple causal
chain: pauses and emphasis often depend on global context, while
coarticulation reflects interactions between both past and future
phones. Even when the model is conditioned on the full text or phone
sequence, the decoding order can influence how effectively these
acoustic dependencies are captured. Exploring alternatives to
left-to-right generation is therefore important not only for assessing
the widely adopted and unchallenged convention, but also for improving
synthesis quality.

Viewing decoding order as a modelling choice, consider a factorisation
of the ground-truth distribution $`p(\bm{y}_{1:T})`$ for a length-$`T`$
sequence. Let $`\mathcal{P}=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{I}\}`$ be a partition of
the index set $`\{1,\dots,T\}`$ and let $`S_{I}`$ denote the
permutations of $`\{1,\dots,I\}`$. For any $`\bm{\sigma}\in S_{I}`$, the
chain rule yields

```math
p(\bm{y}_{{1:T}}\mid\bm{\sigma},\mathcal{P})=\prod_{i=1}^{I}p(\bm{y}_{B_{\bm{\sigma}(i)}}\mid\bm{y}_{B_{\bm{\sigma}(<i)}}), \tag{1}
```

where $`B_{\bm{\sigma}(i)}`$ is the subset of indices revealed at step
$`i`$. While (1) is an exact identity for any $`\bm{\sigma}`$, the
invariance no longer holds once the conditionals are approximated by a
parameterised model $`p_{\theta}`$. Because different orders expose the
model to different acoustic contexts, the resulting approximation of
$`p(\bm{y}_{1:T})`$ becomes dependent on the chosen $`\bm{\sigma}`$.
This work focuses on the one-by-one decoding regime where $`|B_{i}|=1`$
for all $`i`$ (thus $`I=T`$). Left-to-right decoding is the special case
$`B_{\bm{\sigma}(i)}=\{i\}`$, but (1) imposes no such constraint. While
separate AR models could be trained under different fixed orders to
identify the optimal approximation, the $`T!`$ possible permutations
make this computationally prohibitive and prevents comparison within a
single unified framework.

The masked diffusion model (MDM) \[6, 7, 8\] is a recently proposed
framework that supports arbitrary decoding orders
$`\bm{\sigma}\in S_{T}`$, thereby permitting AR factorisations under any
permutation. During training, the model predicts randomly masked tokens
from visible ones, so that each token is, in expectation, equally likely
to be reconstructed. This order-agnostic objective avoids favouring any
particular information-revealing order. At inference (Fig. 1), any
generation order $`\bm{\sigma}`$ can be selected, unmasking positions
sequentially conditioned on previously revealed tokens, allowing direct
assessment of how order affects generation quality. To ensure that such
analysis is not confounded by representation learning, we obtain tokens
by directly discretising Mel-spectrograms via scalar quantisation. Using
this framework, we investigate how different decoding orders affect the
quality of AR speech synthesis.

## 2 Related work

### 2.1 Generative modelling framework

Many recent works explore combining diffusion with autoregression for
sequence generation. Block Diffusion \[9\] and speech models such as
DiffAR \[10\] and DiTAR \[11\] group consecutive frames into blocks and
maintain a left-to-right AR generation across blocks, while predicting
content within each block in parallel. We instead adopt the
order-agnostic framework of \[7, 8\] and focus on the one-by-one regime.
The prior work most similar in method is MaskGCT \[12\], which optimises
a similar loss. Our objective, however, is explicitly designed to enable
permutation-invariant generation. Beyond speech synthesis, alternative
generation orders have been examined in reasoning \[8\], text generation
\[13\] and text-to-image \[14\] settings, where the choice of
$`\bm{\sigma}`$ affects performance, motivating exploration beyond
left-to-right factorisations.

### 2.2 Speech tokenisation

Speech tokens are typically derived through learned quantisation \[3,
4\] or clustering-based methods \[15\]. Because these discretisation
mappings are trained under specific reconstruction or self-supervised
objectives, they may introduce latent inductive biases to the resulting
token inventory. As a result, analyses based on a particular learned
tokeniser may not generalise to alternative or future tokenisation
schemes. To isolate the effect of decoding order from the influence of a
learned encoder, we apply scalar quantisation directly to
Mel-spectrograms. This signal-derived, deterministic, parameter-free
approach ensures that the tokenisation remains a transparent reflection
of the acoustic signal. Scalar quantisation has previously been used for
discrete generative modelling in the image domain, notably by FSQ-VQ
\[16\]. As demonstrated in Sec. 4.2, these quantised Mel-spectrograms
remain compatible with off-the-shelf HiFi-GAN \[17\] vocoders without
the need for specialised retraining.

## 3 Method

Autoregressive sequence decoding can be characterised by two design
choices, the order $`\bm{\sigma}`$ in which frames are generated and the
update size $`|B_{i}|`$, i.e., how many elements are decoded at each
step. While many models impose a fixed schedule (e.g., left-to-right,
one by one), the framework used here performs iterative masked
prediction (Fig. 1), producing a predictive distribution for all
positions at each step. A subset of positions is then selected according
to a decoding schedule and updated, and the process repeats until all
tokens are generated. To study decoding order, we restrict the model to
update one position at a time ($`|B_{i}|=1`$ for every step) without
replacement, making it directly comparable to conventional AR models, as
in \[7\].

The following sections describe inference and training. Let $`\bm{\mu}`$
denote a prior derived from text, with temporal length $`T`$.
Conditioned on $`\bm{\mu}`$, the model generates an output sequence
$`\bm{y}`$ of length $`T`$. In models such as Grad-TTS \[18\] and
Matcha-TTS \[19\], all frames are updated simultaneously, whereas our
model updates one frame at a time for $`T`$ steps. Since MDMs operate on
discrete data, Sec. 3.3 details the quantisation method, while the
motivation for this choice is discussed earlier in Sec. 2.2. Although
decoding could be applied to individual time–frequency bins, we perform
generation at the frame level. Sec. 3.4 presents the decoding
strategies.

For each generation step $`t=1,\dots,T`$ and position
$`i\in\{1,\dots,T\}`$, we define the indicator masks

where $`\bm{m}`$ indicates previously generated frames and $`\bm{n}`$
the current position. Conditioned on $`\bm{\mu}`$ and the context
$`\bm{m}\odot\bm{y}`$, the model outputs parameters of a categorical

$`\mathcal{C}(\bm{y}|\bm{f}_{\theta}(\bm{m}\odot\bm{y},\bm{\mu},\bm{m}))`$,

### 3.2 Order-agnostic training

Algorithm 2 summarises the training procedure. Rather than maximising
the chain-rule likelihood in Eq. (1), which requires sequential
prediction under a chosen permutation and is expensive when
marginalising over permutations, we optimise the evidence lower bound
(ELBO) on the log-likelihood \[7\]:

```math
\displaystyle\log p(\bm{y}) \displaystyle\geq T\cdot\mathbb{E}_{t,\bm{\sigma}}\left[\frac{1}{T-t+1}\sum_{k\in\bm{\sigma}(\geq t)}\log p(\bm{y}_{k}\mid\bm{y}_{\bm{\sigma}(<t)})\right], \tag{3}
```

where $`t\sim\mathcal{U}(1,\ldots,T)`$ and $`\bm{\sigma}`$ is uniformly
sampled from $`S_{T}`$. The formulation in Eq. (3) allows the
conditional log-likelihood terms for all $`k\in\bm{\sigma}(\geq t)`$ to
be computed in parallel for a sampled pair $`(t,\bm{\sigma})`$, yielding
an efficient and order-agnostic objective. To approximate the
expectation, we use a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate per step.

### 3.3 Quantisation

We apply a shared linear quantiser across frequency bins, yielding a
parameter-free discretisation that avoids encoder-induced biases.
Specifically, for a value $`y\in[a,b]`$, the quantised index $`\hat{y}`$
is given by:

```math
\hat{y}=\operatorname{round}\left(\frac{y-a}{b-a}\cdot(Q-1)\right),\quad\hat{y}\in\{0,1,\dots,Q-1\}, \tag{4}
```

where $`Q`$ denotes the number of quantisation bins. Since the model
predicts entire frames, each with $`n_{f}`$ bins taking one of $`Q`$
values, a frame corresponds to one of $`Q^{n_{f}}`$ possible tokens.

### 3.4 Decoding strategies

We examine fixed orders, left-to-right (l2r), and right-to-left (r2l) as
well as adaptive top-$`K`$ and duration-guided strategies. These
adaptive strategies prioritise high-confidence frames, inherently
resulting in a non-linear generation path. To further explore this
property, we evaluate stochasticity by interpolating between l2r and
fully random (uro) orders. This allows us to observe how the model
reacts to non-linearity, whether the specific decoding order is
confidence driven or not.

#### 3.4.1 Top-$`K`$ probability

At step $`t`$, for each undecoded position $`k\in\bm{\sigma}(\geq t)`$
we compute

```math
\displaystyle s_{k}(t)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{f}}\max_{j\in\{0,\dots,Q-1\}}\log p(\bm{y}_{k,i}=j\mid\bm{y}_{\bm{\sigma}(<t)}), \tag{5}
```

which sums the maximum log-probabilities across all $`n_{f}`$ bins. The
next position is chosen as

```math
\displaystyle k_{t}^{\star}\;=\;\arg\max_{k\in\bm{\sigma}(\geq t)}s_{k}(t), \tag{6}
```

The decoding order is then updated as
$`\bm{\sigma}(t)\leftarrow k_{t}^{\star}`$. In this work, we set
$`K=1`$. This strategy determines only decoding positions, not their
values. We consider two variants, top1, which samples from the predicted
distribution, and top1\*, which selects the most probable values at each
step.

#### 3.4.2 Duration-guided decoding

While inspecting the adaptively determined orders (see Sec. 4.4), we
observed that the model often decodes contiguous positions, selecting
adjacent frames consecutively. This suggests a preference for local
continuity. To examine whether chunk-wise decoding improves generation
quality, we first segment each utterance. A simple approach is to use
the duration predictor, which estimates how many frames each frame
spans. To select which segment to decode, we compute the average
frame-level confidence $`s_{k}(t)`$ within each segment (Eq. (5)), and
select the segment with the highest score; however, since the confidence
is based on predicted probabilities, we note these probabilities may not
be well calibrated \[20\]. The frames within the chosen segment are then
updated in a random order, although different strategies could also be
considered. This decoding scheme is denoted as dur.

#### 3.4.3 Stochastic path interpolation

To evaluate the impact of non-linearity, we control the degree of
randomness by perturbing the left-to-right order $`\bm{\sigma}_{l2r}`$
with swaps between two randomly chosen positions. Theoretically,
approximately $`T\log T`$ swaps produce a near-uniform permutation
distribution \[8\]. We scale this quantity by a factor $`\beta\in(0,1)`$
to obtain a coarse measure of randomness, yielding orders close tol2r as
$`\beta\to 0`$ and close to uro as $`\beta\to 1`$.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental setup

We use LJSpeech \[21\], a corpus with $`13,100`$ clips (1-10s) of read
speech by a single female native English speaker. We adopt the same
split as Grad-TTS \[18\]. As in \[18, 19\], the model consists of a text
encoder, a duration predictor and a decoder. The encoder produces a
latent text representation, upsampled using predicted durations to
obtain $`\bm{\mu}`$, which serves as the decoding prior. We train the
decoder as described in Sec. 3.2. For each time-frequency bin, the
decoder predicts parameters for a mixture of $`5`$ logistic components,
sampled independently across Mel bins. At inference, a component is
selected via the Gumbel-Max trick with temperature $`t_{1}`$ and the bin
value is drawn from the chosen logistic distribution with temperature
$`t_{2}`$.

We evaluate with Mel-Cepstral Distortion (MCD), $`\log F_{0}`$,
UTMOSv2 \[22\], and Word Error Rate (WER), all on 50 randomly sampled
test-set utterances. WER is obtained using a HuBERT-based ASR model
trained on read speech. ¹¹ 1

$`\log F_{0}`$ are calculated against vocoded references. For subjective
evaluation, we report MOS via Amazon Mechanical Turk from 75 Master
Workers (self-identified native English speakers), with 10 ratings per
sample on average.²² 2

### 4.2 Effect of Mel-spectrogram quantisation

Following Sec. 3.3, we conduct preliminary experiments using scalar
quantisation of Mel-spectrograms. On the validation set, Mel features
are discretised into $`Q`$ levels and vocoded with HiFi-GAN. As
expected, lower $`Q`$ increases MCD and $`\log F_{0}`$ (see Fig. 2). At
$`Q=10`$, both remain low ($`2.40`$ and $`0.19`$), indicating limited
degradation relative to continuous Mel-spectrograms. WER is already
relatively low at $`Q=5`$ ($`7.48`$), decreases with larger $`Q`$, and
stabilises at $`5.22`$ from $`Q=100`$ onward. These results show that
Mel-spectrograms are sufficiently redundant to support simple scalar
quantisation while maintaining low MCD, $`\log F_{0}`$ and WER, and that
standard vocoders such as HiFi-GAN can reconstruct high-quality speech
from heavily quantised inputs. We therefore use $`Q=100`$ in subsequent
experiments.

### 4.3 Controlling randomness in decoding order

Following Sec. 3.4.3, decoding order randomness is controlled by varying
$`\beta`$: $`0.001,0.003,0.01,0.025,0.1,0.35,0.5,1.0`$. Smaller
$`\beta`$ values yield orders close to l2r, whereas larger values
approach fully mixed sampling (uro). To reduce variance due to random
swaps, each $`\beta`$ setting is evaluated over $`5`$ independent runs
and results are averaged per utterance.

As shown in Fig. 3, $`\log F_{0}`$ is largely insensitive to $`\beta`$.
As $`\beta`$ increases, MCD decreases, indicating reduced spectral
distortion under more mixed order. UTMOS, on the other hand, declines
with increasing randomness, suggesting a preference for more sequential
decoding. In contrast, WER exhibits non-monotonic behaviour, with a
minimum at $`\beta=0.01`$. Overall, the effect of decoding-order
randomness on synthetic speech is metric-dependent. Although one might
expect more left-to-right decoding to be advantageous, it does not
consistently outperform more mixed orders.

### 4.4 The impact of decoding order

We now evaluate specific decoding strategies directly. For the baseline,
we train Grad-TTS on full utterances instead of using a released
checkpoint trained on segmented inputs ($`172`$ frames). We evaluate two
decoding strategies, fixed 100-step decoding (grad-100) and length-based
decoding (grad-L). The former uses a sufficiently large number of steps
to ensure high synthesis quality, while the latter matches the number of
decoding steps used by the proposed model.

Automatic evaluation results are shown in Fig. 4. Overall, the adaptive
strategy dur performs the best, closely followed by r2l, with low WER,
MCD, $`\log F_{0}`$ and relatively high UTMOS (ground truth WER:
$`6.64`$). Among sequential strategies, r2l outperforms l2r in WER,
UTMOS and MCD with comparable $`\log F_{0}`$, indicating that
left-to-right decoding typical of AR models is not optimal. top1\*
performs similarly to top1 in UTMOS and MCD. However, top1 shows lower
pitch distortion while top1\* achieves better intelligibility.
Deterministic argmax selection in top1\* collapses each bin to its most
probable value, reducing spectral variance across frequency bins. This
loss of fine-grained variation may weaken harmonic structure and degrade
pitch naturalness. Although the Grad-TTS baselines generate continuous
Mel-spectrograms, they yield marginal gains in UTMOS compared to other
approaches but suffer from much higher MCD. Between the two models,
grad-L shows worse $`\log F_{0}`$ despite improved WER.

MOS results show similar trend (Table 1). Among systems, top1 achieves
the highest MOS after the vocoded reference, while uro performs worst.
r2l ranks second among the trained systems, outperforming some adaptive
strategies and further suggesting that left-to-right decoding is
suboptimal. The gap between top1 and top1\* likely reflects higher pitch
distortion in top1\*, which reduces perceived naturalness despite better
intelligibility. dur shows only mediocre performance; the benefit of
confidence-based, segment decoding appears to be offset by the random
order within each segment. l2r and top1\* are slightly worse than the
Grad-TTS baselines.

Analysis shows that confidence-based decoding frequently generates
consecutive frames in a right-to-left order. We quantify this using the
right-to-left transition ratio $`\rho_{r2l}`$, defined as the proportion
of steps satisfying $`\bm{\sigma}(t-1)-\bm{\sigma}(t)=1`$ (normalised by
sequence length minus one); $`\rho_{l2r}`$ is defined analogously. For
top1, $`\rho_{r2l}\approx 0.9`$ and $`\rho_{l2r}\approx 0.05`$,
indicating decoding dominated by local right-to-left expansions. The
strong subjective performance of top1, with the competitive results of
r2l, suggests that locally right-to-left progression is associated with
improved perceptual quality. The weaker performance of dur, which breaks
this directional continuity through random ordering within segments, is
consistent with this observation. While this does not imply causality,
it indicates a directional pattern correlated with better performance.

## 5 Conclusion and discussion

This study shows that the conventional left-to-right decoding order in
AR speech synthesis is suboptimal for capturing complex acoustic
dependencies. Using an order-agnostic MDM, we find that strategies such
as right-to-left and adaptive confidence-based decoding improve both
objective and subjective metrics, with top1 achieving the highest
perceived naturalness. Our results indicate that decoding order is a
critical yet overlooked modelling factor. Performance improves when
decoding balances global context with local coherence, particularly
through contiguous frame generation with a locally dominant
right-to-left progression. Scalar-quantised Mel-spectrograms ensure that
these improvements stem from decoding order rather than encoder-specific
token representations. Experiments also show substantial redundancy in
Mel-spectrograms, as high-quality speech can be reconstructed from
heavily quantised inputs. Future work will examine whether these
findings generalise to multi-speaker systems or more diverse acoustic
conditions.
