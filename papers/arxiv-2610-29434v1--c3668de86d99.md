# RubatoCodec: Budgeted Adaptive Speech Tokenization with Streaming Decoding

Chihiro Arata Affiliation: Third Intelligence, Inc.    Shohei Taniguchi
Affiliation: Third Intelligence, Inc.    Gouki Minegishi
Affiliation: Third Intelligence, Inc.    Kan Hatakeyama-Sato

###### Abstract

Neural speech codecs provide discrete tokens for speech generation.
Lower frame rates shorten token sequences, but each token must then
carry more linguistic and acoustic information. Variable-frame-rate
codecs address this trade-off by adapting token allocation to speech
content, but threshold-based allocation lets content determine the token
count, so the sequence length a speech generator must produce varies
across utterances and datasets at a fixed setting. We present
RubatoCodec, a variable-frame-rate speech codec built on X-Codec-2 with
an exact utterance-level event budget and streaming decoding. Its
Adaptive Firing mechanism uses changes in codec features and waveform
spectra to divide speech into variable-duration events without a learned
boundary predictor. Discrete codes and an explicit duration represent
each event, allowing a speech generator to predict and decode one event
at a time. Controlled experiments show that adaptive allocation
outperforms fixed-rate coding in reconstruction fidelity and
intelligibility at low event rates. A model trained on large-scale
multilingual speech achieves competitive reconstruction quality across
English, Japanese, and Mandarin, with the best intelligibility
preservation among the compared codecs on the evaluated English and
Japanese benchmarks at comparable bitrates. In a matched text-to-speech
comparison, the adaptive representation yields lower WER and higher
UTMOS and UTMOSv2 with comparable speaker similarity. The speech
generator also learns the codec’s utterance-level event budget without
an explicit generation-time rate constraint. These results demonstrate
that budgeted adaptive speech representations support both high-quality
reconstruction and streaming text-to-speech generation. Code and weights
are available at
[https://github.com/third-intelligence/RubatoCodec](https://github.com/third-intelligence/RubatoCodec)
and
[https://huggingface.co/third-intelligence/RubatoCodec](https://huggingface.co/third-intelligence/RubatoCodec).

## 1 Introduction

Neural speech codecs encode waveforms as discrete tokens for
reconstruction \[1, 2, 3\] and language-model-based generation,
including AudioLM \[4\], VALL-E \[5\], and recent synthesis and spoken
dialogue systems \[6, 7, 8, 9\]. For generation, the codec’s frame rate
determines the temporal sequence length: ten seconds of speech requires
500 positions at 50 Hz. Lower rates shorten this sequence but require
each position to represent a longer acoustic interval. Recent tokenizers
operate at 12.5 Hz \[6, 8, 9\], where each token spans 80 ms. At this
resolution, where boundaries fall relative to speech content matters: a
fixed grid can merge a rapid phonetic transition into a single token
while spending as many tokens on a pause as on the transition.

Speech is temporally nonstationary: pauses and steady vowels change
slowly, whereas transitions change rapidly. Variable-rate
representations and variable-frame-rate codecs exploit this by assigning
different durations to frames \[10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15\].
Threshold-based allocation, however, lets content determine the frame
count, so rates vary across utterances and datasets at a fixed setting
\[14, 15\]. For example, one released threshold-based codec ranges from
46.1 to 81.5 events/s under a single threshold across our five
evaluation datasets (Section 5.4.1). Calibrating the threshold on
representative data can match an average rate, but it neither fixes the
count for an individual utterance nor preserves that average when the
data distribution shifts. Sequence length, and with it downstream
modeling cost, then cannot be determined from audio duration alone. An
explicit event budget removes this dependence: placement adapts to
content, while utterance duration determines the sequence length.

Prescribed-count segmentation has been explored in codecs and speech
representation methods \[11, 12, 16\]. We build on this principle with a
codec that uses a deterministic firing signal to allocate events, learns
duration-aware representations of those events, and decodes them
causally.

RubatoCodec separates the number of events from their placement. A
retention ratio $`R`$ fixes the number of events for $`T`$ latent frames
at $`K=\operatorname{round}(RT)`$ (Section 3.3.2), and Adaptive Firing
places them by cumulative allocation of a signal derived from latent
dissimilarity and waveform spectral flux, without a learned boundary
predictor. The default $`R=0.25`$ yields 12.5 events/s on average from a
50-Hz latent grid, with variable event durations. The name reflects the
musical term rubato: local timing flexes while the overall tempo is
preserved.

RubatoCodec builds on X-Codec-2 \[7\], retaining its acoustic/semantic
encoder and spectral synthesis backbone while introducing adaptive
aggregation and causal decoding. Each event carries discrete codes and
an explicit duration, allowing a downstream model to predict and decode
completed events incrementally. We evaluate the design through
controlled codec comparisons, multilingual reconstruction, and
downstream TTS training.

Our contributions are:

1.  1.  Budgeted content-adaptive speech tokenization. Adaptive Firing
        combines an exact utterance-level event budget with cumulative
        allocation driven by latent dissimilarity and waveform spectral
        flux. An onset-anchored aggregator combines frame features and
        duration within each event to construct its quantizer input. Event
        boundaries require neither a learned boundary predictor nor
        rate-penalty optimization.
2.  2.  Controlled analysis of adaptive allocation. Matched comparisons show
        reconstruction gains over fixed-rate downsampling at low event
        rates, and a placement-only comparison indicates that
        content-adaptive boundaries contribute to these gains. We also
        characterize how event rate and quantizer depth trade off at matched
        bitrates.
3.  3.  A code–duration interface for streaming generation. Each event
        carries semantically supervised codes and an explicit duration,
        which a causal decoder renders as they become available. Large-scale
        multilingual evaluation shows competitive reconstruction quality,
        including the best intelligibility preservation among the compared
        codecs on the evaluated English and Japanese benchmarks at
        comparable bitrates. In matched TTS training, the representation
        yields lower WER and higher UTMOS and UTMOSv2 scores with comparable
        speaker similarity, and the generator learns the codec’s
        utterance-level event budget without an explicit rate constraint.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Neural speech codecs for language modeling

SoundStream and EnCodec combine residual vector quantization with
adversarial waveform reconstruction \[1, 2\]; DAC improves the
quantization and reconstruction components \[3\]. Quantizer depth
controls the information carried per temporal position. BigCodec instead
studies high-capacity single-codebook representations at low bitrates
\[17\].

Semantic supervision encourages codec tokens to retain linguistic
content. SpeechTokenizer supervises the first RVQ layer with
self-supervised features \[18\], while Mimi combines a semantically
distilled VQ with a parallel acoustic RVQ \[6\]. X-Codec-2 fuses an
acoustic encoder with frozen w2v-BERT 2.0 features and uses semantic
reconstruction supervision \[7\]. RubatoCodec builds on this
architecture, including its Vocos-style spectral decoder \[19\], and
adopts the synthesis upsampling approach of Inworld TTS \[20\].

Low-frame-rate tokenizers reduce the temporal sequence length presented
to a generative model. Mimi and the publicly released
Qwen3-TTS-Tokenizer-12Hz operate at 12.5 Hz with semantic supervision
\[6, 8\], while MOSS-Audio-Tokenizer learns a low-rate representation
through jointly optimized reconstruction, adversarial, and audio-to-text
objectives without an external semantic distillation teacher \[9\].

### 2.2 Variable-frame-rate speech coding

Early variable-rate discrete representations combine slow autoencoders
with run-length coding \[10\]. Recent codecs adapt temporal resolution
through hierarchy selection, merging, or boundary selection. TFC uses
waveform-derived temporal entropy and utterance-level granularity ratios
\[11\]; VARSTok clusters contiguous latent frames and encodes duration
in token identity \[13\]; FlexiCodec merges frames using frozen
SenseVoice features and adjusts rate through its merge threshold \[14\].
RubatoCodec constructs its firing signal from fused-latent dissimilarity
and waveform spectral flux.

CodecSlime constrains segment count through dynamic programming over
feature distortion, adapting a pretrained fixed-rate codec through its
Melt-and-Cool procedure \[12\]. RubatoCodec instead normalizes its
firing signal to the event budget and places boundaries by cumulative
firing, without searching candidate segmentations. Its trainable codec
components learn from scratch with adaptive allocation active throughout
training.

Elastic Time augments a frozen audio autoencoder with a learned latent
predictor, using greedy or dynamic-programming boundary selection to
retain a target number of anchor frames and reconstruct omitted frames
\[21\]. It adjusts the frame rate of continuous latents at inference;
RubatoCodec instead produces discrete codes with explicit durations for
causal decoding.

DTM-Codec’s Path Length Equalization selects boundaries at successive
thresholds of accumulated latent change \[15\]. Its fixed threshold lets
total change determine the event count; RubatoCodec normalizes bounded
increments to a prescribed count. DyCAST uses character alignment or a
learned boundary predictor and supports transmitted durations,
length-conditioned duration prediction, or free duration prediction
\[22\]. Its total-length constraint concerns decoded durations, not the
number of encoded events.

Adaptive placement, inference-time rate adjustment, and exact event
counts are distinct properties. FlexiCodec and DTM-Codec offer
threshold-based adjustment; TFC, CodecSlime, and RubatoCodec enforce
prescribed counts.

The dynamic tokenizer in The WER Trap normalizes frame weights to a
prescribed token count before soft aggregation and FSQ \[16\]. It
follows Continuous Integrate-and-Fire (CIF), which accumulates learned
weights into feature sums and uses target-length normalization during
training \[23\]. Beyond waveform coding, Once-for-All compression
extends CIF to inference-time rate adjustment \[24\], and SpeechCLIP+
combines CIF with vector quantization and input-length-based targets
\[25\].

RubatoCodec shares cumulative budget allocation but separates boundary
placement from event representation: deterministic statistics set event
boundaries, and an aggregator combines the onset feature, the mean of
the enclosed frame features, and the duration for each event. Table 1
compares VFR codecs in terms of temporal allocation, event-count
control, timing representation, and training procedure.

### 2.3 Duration-aware and streaming speech generation

Variable-duration speech generation must model both content and timing.
Run-length Transformers model compressed event sequences \[10\], while
VARSTok includes duration in its vocabulary \[13\]. FlexiCodec predicts
semantic tokens and frame lengths before flow-matching synthesis \[14\];
DyCAST also evaluates TTS with dynamic tokens and duration decoding
\[22\]. QuarkAudio and FlexiSLM further explore adaptive representations
for generation \[26, 27\].

Streaming synthesis additionally requires a decoding path that can
render available codes incrementally. Mimi and Qwen3-TTS-Tokenizer-12Hz
provide low-rate representations for streaming synthesis \[6, 8\].
MOSS-Audio-Tokenizer uses causal Transformer encoding and decoding
\[9\].

RubatoCodec supplies explicit event durations to a causal waveform
decoder. A downstream model predicts code–duration pairs, which restore
the decoding grid and can be synthesized as they become available.

## 3 RubatoCodec Architecture

RubatoCodec retains X-Codec-2’s acoustic/semantic encoding, feature
fusion, semantic reconstruction pathway, and Transformer-based spectral
synthesis backbone \[7\]. We modify the temporal bottleneck through
Adaptive Firing, interval aggregation, hybrid residual quantization, and
duration-aware grid reconstruction (Figure 1). Causal decoding enables
incremental synthesis. RubatoCodec also uses a factor-three upsampler
inspired by Inworld TTS that raises the synthesis feature rate \[20\].

Figure 1: RubatoCodec architecture. The codec builds on the X-Codec-2
encoder and decoder backbone \[7\] and is shown at the default retention
ratio of 0.25. Latent change and waveform spectral flux guide budgeted
event allocation. The interval aggregator combines each event’s onset
feature with a learned correction based on that feature, the mean of its
frame features, and its duration. Quantized event representations and
durations restore the 50-Hz decoding grid; the Inworld-TTS-inspired
upsampler increases the synthesis feature rate \[20\].

### 3.1 Representation and signal path

The encoder maps 16-kHz audio to $`T`$ feature vectors on a nominal
50-Hz grid, with the aligned 44.1-kHz waveform serving as the
reconstruction target. RubatoCodec partitions this grid into $`K`$
nonempty intervals, each called an event. Event $`i`$ is represented by
$`Q`$ quantizer indices
$`\mathbf{c}_{i}=(c_{i,\allowbreak 0},\ldots,c_{i,Q-1})`$, where $`Q`$
is the number of quantizer layers (Section 3.4), and a duration
$`d_{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}`$ giving the number of 20-ms grid frames it
spans. Let $`p_{i}`$ be the index of the first grid frame of event
$`i`$, with $`p_{0}=0`$:

```math
p_{i}=\sum_{j<i}d_{j},\qquad\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}d_{i}=T. \tag{1}
```

Table 2 summarizes the architecture. The decoder emits $`882T`$ output
samples because $`44100/50=882`$.

### 3.2 Acoustic and semantic encoders

Following X-Codec-2 \[7\], the acoustic encoder uses strided
convolutions with base width 48, residual dilations
$`1,\allowbreak 3,\allowbreak 9`$, and Snake-beta activations to produce
acoustic features $`a_{t}`$. Its total waveform hop size is 320 samples
at 16 kHz, corresponding to a 50-Hz frame rate. A frozen w2v-BERT 2.0
model \[28\] supplies layer-16 features $`b_{t}^{\star}`$ at the same
50-Hz frame rate. A learned convolutional encoder transforms these into
$`b_{t}`$, which is fused with the acoustic features. The frozen
features $`b_{t}^{\star}`$ also serve as the semantic reconstruction
targets in Section 3.4. Writing the aligned streams as
$`a_{t},b_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}`$, their fusion is

```math
h_{t}=W_{\mathrm{prior}}[a_{t};b_{t}]+b_{\mathrm{prior}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2048}. \tag{2}
```

### 3.3 Adaptive Firing

Adaptive Firing uses a firing signal derived from latent dissimilarity
and waveform spectral flux to partition the 50-Hz latent sequence into
$`K`$ events. A separate aggregator combines the frame features within
each event into one vector; unlike CIF \[23\], the firing signal
determines only the boundaries and does not weight the pooled features.

#### 3.3.1 Firing signal

The firing signal sums two mean-normalized components, multi-scale
latent dissimilarity and log-mel spectral flux:

```math
s_{t}=s_{t}^{\mathrm{latent}}+\mathcal{N}(f)_{t}. \tag{3}
```

Here, $`\mathcal{N}`$ denotes sequence-wise mean normalization,
$`s_{t}^{\mathrm{latent}}`$ is the normalized multi-scale
latent-dissimilarity component, and $`f_{t}`$ is the log-mel spectral
flux. Each is defined below.

Normalization. Define sequence-wise mean normalization for a nonnegative
scalar sequence $`v`$ as

```math
\mathcal{N}(v)_{t}=\frac{v_{t}}{\max(\epsilon,T^{-1}\sum_{u}v_{u})},\qquad\epsilon=10^{-8}. \tag{4}
```

Latent dissimilarity. The multi-scale latent-dissimilarity component
uses offset pairs
$`\mathcal{O}=\{(1,\allowbreak 0),(1,\allowbreak 1),(2,\allowbreak 1)\}`$:

```math
\displaystyle\ell_{t}^{a,b} \displaystyle=\max\{0,1-\cos(h_{t-a},h_{t+b})\}, \tag{5}
```

```math
\displaystyle s_{t}^{\mathrm{latent}} \displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{N}(\ell^{a,b})\right)_{t}.
```

Unavailable edge comparisons are set to zero. This latent-dissimilarity
component follows the use of feature dissimilarity for dynamic chunking
\[29\], combining comparisons at multiple temporal offsets.

Spectral flux. The waveform component is log-mel spectral flux. Let
$`M_{m,t}`$ denote the mel-spectrogram magnitude at mel bin $`m`$ and
frame $`t`$, computed with 80 mel bins, a 1,024-point STFT, and a
320-sample hop at 16 kHz. The flux is

```math
f_{t}=\frac{1}{80}\sum_{m=1}^{80}\left|\log(M_{m,t}+10^{-5})-\log(M_{m,t-1}+10^{-5})\right|. \tag{6}
```

Flux is computed using a centered STFT with reflection padding. Its
first value and final two values are set to zero to suppress boundary
artifacts, after which the sequence is truncated or zero-padded on the
right to match the latent-grid length.

Normalization makes the combination depend on the relative temporal
distributions of its components rather than their raw scales. The
waveform component also provides an informative allocation signal early
in training, before the acoustic encoder has learned meaningful
features.

#### 3.3.2 Budget-constrained event allocation

For a target retention ratio $`0<R\leq 1`$, the integer event budget is

```math
K=\max\{1,\min(T,\operatorname{round}(RT))\}. \tag{7}
```

Here, $`\operatorname{round}(\cdot)`$ rounds half to even. The first
frame always fires and consumes one unit of this budget, leaving
$`L=T-1`$ frames to receive $`M=K-1`$ further event starts. For $`M>0`$,
distribute the remaining budget over these $`L`$ frames using bounded
increments

```math
\delta_{t}=\min(\alpha s_{t},1),\qquad\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\delta_{t}=M. \tag{8}
```

We find $`\alpha`$ by bisection, after expanding the upper bracket until
the constraint is met or a limit is reached.

For cumulative increments $`C_{t}=\sum_{u=1}^{t}\delta_{u}`$,
provisional event starts are the first crossings of thresholds
$`j-\tfrac{1}{2}`$, for $`j=1,\ldots,M`$. The last threshold lies half a
unit below $`M`$, providing a margin against endpoint rounding error.
These cumulative threshold crossings follow the integrate-and-fire
principle used in CIF \[23\]; here, bounded increments and the discrete
position correction below enforce the prescribed number of distinct
event starts.

Let $`q_{j}`$, for $`j=0,\ldots,M-1`$, be the zero-based position of the
$`(j+1)`$-th threshold crossing among these $`L`$ frames. If a threshold
is not reached, its position is set to $`L`$. A monotone correction
handles coincident positions and unreachable thresholds:

```math
u_{j}=j+\max_{0\leq\ell\leq j}(q_{\ell}-\ell),\qquad v_{j}=\min(u_{j},L-M+j),\qquad p_{j+1}=1+v_{j}. \tag{9}
```

The running maximum and capacity bound in (9) produce strictly
increasing positions satisfying $`0\leq v_{j}\leq L-M+j<L`$. Together
with $`p_{0}=0`$, these give exactly $`K`$ distinct event starts. For
$`K=1`$, the single event covers the whole grid. Figure 2 illustrates
the construction on a short synthetic firing signal.

Two cases need separate handling. When the firing signal over these
$`L`$ frames is negligible, we use uniform increments
$`\delta_{t}=M/L`$. When the equality in (8) is infeasible, we retain
the increments returned by the bounded search and apply the same
position correction in (9), preserving exactly $`K`$ nonempty events.

Figure 2: Cumulative allocation for a synthetic firing signal. The
example uses $`T=20`$, $`R=0.25`$, and $`K=5`$. (a) Firing signal. (b)
Cumulative bounded increments cross thresholds 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, and 3.5 at
frames 3, 7, 12, and 17. (c) Together with the fixed start at frame 0,
these crossings define five events spanning 3, 4, 5, 5, and 3 grid
frames, respectively. All positions are computed from the displayed
signal using equations (8–9); no position correction is needed in this
example.

The budget is defined over the $`T`$ encoder frames, corresponding to a
represented duration of $`T/50`$ seconds after any boundary padding. The
utterance-average event rate on this grid is $`50K/T`$. Away from the
minimum-one-event clamp,

```math
\left|\frac{50K}{T}-50R\right|\leq\frac{25}{T}\ \text{events/s}. \tag{10}
```

When $`RT`$ is integral, the grid-average event rate is exactly $`50R`$.
Allocation costs $`O(T)`$ per bisection iteration, plus a linear-time
threshold search and position correction.

Event allocation is deterministic and non-differentiable. Reconstruction
and representation losses train the features and interval aggregator
through the selected event representations. Boundaries therefore evolve
during training as the features change, while the event count is
enforced directly without a rate loss. The allocation constrains the
total event count without imposing a maximum event duration. Sustained
high-change regions can receive multiple events on different grid
frames.

#### 3.3.3 Interval aggregation

Define $`p_{K}=T`$, $`d_{i}=p_{i+1}-p_{i}`$, and the mean of the latent
frame features within event $`i`$:

```math
\bar{h}_{i}=\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{t=p_{i}}^{p_{i+1}-1}h_{t}. \tag{11}
```

The quantizer input is the onset feature plus a learned correction:

```math
g_{i}=h_{p_{i}}+\operatorname{MLP}\!\left([h_{p_{i}};\bar{h}_{i};E_{d}(\min(d_{i},64))]\right). \tag{12}
```

The residual connection from the onset feature preserves information at
the event start; the learned correction incorporates the mean and
duration. The learned duration embedding $`E_{d}`$ is indexed up to 64,
without capping the actual event duration.

### 3.4 Hybrid residual quantization and semantic supervision

#### 3.4.1 Residual quantization

RubatoCodec uses $`Q=16`$ quantizer layers. Layer zero is finite scalar
quantization with levels
$`[8,\allowbreak 8,\allowbreak 8,\allowbreak 8]`$, yielding
$`8^{4}=4096`$ indices \[30\]. Layers 1–15 use DAC-style vector
quantization with a factorized 8-dimensional codebook lookup, normalized
lookup vectors, and 4,096 entries per layer \[3\]. Each layer therefore
requires 12 fixed-width index bits.

Each layer quantizes the residual left by the preceding layers. With
$`r_{i,\allowbreak 0}=g_{i}`$, the reconstructed event vector at depth
$`D`$ is

```math
q_{i,j}=Q_{j}(r_{i,j}),\quad r_{i,j+1}=r_{i,j}-q_{i,j},\quad\hat{g}_{i}^{(D)}=\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}q_{i,j}. \tag{13}
```

Here, $`Q_{j}`$ denotes the quantizer at layer $`j`$. Using the first
$`D`$ layers adjusts the number of code indices per event while
retaining the same event boundaries and durations.

#### 3.4.2 Semantic distillation

Semantic supervision encourages the first layer to preserve linguistic
information for downstream modeling. We adapt X-Codec-2’s semantic
reconstruction objective \[7\], sharing the motivation of Mimi \[6\] but
using a serial residual chain rather than parallel semantic and acoustic
quantizers.

A training-only semantic head predicts frozen w2v-BERT 2.0 features from
the first-layer reconstruction. Its input uses the same sparse 50-Hz
grid composition and offset embeddings as the waveform decoder
(Section 3.5). Durations determine the grid placement; no separate
duration embedding is added to the head. The semantic head consists of a
linear projection from 2,048 to 1,024 dimensions followed by a residual
convolutional network with four layers of kernel size three. Symmetric
padding gives the head access to four grid frames on either side,
including neighboring events within that range.

The head’s output $`\hat{b}_{t}`$ is trained to match the frozen teacher
features $`b_{t}^{\star}`$ at event starts using a dimension-normalized
masked mean squared error:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sem}}=\frac{\sum_{t}m_{t}\|\hat{b}_{t}-b_{t}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}}{D_{s}\sum_{t}m_{t}},\qquad m_{t}=\mathbf{1}[t\in\{p_{i}\}]. \tag{14}
```

Here, $`D_{s}`$ is the feature dimension. The mask $`m_{t}`$ assigns one
teacher target at each event’s onset, where its first-layer
representation enters the grid, leaving the residual layers to encode
additional waveform detail.

### 3.5 Causal grid decoding

The decoder places each dequantized event vector at its starting grid
position and uses a learned empty vector between starts. Let $`E_{r}`$
denote the learned offset embedding. With offset $`r=t-p_{i}`$ inside
event $`i`$,

```math
z_{t}=\begin{cases}\hat{g}_{i}^{(D)}+E_{r}(0),&r=0,\\
e_{\mathrm{empty}}+E_{r}(\min(r,64)),&r>0.\end{cases} \tag{15}
```

Duration $`d_{i}`$ sets the number of grid frames, and the offset
embedding identifies their positions within the event. The causal
decoder can attend to the onset feature to synthesize the audio for that
event. Appendix A.3 compares this sparse input with repeating the event
vector and supports the sparse design for signal fidelity.

The decoder retains the X-Codec-2 Transformer backbone \[7\], with
causal attention and convolutional dependencies. A projection maps the
2,048-dimensional grid to this 1,024-dimensional backbone, which uses a
causal embedding convolution, 12 Transformer layers, rotary positions,
and 16 attention heads. A factor-three transposed-convolution upsampler,
inspired by the super-resolution module in Inworld TTS \[20\], produces
150-Hz synthesis features. A Vocos-style spectral head predicts
magnitude and phase, followed by forward-window ISTFT with a hop size of
294 samples for 44.1-kHz output \[19\]. Convolutions are left-padded,
transposed convolutions are right-trimmed, and attention excludes future
frames, so synthesis depends only on the current and preceding events.

The ISTFT places each 1,176-sample synthesis window at the current
frame’s hop position and extends it forward in time. Each frame
finalizes the next 294 samples, carrying waveform and
window-normalization overlap buffers into subsequent frames. With three
synthesis frames per grid step, an event of duration $`d_{i}`$ accounts
for $`882d_{i}`$ output samples. At startup, normalization assumes
preceding silent windows, yielding a 20-ms fade-in; at termination, the
remaining window overhang is discarded rather than emitted as additional
audio.

During inference, the decoder periodically discards its cached states
and rebuilds them from a window of recent events beginning at an event
onset, which limits long-utterance extrapolation. At each such history
recomputation, a short crossfade blends the audio decoded before and
after the recomputation. Streaming decoding processes incoming events
incrementally, reusing decoder states between history recomputations and
briefly buffering audio for the crossfade.

### 3.6 Model size and bitrate

RubatoCodec contains approximately 246.1M codec parameters: 72.3M in the
encoding path, 172.7M in the decoding path, and 1.1M in the quantizer.
The additional frozen w2v-BERT 2.0 encoder has approximately 580M
parameters, bringing the complete model to approximately 826M.
Token-to-waveform synthesis uses the decoding path and quantizer without
the encoding components.

Each event contains $`D`$ code indices of 12 bits each. For $`K`$ events
over a represented duration of $`\tau=T/50`$ seconds, the content
bitrate is

```math
B_{\mathrm{content}}=\frac{12DK}{\tau}. \tag{16}
```

At 12.5 events per second, depths 1, 4, 8, and 16 correspond to content
bitrates of 0.15, 0.60, 1.20, and 2.40 kbit/s, respectively. Timing
bitrate is estimated from the empirical entropy $`\hat{H}(d)`$ of event
durations, pooled within each evaluation dataset (Section 5.1.2). The
estimated total bitrate is
$`B_{\mathrm{total}}=B_{\mathrm{content}}+(K/\tau)\hat{H}(d)`$.

## 4 Training Data and Optimization

We train adaptive and fixed-rate architectures on a shared English
subset at 6.25, 12.5, and 25 events/s, and adaptive and uniform-stride
models at 12.5 events/s for the placement comparison. We then train a
multilingual RubatoCodec on a larger speech mixture for comparison with
external codecs.

### 4.1 Objectives and phases

Both settings combine reconstruction, semantic supervision,
quantization, adversarial, and perceptual objectives:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{G}=15\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}+5\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sem}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VQ}}+\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{phase}\geq 2}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}})+\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{phase}=3}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{perc}}. \tag{17}
```

Multi-resolution mel reconstruction. Following DAC \[3\],
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mel}}`$ sums mean absolute log-mel differences at
44.1 kHz over eight resolutions: window lengths
$`32,\allowbreak 64,\allowbreak 128,\allowbreak 256,\allowbreak 512,\allowbreak 1024,\allowbreak 2048,\allowbreak 4096`$,
quarter-window hops, and corresponding mel-bin counts
$`5,\allowbreak 10,\allowbreak 20,\allowbreak 40,\allowbreak 80,\allowbreak 160,\allowbreak 320,\allowbreak 640`$.
Magnitudes are clamped to $`10^{-5}`$ before the base-10 logarithm.

Semantic reconstruction. Equation (14) supervises the first layer with
frozen w2v-BERT 2.0 layer-16 features. RubatoCodec evaluates the loss at
event starts. For fixed-rate controls, the 50-Hz teacher features are
averaged over nonoverlapping groups of 8, 4, or 2 frames to match the
6.25-, 12.5-, or 25-Hz latent grid, respectively. These averaged
features serve both as inputs to the semantic encoder for feature fusion
and as targets for the semantic loss, which is evaluated at every latent
position.

Quantization. $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VQ}}`$ sums squared-error
commitment and codebook losses over residual VQ layers \[3\], with
weights 0.25 and 1.0 and stop-gradients on the code vector and encoder
representation, respectively. FSQ has neither loss \[30\].

Adversarial training and feature matching. We use HiFi-GAN’s
multi-period discriminator with periods
$`2,\allowbreak 3,\allowbreak 5,\allowbreak 7,\allowbreak 11,\allowbreak 17,\allowbreak 23,\allowbreak 37`$
\[31\] and a UnivNet-style multi-resolution STFT-magnitude discriminator
\[32\] with FFT/window sizes
$`216,\allowbreak 348,\allowbreak 568,\allowbreak 920,\allowbreak 1494,\allowbreak 2414,\allowbreak 3908,\allowbreak 6328`$
and half-window hops. Least-squares GAN losses use target labels one for
real audio and zero for reconstructed audio, while the generator targets
one \[31\]. $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}`$ sums mean absolute differences
of intermediate activations across both discriminator families, with
detached target features. Discriminator parameters remain fixed during
generator updates.

Perceptual reconstruction. Following Llasa’s late-stage waveform
supervision \[7\], $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{perc}}`$ compares frozen
speech-model features of target and reconstructed audio resampled to 16
kHz. We use wav2vec2-XLS-R-300M \[33\], averaging mean squared feature
error over layers 10–21. Gradients propagate through the frozen
extractor to the codec.

Training phases. The first 10% of updates use mel, semantic, and
quantization losses; the next 75% add adversarial and feature-matching
losses; the final 15% also use perceptual loss. Trainable codec
components start from scratch, while both pretrained feature extractors
remain frozen.

### 4.2 Training for architecture comparisons

Fixed-rate controls downsample before quantization to produce uniformly

aggregates features within variable-duration events, restoring their

#### 5.1.2 Metrics

Reconstruction-quality metrics use audio resampled to 16 kHz.

Event rate and bitrate. We compute event rate and bitrate per utterance
using the duration represented by the codec’s temporal grid, and report
their arithmetic means within each dataset. Event rate is the number of
encoded events per second; for fixed-rate codecs, each frame counts as
one event. Bitrate includes code-index and timing costs (Section 3.6).
Explicit timing symbols are assigned their dataset-pooled empirical
entropy per symbol; timing embedded in token indices is included in the
code-index cost.

Intelligibility. We report dWER for English and dCER for Japanese and
Mandarin Chinese, comparing the ASR transcript of reconstructed speech
against that of the original waveform. English and Japanese speech are
transcribed with Whisper-large-v3 \[47\], and Mandarin with
Paraformer-zh \[48\]. The same normalization is applied to both
transcripts: Whisper’s English normalization for English, Whisper’s
basic normalization with spaces removed for Japanese, and the
seed-tts-eval procedure for Mandarin \[46\]. Lower dWER and dCER
indicate better preservation of the original recording’s ASR output.

Speech quality. We measure PESQ-WB \[49\] and STOI \[50\] against the
original waveform, together with the reference-free predictors UTMOSv2
\[51\] and DNSMOS \[52\]. The main tables use the overall-quality output
of DNSMOS. Higher values are better for these metrics. For PESQ and
STOI, the resampled waveforms are truncated to their common length from
the start, without explicit delay correction or temporal warping.

Speaker preservation. Speaker similarity (SIM) is the cosine similarity
between embeddings of the original and reconstructed utterances,
extracted with a fine-tuned WavLM-Large speaker verification model
\[53\]. Higher values indicate better preservation of speaker
characteristics.

For codec reconstruction, dWER and dCER are micro-averaged within each
dataset: total edit counts are divided by total reference words or
characters. Other reconstruction-quality scores are averaged over
utterances.

## 6 Duration-Aware Text-to-Speech Modeling

Figure 11: MOSS-TTS adapted to RubatoCodec, shown with eight codebooks.
The temporal Transformer predicts each event from the conditioning
prefix and the completed preceding events; the local Transformer then
predicts continuation or stop, the codebook tokens, and finally the
duration. Completed code–duration pairs can be passed to the causal
RubatoCodec decoder for streaming synthesis.

### 6.1 Generative interface

We adapt the MOSS-TTS-Local-Transformer v1.5 implementation \[56\] to
RubatoCodec (Figure 11), retaining its temporal–local autoregressive
structure, reference-and-text input format, and continuation/stop
prediction. We add duration embeddings to the temporal input and predict
each event’s duration at the final local position, conditioned on all
codebook tokens for that event. We use Qwen3-0.6B \[57\] in place of the
Qwen3-4B temporal backbone and a four-layer local Transformer.

Reference speech and target text form the conditioning prefix, with the
reference preceding the text. At text positions, only the text-token
embedding contributes; at speech-event positions, the audio-slot token
embedding is summed with the codebook and duration embeddings. Each
completed event is fed back to the temporal Transformer to predict the
next event. The first event is predicted from the state at the
audio-start token following the conditioning prefix.

Each event is represented by $`e_{i}=(\mathbf{c}_{i},d_{i})`$. Given
text $`y`$, optional reference $`r`$, and preceding events $`e_{<i}`$,
the first local state predicts the continuation probability
$`p(a_{i}=1\mid y,r,e_{<i})`$, where $`a_{i}=1`$ denotes continuation
and $`a_{i}=0`$ denotes stop. Conditional on continuation, the next
event follows

```math
\displaystyle p(\mathbf{c}_{i},d_{i}\mid y,r,e_{<i},a_{i}=1) \displaystyle=\left[\prod_{j=0}^{D-1}p(c_{i,j}\mid y,r,e_{<i},a_{i}=1,c_{i,<j})\right] \tag{18}
```

```math
\displaystyle\times p(d_{i}\mid y,r,e_{<i},a_{i}=1,\mathbf{c}_{i}).
```

During training, speech events are labeled as continuation and the
following end token as stop; codebook and duration losses apply only to
speech events. During inference, generation terminates when stop is
sampled.

We also compared alternative positions for duration prediction in a
small ablation study; details are provided in Appendix B.

Once the local Transformer has generated an event’s codebook tokens and
duration, the causal codec decoder (Section 3.5) incrementally
synthesizes the corresponding $`d_{i}/50`$ seconds of audio. Decoder
state is reused across events, with brief buffering at history
recomputations (Section 3.5).

## 7 Discussion

### 7.1 Adaptive allocation under an explicit budget

An exact event budget leaves useful freedom to distribute temporal
resolution within an utterance. This freedom matters most at lower event
rates: the gains over fixed-rate downsampling appear at 6.25 and 12.5 Hz
and narrow at 25 Hz (Section 5.2.1). Those comparisons change placement,
interval aggregation, and decoding grid together, and the adaptive codec
has lower speaker similarity there. With aggregation and grid held
fixed, however, adaptive placement raises speaker similarity relative to
uniform stride (Section 5.2.2). Although the two comparisons use
different training data and lengths, this suggests that the SIM gap in
Section 5.2.1 stems from interval aggregation and grid design rather
than from placement itself.

The uniform-stride comparison (Section 5.2.2) supports content-dependent
event placement with aggregation and decoding grid held fixed. The
firing-rule comparison (Appendix A.4) separately favors cumulative
allocation over the tested level-thresholding rule. Cumulative
allocation produces approximately uniform spacing under a constant
firing signal and adjusts that spacing with the cumulative bounded
increments. Broad temporal coverage combined with content-dependent
placement is one possible explanation for its performance, but these
experiments do not isolate that mechanism.

The firing analysis shows how the budget is used: events longer than 300
ms predominantly cover low-energy regions across all five datasets,
leaving more events for the remaining speech (Section 5.4.2). Budgeted
and threshold-based allocation also make different trade-offs. A
prescribed count gives predictable sequence lengths without
dataset-specific calibration, but equally long utterances receive the
same budget regardless of acoustic complexity. Threshold-based methods
can instead spend more events on difficult utterances.

### 7.2 Temporal resolution and quantizer depth for speech generation

Temporal resolution and quantizer depth are not interchangeable ways of
spending a bitrate budget. In the evaluated configurations, additional
residual layers do not fully compensate for coarser segmentation: 12.5
Hz approaches 25 Hz near 1.2 kbit/s, but 6.25 Hz falls clearly behind,
and 25 Hz regains a fidelity advantage near 2.4 kbit/s (Section 5.2.3).

For generation, these choices also distribute computation differently.
Hierarchical models such as Moshi, Qwen3-TTS, and MOSS-TTS predict
codebook tokens with a smaller within-event module \[6, 8, 56\].
Increasing depth adds predictions there, whereas increasing event rate
adds steps to the larger temporal backbone. The reconstruction results
therefore motivate 12.5 Hz as a practical compromise between quality and
sequence length, without establishing it as optimal. Because
RubatoCodec’s waveform decoder still operates on a 50-Hz grid, the
shorter sequence reduces downstream modeling cost but not
waveform-decoder computation.

The inference-time ratio experiment (Appendix A.5) shows asymmetric
tolerance to event rates outside the training setting: raising the rate
improves reconstruction at additional bitrate, whereas aggressive
reduction degrades it sharply even at greater quantizer depth. The model
thus tolerates higher, but not lower, inference rates.

### 7.3 From encoded durations to generated timing

The matched TTS comparison shows that codes and durations can be
generated jointly while improving intelligibility and predicted quality
over the fixed-rate codec (Section 6.2). Each completed event can be
passed directly to the causal waveform decoder, so the same
representation serves both temporal compression and incremental
synthesis.

The generator also learns the codec’s utterance-level event budget.
Generated event counts match the codec’s budget rule for 88.3% of
utterances and fall within one event for 99.9% (Section 6.3), so the
generator offsets long events with short ones within each utterance.
Generated sequence length therefore remains predictable, although no
rate constraint is imposed during generation. Long events also
predominantly correspond to low-energy regions in both encoded speech
and generated audio under the same measurement criterion (Sections 5.4.2
and 6.3), indicating that the generator reproduces the codec’s use of
long events for near-silence. The concentration of long events at
utterance boundaries is consistent with learning leading and trailing
silence from HiFiTTS-2, although that training-data correspondence has
not been measured directly.

Pause placement, however, is not always natural: informal listening
revealed an unnatural within-phrase pause associated with a long event
(Section 6.3). Efficiently representing silence and placing pauses
naturally are therefore distinct modeling concerns.

### 7.4 Reconstruction quality and multilingual performance

On the English benchmarks, RubatoCodec combines strong intelligibility
preservation with high predicted-quality scores, while the strongest
comparators retain higher signal fidelity and speaker similarity
(Section 5.3). On the Japanese benchmarks, its intelligibility advantage
extends to both read and emotional speech, whereas predicted-quality
rankings depend on the dataset. In Mandarin, intelligibility
preservation remains competitive, although the strongest alternatives
achieve lower dCER. These patterns distinguish preservation of
linguistic content, speaker characteristics, and waveform detail;
improvements in one need not imply improvements in the others.
RubatoCodec’s UTMOSv2 scores exceed those of the original recordings on
LibriSpeech, indicating that higher predicted quality should not be
interpreted as greater fidelity to the source. The 44.1-kHz output rate
is a synthesis capability, not evidence of full-band source recovery.
Encoding uses 16-kHz input, so higher frequencies must be inferred.

The English, Japanese, and Mandarin results establish reconstruction
across languages with a shared model and allocation rule. Combined with
the cross-dataset rate analysis (Section 5.4.1), they demonstrate both
predictable encoded sequence lengths and content preservation across the
evaluated languages.

### 7.5 Scope and limitations

The reported architecture comparisons use one trained model per
configuration, and supporting architectural ablations have narrower
coverage. In particular, firing-signal selection uses an early
single-FSQ, low-bitrate design and a smaller training budget; its
ranking may change with the current residual quantizer and training
scale (Appendix A.1). The placement comparison uses one seed and English
read speech, so its robustness across training runs, languages, and
recording conditions remains untested. Firing statistics characterize
duration and energy associations, not phonetic alignment or optimal
allocation, and do not separate the quality contribution of allocation
within speech from that of allocation to low-energy regions.

External codecs differ in training resources and operating points.
Reporting realized rates and timing costs makes these differences
visible but does not control them. All evaluated languages occur in
RubatoCodec’s training mixture, so the multilingual results do not
establish unseen-language transfer or language robustness attributable
solely to Adaptive Firing. Dataset differences also confound language,
speakers, and recording conditions.

TTS evidence covers one English audiobook corpus and codecs from the
controlled comparison (Section 4.2). The comparison evaluates the
complete representation and does not isolate the TTS contribution of
adaptive placement. It also does not establish the quality attainable
with the multilingual codec, broader data, or a larger generator. Long
events are more frequent in TTS than in the reconstruction evaluation,
but the corpora differ. Human evaluation remains necessary to assess
reconstruction and TTS preferences and pause naturalness.

## 8 Future Work

For the codec, the priority is signal fidelity at low event rates: how
far improved acoustic representations and reconstruction objectives can
close PESQ/STOI gaps without longer sequences, and when finer temporal
resolution becomes necessary. Training with multiple retention ratios
could broaden the inference-time tolerance observed in Appendix A.5,
following adjustable-rate coding and sequence compression \[14, 24\].
Online encoding would further require a causal acoustic–semantic path
and a windowed or running budget in place of the utterance-level
guarantee, trading buffering latency against placement quality and rate
control.

For generation, larger-scale TTS with the multilingual codec, broader
data, and larger backbones would test whether reconstruction
improvements carry over, and explicit durations could support
utterance-length and pause control. Finally, applying Adaptive Firing to
other codec backbones and to music or environmental sound would test its
generality.

## 9 Conclusion

We introduced RubatoCodec, a speech codec that combines content-adaptive
event placement, an exact utterance-level event budget, and streaming
waveform decoding. By representing speech as discrete codes paired with
durations, it allows temporal resolution to follow speech content while
keeping sequence length predictable.

Controlled experiments support the value of adaptive allocation for
reconstruction at low event rates. Multilingual evaluation demonstrates
competitive reconstruction quality, with the best intelligibility
preservation among the compared codecs on the evaluated English and
Japanese benchmarks at comparable bitrates. Signal fidelity remains an
area for improvement relative to the strongest alternatives. In matched
text-to-speech experiments, the adaptive representation improves
intelligibility and predicted speech quality with comparable speaker
similarity. The generator also learns the codec’s utterance-level event
budget without an explicit rate constraint.

These results support budgeted adaptive speech tokenization as a
practical representation for both reconstruction and speech generation.
Combining content-dependent timing with predictable sequence length
provides a basis for compact speech modeling and streaming synthesis.

## Appendix A Supporting Codec Ablations

### A.1 Firing-signal selection

We conducted this preliminary study before introducing residual vector
quantization, using a single FSQ codebook to compare candidate firing
signals (Table A1). Each model was trained for 100,000 steps and
evaluated on 200 utterances from a HiFiTTS-2 validation split
constructed by holding out speakers from the training subset. The scores
serve primarily to compare signal choices within this early
configuration; their absolute values do not characterize the final codec
and are not directly comparable with the LibriSpeech test-clean results
in Section 5.2.1.

| Firing signal                                                | PESQ-WB $`\uparrow`$ | STOI $`\uparrow`$ | dWER (%) $`\downarrow`$ | SIM $`\uparrow`$ |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------- | ----------------- | ----------------------- | ---------------- |
| Adjacent latent dissimilarity                                | 1.262                | 0.787             | 3.30                    | 0.397            |
| Multi-scale latent dissimilarity                             | 1.307                | 0.804             | 2.81                    | 0.458            |
| Waveform flux                                                | 1.327                | 0.810             | 2.20                    | 0.447            |
| Adjacent latent dissimilarity + flux                         | 1.324                | 0.808             | 2.53                    | 0.426            |
| Multi-scale latent dissimilarity + flux                      | 1.345                | 0.810             | 2.31                    | 0.454            |
| Learned difficulty                                           | 1.335                | 0.811             | 2.31                    | 0.412            |
| Learned difficulty + flux                                    | 1.336                | 0.813             | 1.36                    | 0.418            |
| Learned difficulty + multi-scale latent dissimilarity        | 1.330                | 0.810             | 1.86                    | 0.412            |
| Learned difficulty + multi-scale latent dissimilarity + flux | 1.319                | 0.811             | 2.04                    | 0.404            |

Table A1: Firing-signal ablations with a single FSQ codebook. Bold marks
the best score in each column.

Latent dissimilarity and flux. Multi-scale latent dissimilarity and
waveform flux are the two components described in Section 3.3.1: the
latent-dissimilarity component in Equation (5) and log-mel spectral flux
in Equation (6). Adjacent latent dissimilarity is the single-offset
variant of Equation (5), using only $`(a,b)=(1,\allowbreak 0)`$, so it
compares each latent frame with its immediate predecessor. The
multi-scale-plus-flux row uses the firing signal defined in
Equation (3).

Learned difficulty. A separate convolutional network takes the current
and preceding latent frames and predicts a nonnegative scalar
reconstruction difficulty. During training, its target is the decoder’s
framewise absolute log-mel reconstruction error, averaged over mel bins
and normalized by its utterance mean. The network learns this target
with a mean-squared regression loss; during encoding, its prediction
supplies the firing signal without requiring waveform reconstruction. In
mixed-signal variants, each component is normalized to unit utterance
mean before summation.

We prioritize firing signals that require no additional trainable
predictor. Among these candidates, multi-scale latent dissimilarity
combined with spectral flux achieves the highest PESQ-WB, with STOI and
speaker similarity comparable to flux alone, although dWER is slightly
higher. Learned difficulty combined with flux yields lower dWER but
requires an additional predictor and has lower speaker similarity. We
therefore adopt the combined firing signal as a balance between
architectural simplicity and reconstruction quality in this exploratory
comparison.

### A.4 Firing decision rules

This ablation compares cumulative allocation with level thresholding of
the firing signal. Both models use the architecture in Section 3,
including the firing signal defined in Equation (3), interval
aggregation, hybrid residual quantizer, sparse decoding grid, and fully
causal waveform decoder. Each model is trained from scratch for 100,000
steps on the approximately 5,000-hour HiFiTTS-2 subset using the
settings in Section 4.2, with retention ratio $`R=0.25`$. Evaluation
uses LibriSpeech test-clean.

Level thresholding selects frames using an utterance-specific threshold
at the signal’s $`(1-R)`$-quantile, with the initial frame always
retained. Training uses a sigmoid gate with a straight-through
estimator, while inference uses hard decisions. Cumulative allocation
uses the bounded cumulative allocation in Section 3.3.2 and enforces the
prescribed event count without differentiating through boundary
selection. The two rules differ in both event-count enforcement and the
training gradient through boundary selection, so this comparison
evaluates the complete firing rules rather than isolating placement
alone.

| Cumulative allocation | 8     | 3.448                | 3.314               | 2.379                | 0.917             | 0.80                    | 0.757            |

Cumulative allocation achieves higher PESQ-WB, STOI, speaker similarity,

which difference between the rules causes the improvement.

## Appendix B Duration Prediction Order

This ablation compares four ways to predict event duration alongside
codebook tokens. The independent-head variant predicts continuation,
duration, and the first codebook token through three separate heads at
the first local Transformer position; the remaining codes are predicted
autoregressively without conditioning on the current duration. The other
variants insert duration into the local autoregressive sequence before
all codes, after the first (semantically supervised) code, or after all
codes (Table B1). Duration is conditioned on preceding codes, and
subsequent codes are conditioned on duration. The duration-last variant
corresponds to Equation (18).

All four models share a Qwen3-0.6B temporal backbone \[57\], a
single-layer local Transformer, eight codebooks, and the same frozen
codec. Each is trained for 20,000 steps on approximately 4,000 hours
from the HiFiTTS-2 44-kHz subset \[34\], with a global batch of 128 and
training seed 42. AdamW \[59\] uses learning rates of $`10^{-4}`$ for
the pretrained backbone and $`3\times 10^{-4}`$ for newly initialized
modules, with 1,000 warmup steps followed by cosine decay. Reference
speech is supplied with probability 0.9 and limited to 375 events.

We evaluate duration cross-entropy on a HiFiTTS-2 validation split with
speakers held out from TTS training, and TTS WER on the 1,088 English
utterances in seed-tts-eval \[46\].

| Duration prediction     | Local prediction order                                                                                                  | Duration CE (nats) $`\downarrow`$ | WER (%) $`\downarrow`$ |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Independent head        | Parallel duration prediction; autoregressive codes                                                                      | 0.9698                            | 4.71                   |
| Before all codes        | $`d_{i}\rightarrow c_{i,\allowbreak 0}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow c_{i,\allowbreak 7}`$                                | 0.9737                            | 4.43                   |
| After the semantic code | $`c_{i,\allowbreak 0}\rightarrow d_{i}\rightarrow c_{i,\allowbreak 1}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow c_{i,\allowbreak 7}`$ | 0.8538                            | 4.40                   |
| After all codes         | $`c_{i,\allowbreak 0}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow c_{i,\allowbreak 7}\rightarrow d_{i}`$                                | 0.4265                            | 4.00                   |

Table B1: Effect of duration prediction structure and order. The
codebook indices follow Equation (18). Lower values are better for both
metrics; bold marks the best score in each column.

Duration cross-entropy is not directly comparable across orders:
duration-last conditions on all current-event codes, which may retain
duration information from interval aggregation (Equation (12)). Its
lower cross-entropy therefore reflects easier prediction under this
conditioning rather than better modeling of temporal structure.

We adopt duration-last prediction based on its lower duration
cross-entropy and TTS WER in this small-scale comparison (Table B1).
