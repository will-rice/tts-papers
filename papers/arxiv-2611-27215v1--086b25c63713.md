# Neural Defend Ranks \#1 in Global Audio Deepfake Detection Benchmark – ARCAS on the Podonos Independent Leaderboard

###### Abstract

Commercial detectors of AI-generated audio are usually promoted with
results on internal test sets that buyers cannot inspect. Independent
benchmarks with private labels give a more reliable comparison. We
report the results of Neural Defend’s ARCAS foundation models on the
Podonos audio deepfake detection benchmark, an independent leaderboard
that scores commercial detection APIs and open-source detectors on one
held-out set of real speech and speech from current text-to-speech and
voice-cloning systems, delivered in six file formats, against labels
that only Podonos holds. In the release of 29 October 2026, which covers
28 systems from 16 commercial vendors and the leading open models, ARCAS
290M and the smaller ARCAS 90M share first place, ahead of every other
commercial system, including Resemble AI, Hiya, Pindrop, Hive, and
Reality Defender. Both models make fewer errors than any other system by
a statistically clear margin, keep false positives and false negatives
in balance at their default threshold, process every submitted file
without rejection, and sit on the accuracy–latency frontier of the
leaderboard. As for every commercial system added since Podonos retired
its API runner, the ARCAS predictions were submitted by Neural Defend
and scored by Podonos against its private labels. We describe the
benchmark’s construction and protocol in full, reproduce the complete
leaderboard and its history across five releases, and analyse error
balance, statistical separation, speed, and the gap between commercial
and open-source detectors. The results agree with the companion
evaluation of ARCAS 290M on the complete test sets of ten public
benchmarks and establish ARCAS as the most accurate AI-generated audio
detector evaluated on this independent benchmark.

## 1 Introduction

Voice cloning needs only seconds of reference audio, and human listeners
identify current synthetic speech as fake about 69% of the time (Müller
and Choong, 2026). Banks, contact centres, telecom carriers, and
platforms therefore buy AI-generated audio detection as a service, but
vendors’ claims are hard to compare. Most vendors report accuracy on
internal test sets, on selected public datasets, or on leaderboard
snapshots of different dates, often with different definitions of
accuracy. Open-source detectors trained on older academic corpora give
little guidance either: when Deepfake-Eval-2024 tested them on audio
circulated in 2024, their AUROC fell by about half (Chandra et al.,
2025).

Independent benchmarks with private labels address this problem. A third
party builds a test set from current generators, keeps the labels
private, scores every system with one protocol, and publishes the
results for anyone to inspect. The Podonos audio deepfake detection
benchmark is a private-label leaderboard of this kind that focuses
specifically on commercial detection APIs (Podonos, 2026c; Podonos,
2026e). Since May 2026 it has grown from eight to 28 systems in five
releases, and it now includes 18 commercial entries from 16 vendors and
11 systems with downloadable weights (Podonos, 2026d).

In this report we present the results of Neural Defend’s ARCAS
foundation models on this benchmark. ARCAS 290M is Neural Defend’s
production model for detecting AI-generated audio; ARCAS 90M is a
smaller and faster model of the same family for latency-sensitive use.
In the October 2026 release, both models share first place with 99.93%
accuracy: three errors in 4,524 files, with a false-positive rate of
0.1% and a false-negative rate of 0.0% (Podonos, 2026d). The next
system, deetech.ai, makes 20 errors, and the best results from Resemble
AI, Hiya, Pindrop, Hive, and Reality Defender range from 24 to more than
a thousand errors on the same held-out test set.

The same model also leads on public data, under far broader conditions.
In the companion report, ARCAS 290M is evaluated on the complete
official test sets of ten public benchmarks, 5,644,079 recordings in
total, and reaches 0.14% equal error rate (EER) on the ASVspoof 5
evaluation set, below every commercial detector reported on that set
(Neural Defend, 2026a). Together the two reports give consistent
evidence from a private-label commercial comparison and from complete
public test sets.

Our contributions are:

- •
  A complete account of the Podonos benchmark. We document its test set,
  telephony tracks, submission protocol, metrics, and provenance rules,
  collected from every public release, so the results can be read
  without consulting scattered sources.
- •
  Leading results on an independent benchmark. ARCAS 290M and ARCAS 90M
  rank first of 28 systems, ahead of all 15 other commercial vendors,
  with balanced errors and full coverage.
- •
  Analysis beyond the leaderboard. We add confidence intervals and
  significance tests, error-balance and accuracy–latency analysis, the
  history of the leaderboard across five releases, and a comparison of
  commercial and open-source detectors.
- •
  Consistency with public benchmarks. We relate the Podonos results to
  the full-scale public evaluation of ARCAS 290M and state what the
  benchmark does and does not measure.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 How detection vendors report performance

Published evidence for commercial detectors falls into three groups. The
first is internal evaluation: vendors report accuracy on proprietary
test sets whose generators, sample counts, and class balance are not
released, so the numbers cannot be reproduced or compared across
vendors. The second is results on selected public datasets. A few
vendors have published peer-reviewed work, such as Reality Defender’s
ASVspoof 5 challenge system, which reached 5.5% EER on the Track 1
evaluation set (Zhu et al., 2024), but most public numbers come from
older corpora or are combined across datasets with vendor-chosen
weights. The third is independent leaderboards. The Speech DF Arena
scores open and proprietary systems on 14 public datasets, including
ASVspoof 5 (Dowerah et al., 2026), and the Podonos benchmark scores
commercial APIs on a private-label set of current voice-cloning audio
(Podonos, 2026d).

Only the third group lets a buyer compare vendors on the same files with
the same rules. Leaderboard results still have to be read with their
date and release, because rankings change as systems are added: in the
five Podonos releases to date, the first place has changed hands three
times. Independent results also show what internal tests cannot: how a
system behaves at its shipped threshold, how many files it declines, and
how its errors split between missed fakes and false alarms. These are
the properties a contact centre, bank, or platform experiences once a
detector is deployed in production.

### 2.2 Why open-source detectors fail on current audio

Most open-source detectors are trained on ASVspoof 2019, whose attacks
were built with text-to-speech and voice-conversion systems from 2018
and earlier. Their accuracy falls sharply on current generators and
recording conditions (Chandra et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Huang
et al., 2025). On the Podonos benchmark, nine of the 11 downloadable
systems score between 47.63% and 62.89% accuracy, close to chance on a
balanced test set, and one of them labels every clip as fake (Podonos,
2026d). Their training data predates the zero-shot voice-cloning systems
that attackers use, so their errors reflect outdated training rather
than the difficulty of the test set. This gap is the reason commercial
detection exists as a product category, and it is why buyers need
independent comparisons between commercial systems rather than against
academic baselines.

## 3 The Podonos Benchmark

This section describes the benchmark as published by Podonos in its
repository and in the five release posts (Podonos, 2026e; Podonos,
2026c; Podonos, 2026b; Podonos, 2026a; Podonos, 2026f; Podonos, 2026d).
Where the sources differ, we follow the repository.

### 3.1 Test set

The studio test set contains 4,524 English clips, balanced between 2,262
real and 2,262 AI-generated recordings, about six hours of audio
(Table 1). Real speech comes from three public corpora: VCTK (Yamagishi
et al., 2019) (110 English speakers with a range of accents), LJ Speech
(Ito and Johnson, 2017) (one speaker), and LibriTTS train-clean-360 (Zen
et al., 2019) (904 speakers in the full corpus). AI-generated speech
comes from about 25 text-to-speech and voice-cloning systems, including
commercial APIs such as ElevenLabs and open-weight models such as F5-TTS
and Chatterbox; the full list is not published, so detectors cannot be
tuned to it.

Synthetic clips that fail a Whisper transcription check against their
input text are discarded, so the set contains only intelligible speech.
Each clip is then converted to one of six file formats, in close to
equal numbers. Detectors must therefore handle lossy and lossless
compression, as with typical user-uploaded audio.

resamples each clip to 8 kHz, applies a 300–3400 Hz band-pass filter,

VoIP calls. The wideband track resamples to 16 kHz, applies a 50–7000 Hz
filter, and uses one of four codecs used in 4G and 5G calls. Codec

relevant to contact centres, where incoming calls arrive band-limited

### 3.3 Protocol and metrics

Each participant submits one prediction file per track, labelling every
clip _real_ or _fake_, optionally with the latency of each call. Systems
are scored at their default threshold, with no per-system tuning.
Podonos computes accuracy, $`F_{1}`$, false-positive rate (FPR, real
speech flagged as fake), and false-negative rate (FNR, fake speech
missed). Equal error rate is not reported, because deployed systems use
a fixed threshold. Declined or failed files are excluded and reported as
a rejection rate.

Latency is the mean per-file inference time in milliseconds, and the
real-time factor (RTF) is the mean ratio of latency to clip duration.
Podonos measured latency itself, including network round-trips, for the
three commercial systems it ran against vendor APIs (Resemble DETECT-3B
Omni, Hive, and Reality Defender); for other commercial systems,
including ARCAS, latency is reported by the vendor on its own hardware.
Since that API runner was retired, every new commercial result,
including ARCAS, comes from a vendor-submitted prediction file scored
against the private labels.

Since October 2026 the rules allow one submission per vendor per month,
with one model per track and no scoring of multiple checkpoints or
thresholds; timing data are required, and each vendor sees its result
before deciding whether to publish it (Podonos, 2026d).

### 4.1 Overall ranking

ARCAS 290M and ARCAS 90M share first place among 28 systems, each with
99.93% accuracy, $`F_{1}`$ of 0.999, FPR of 0.1%, and FNR of 0.0%
(Table 3). Both scored all 4,524 files without rejection. Each made
three errors; the published rates imply that all three, or two of them,
were real recordings flagged as AI-generated, and that at most one
AI-generated clip was missed.

Figure 1 shows the error rate of every commercial system. The nearest
competitors are deetech.ai (99.56%, 20 errors), Resemble DETECT-World
(99.47%, 24 errors), and Hiya (99.14% on the 4,420 files it accepted).
ARCAS makes 85% fewer errors than deetech.ai and 88% fewer than Resemble
DETECT-World. Against established vendors the gap is larger: Resemble
DETECT-3B Omni makes 88 errors, Pindrop 224, Hive 745, and Reality
Defender 1,076 on the 3,745 files it accepted.

### 4.2 Statistical significance

Small accuracy differences can arise by chance, so we compare error
rates with 95% Wilson intervals (Figure 2). The interval for ARCAS,
0.02–0.20%, overlaps no other system’s interval; the closest,
deetech.ai, spans 0.29–0.68%. A one-sided Fisher exact test, treating
the errors as independent, gives $`p=0.0002`$ for three versus 20
errors. A paired test would be more precise but needs per-file
predictions, which Podonos does not publish. Only ARCAS has a
statistically clear lead over the field; the intervals of each of the
next five commercial systems overlap those of their neighbours.

accurate commercial systems, computed from the published accuracy and

### 4.3 False positives and false negatives

The Podonos protocol scores each system at its own default threshold, so
the balance between false positives and false negatives shows how well
the threshold suits real use. ARCAS has the lowest FPR and the lowest
FNR of all commercial systems (Figure 3). Several other systems keep one
error type low at the cost of the other. Corsound AI flags only 1.0% of
real speech but misses 23.1% of AI-generated speech, and Hive misses
30.5%. Reality Defender misses 3.6% of AI-generated speech but flags
53.7% of real speech as fake. For a contact centre, the first pattern
lets fraud through and the second blocks genuine customers; ARCAS does
neither.

### 4.4 Latency

Detection in live calls and content moderation needs low latency. ARCAS
90M reports 89 ms per file (RTF 0.030) and ARCAS 290M 118 ms (RTF
0.041), so both process audio more than 24 times faster than real time.
Both are faster than Resemble DETECT-World (399 ms), Pindrop (282 ms),
Whispeak (1,052 ms), and Resemble DETECT-3B Omni (1,164 ms). Figure 4
plots accuracy against latency. The frontier of systems that no other
system beats on both measures runs through DetectifAI, Hiya, deetech.ai,
and ARCAS 90M; ARCAS 90M is the most accurate point on it, and the three
faster systems make between 6 and 83 times as many errors. Speed also
determines coverage in deployment: a detector this fast can screen every
call or upload as it arrives, rather than a sample. These latencies are
vendor-reported and indicative.

with timing data and accuracy of at least 94%. The dashed line joins
systems that no other system beats on both measures. Latency is
vendor-reported except for Resemble DETECT-3B Omni, Hive, and Reality
Defender.

### 4.5 Coverage

A detector that declines short or unusual files leaves them unprotected.
ARCAS scored every file. Hiya rejected 2.3% of files, Corsound AI 14.3%,
and Reality Defender 17.2%, including all clips shorter than 1.5 s
(Podonos, 2026c). Their accuracies are measured on the remaining files
only; Podonos reports that re-scoring full-coverage systems on those
subsets moves their accuracy by $`-1.2`$ to $`+4.2`$ percentage points
(Podonos, 2026e). Full coverage matters in practice because the clips
are short, with a mean of 4.80 s, close to the length of a greeting or a
single sentence on a call. A detector that skips short audio leaves
unchecked exactly the segments an attacker would use. ARCAS returned a
verdict for every clip in all six file formats, so its reported accuracy
applies to the complete test set.

improving quickly.

Yamagishi Lab, 2026); Wav2Vec2 trained on ASVspoof 2019 LA (Gustking,
2026); AST trained on ASVspoof 5 (MattyB95, 2026a); Wav2Vec2 trained on
a 2024 data mix (Gary Stafford, 2026); Deepfake-V2, based on wav2vec 2.0
base (MelodyMachine, 2026); AST trained on VoxCelebSpoof (MattyB95,
2026b); RawNet2 (Tak et al., 2021; MattyB95, 2026d) and LCNN-LFCC
(MattyB95, 2026c), both trained on ASVspoof 2019 LA; AASIST trained on
ASVspoof 2019 LA (Jung et al., 2022; Clova AI, 2026); and AASIST3
trained on ASVspoof 5 (lab260, 2026). Pella Research’s pellav2 reaches
95.82%, and NII AntiDeepfake 70.47%. The other nine score 47.63–62.89%.
ARCAS makes about one sixtieth as many errors as pellav2, so academic
detectors are no substitute for a production system. The gap is not
explained by model size alone: several open models use large
self-supervised encoders, yet without current training data they cannot
separate modern voice clones from real speech. Detection quality depends
on keeping pace with the generators that attackers use.

## 5 Discussion

### 5.1 Consistency with public benchmarks

A leaderboard of 4,524 English clips cannot by itself show how a
detector behaves across languages, channels, and generator families. The
companion report answers that question for ARCAS 290M on the complete
official test sets of ten public benchmarks, 5,644,079 recordings in
total (Neural Defend, 2026a). The two sources agree. On ASVspoof 5,
ARCAS 290M reaches 0.14% EER on all 680,774 Track 1 evaluation records,
against 0.38–0.79% for the commercial detectors on the Speech DF Arena
leaderboard (Dowerah et al., 2026) and 5.5% for Reality Defender’s
published challenge system (Zhu et al., 2024). Across five public
benchmarks of whole-file AI-generated speech it detects 99.60% of 4.80
million fakes, and on ASVspoof 5 its accuracy stays at or above 99.69%
in all 12 codec and telephone conditions. The Podonos lead is therefore
not specific to one test set.

### 5.2 What the benchmark measures

The Podonos benchmark tests the situation most buyers face: short
English clips from current voice-cloning systems, delivered in mixed
file formats, scored at the vendor’s default threshold with no access to
the labels. Its private labels, monthly submission limit, and
single-model rule make it hard to tune a system to the test set. Its
balanced classes make accuracy, FPR, and FNR directly interpretable, and
its rejection accounting exposes systems that decline difficult files.

### 5.3 Scope and known limitations

Several limits apply to these results, as they do to every system on the
leaderboard. The ARCAS predictions were submitted by Neural Defend and
scored by Podonos against its private labels, as for every commercial
system added since the API runner was retired; the reported latencies
were measured by Neural Defend on its own hardware. The test set is
English only and small enough that differences of a few errors are
uncertain, which is why we report confidence intervals. Podonos does not
publish per-generator or per-format results for ARCAS, and no results
have yet been released for the 8 kHz and 16 kHz telephony tracks.
Finally, a leaderboard is a snapshot, and later releases may change the
ranking.

## 6 Conclusion

On the Podonos independent benchmark, Neural Defend’s ARCAS 290M and
ARCAS 90M rank first among 28 systems and ahead of all 15 other
commercial vendors, with three errors in 4,524 files, the lowest
false-positive and false-negative rates of any commercial system, full
coverage, and latency below 120 ms per file. Their lead over the next
system is the only statistically clear gap among the leading systems,
and their entry cut the leading error rate by 84%. Together with the
full-scale public evaluation of ARCAS 290M, these results establish
ARCAS as the most accurate AI-generated audio detector on the
independent evidence reviewed here. We encourage other vendors to report
results on independent private-label benchmarks and on complete public
test sets, so that buyers can compare detectors on the same evidence.
