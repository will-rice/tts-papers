## Abstract

Music audio generation spans instrumental clips, full songs with vocals,
edits of existing recordings, and systems that play alongside a musician
in real time. This monograph explains the methods behind these systems
for readers familiar with neural-network training who are new to music
generation. It introduces neural audio codecs and autoencoders,
autoregressive and masked token models, diffusion and flow matching,
sampling acceleration, and preference optimization in a shared notation.
Worked examples connect training objectives and sampling procedures to
published systems. The application sections relate the musical
requirements of instrumental generation, songs, editing, and live
performance to model design. Comparison tables record representations,
methods, inputs, tasks, and release information documented in papers and
official sources. Symbolic composition, speech, and sound effects are
included where they connect to music audio. We explain how to interpret
automatic metrics and learned judges, design and report listening tests,
and assess evidence about data provenance, memorization, attribution,
and output detection. Selected legal decisions and disputes illustrate
the implications for training and release. The reviewed systems combine
established generative paradigms with new representations, planning
stages, and post-training procedures. Differences in data, scale, and
evaluation limit what cross-system comparisons can establish about the
sources of reported gains.

## Preface

We came to this subject first as players and listeners, and later as
researchers in music information retrieval and music technology. The
field has changed quickly, and its literature has become difficult to
follow as a whole. We wrote this monograph for graduate students,
researchers, and engineers entering music audio generation, and for
musicians curious about the methods behind the tools. It brings the main
objectives and sampling procedures into a shared notation and connects
model design to musical aims, practical constraints, and the evidence
behind system comparisons. The mathematical sections assume familiarity
with neural-network training, transformers, and variational
autoencoders. Musical terms are explained where they first matter.
Readers who come to the subject through musical practice may find the
sections on control, neural instruments, and live interaction a good
place to begin. We hope the monograph makes the field easier to enter
than we found it, and that it keeps the music in view.

## Chapter 1 Introduction

A useful music generator must do more than produce plausible sound. A
lyrics-to-song system must coordinate the words, the singing, and any
accompaniment across the song’s sections. An edit must change the
requested feature and preserve the rest of the recording. A system used
in performance must respond soon enough for a musician to act on what
they hear. These requirements place different demands on representation,
generation, and control, and the methods that current systems use to
meet them are scattered across papers that often assume familiarity with
earlier work. Neural audio generation predates the systems treated here
by nearly a decade. Autoregressive waveform models demonstrated that
neural networks can synthesize audio sample by sample (van den Oord et
al., 2016; Mehri et al., 2017), two-stage text-to-speech pipelines
established the pattern of generating an intermediate representation and
rendering it with a neural vocoder (Shen et al., 2018), and transformers
over symbolic tokens captured long-range musical structure (Huang et
al., 2019). Jukebox produced full songs with singing directly in the
audio domain (Dhariwal et al., 2020), but at low fidelity and with
sampling times measured in hours. For music, these systems remained
proofs of concept rather than usable tools.

The change came in early 2023, when three results arrived within months
of each other. MusicLM framed text-to-music generation as hierarchical
language modeling over discrete audio tokens (Agostinelli et al., 2023),
building on the SoundStream codec with residual vector
quantization (Zeghidour et al., 2022), the semantic-plus-acoustic token
decomposition of AudioLM (Borsos et al., 2023), and joint music-text
embeddings (Huang et al., 2022). Other widely used open codecs include
EnCodec and the Descript Audio Codec (Défossez et al., 2023; Kumar et
al., 2023). In the same year, a parallel line of work showed that latent
diffusion reaches comparable quality from continuous
representations (Huang et al., 2023a; Liu et al., 2023a; Schneider et
al., 2024), and MusicGen demonstrated that a single-stage token language
model with a simple codebook interleaving scheme suffices as a strong,
open baseline (Copet et al., 2023). Together these systems established
the two architectural lineages, codec-token language models and latent
diffusion, that organize most of the systems tabulated in this monograph
(Figure 6 gives the counts).

The work that followed these systems spread in several directions at
once. It expanded from clip-level instrumental generation toward full
songs with vocals, lyrics, and verse-chorus structure, first in closed
commercial systems (Suno, 2026b; Udio, 2026; Bai et al., 2024) and then
in open models (Yuan et al., 2025; Ning et al., 2025; Gong et al., 2025;
Lei et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026b). The two lineages converged into
planner-renderer hybrids, in which a language model plans a coarse
structure and a diffusion or flow model renders audio (Lam et al., 2023;
Yang et al., 2025a; Lam et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026). Flow matching,
distillation, and adversarial post-training reduced sampling cost to the
point of faster-than-real-time and streaming generation (Prajwal et al.,
2024; Novack et al., 2025; Evans et al., 2026; Lyria Team et al., 2025;
Novack et al., 2026). Preference alignment, introduced by large-scale
RLHF for music (Cideron et al., 2024b) and by direct preference
optimization for text-to-audio (Majumder et al., 2024), became an
increasingly common post-training stage (Lei et al., 2025; Chen et al.,
2025). A parallel thread developed fine-grained and time-varying
control (Wu et al., 2024b; Tal et al., 2024; Novack et al., 2024b) and
instruction-based editing (Han et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024b; Zhang
et al., 2024a), moving systems from one-shot generators toward
production tools.

Evaluation methods have improved more slowly than generation methods.
The Fréchet Audio Distance (Kilgour et al., 2019), the default automatic
metric for most of this period, is sensitive to the choice of embedding
and reference set (Gui et al., 2024; Tailleur et al., 2024; Jeong, 2026)
and correlates weakly with human preferences for music (Huang et al.,
2025). The community is responding with metrics validated against human
preferences (Huang et al., 2025), expert-annotated benchmarks (Liu et
al., 2025a; Yao et al., 2025a), live human-preference arenas (Kim et
al., 2025b), and learned aesthetics predictors (Tjandra et al., 2025),
but no standard protocol has emerged, particularly for full songs with
lyrics.

The conditions under which music generators are trained and released are
contested. Record-label litigation over training data (Recording
Industry Association of America, 2024) has continued alongside selected
settlements and licensing agreements (Universal Music Group, 2025;
Warner Music Group, 2025b; Warner Music Group, 2025a; Malik, 2025),
while the Munich Regional Court has issued the two judgments discussed
in Section 12 (Landgericht München I, 2025; Landgericht München I,
2026). Commercial agreements change particular relationships between
companies. They do not by themselves establish complete training-data
provenance, settle other parties’ claims, or demonstrate that musicians
have consented or been fairly compensated. We therefore examine data
documentation, memorization, attribution, and output identification as
distinct questions, with their own methods: memorization
measurement (Yuan et al., 2025; Bralios et al., 2024), watermarking (San
Roman et al., 2024), and synthetic-music detection (Rahman et al., 2025;
Comanducci et al., 2025; Afchar et al., 2024). Reporting remains
limited, although some systems document their training sources (Evans et
al., 2025; Nistal et al., 2024a). We also distinguish company
announcements, legal allegations, and court findings according to the
evidence each provides.

Surveys of parts of this literature exist, and this text leans on them
and points to them where they go deeper. The earlier deep-learning
surveys organize generation by objective, representation, and
architecture (Briot et al., 2020; Ji et al., 2020) or by the musical
dimension a system generates (Herremans et al., 2017), and were written
before audio-domain systems reached usable quality. Among recent
surveys, the broadest treatment of music foundation models spans
understanding as well as generation (Ma et al., 2024). Others cover
single branches such as symbolic generation with language-model
methods (Le et al., 2025), video-to-music generation (Ji et al., 2026;
Wang et al., 2025d), music understanding models (Li et al., 2024f),
large audio models across speech, sound, and music (Latif et al., 2023),
generation in live interaction (Agchar et al., 2024), tools and
products (Zhu et al., 2023b), or general audio generation methods (Božić
and Horvat, 2024; Chen et al., 2024c). Evaluation has surveys of its
own (Xiong et al., 2023; Kader and Karmaker, 2025; Lerch et al., 2025),
alignment has one (Herremans and Roy, 2026), ethics has one (Barnett,
2023), and a position paper maps the open research areas of music AI in
the foundation-model era (Wei et al., 2024). The survey closest in scope
organizes music generation by guidance modality across symbolic and
audio output and tabulates methods by task, year, and architecture (Li
et al., 2026a). Its coverage of symbolic generation and of multimodal
fusion designs is broader than ours, and its newest systems date from
early 2025. These surveys provide complementary coverage, and we refer
readers to them for fuller treatments of individual branches. The
purpose here is to teach the foundations that the current systems share,
to show how the design choices behind them appear in published systems,
and to leave the reader able to read the next system’s report, place it,
and judge it.

The monograph has four learning goals. After Sections 2 to 5, the reader
should be able to identify, in a system description, the representation,
the generation method, the conditions, and the evidence behind its
claims, write down the training objective and sampling procedure of its
paradigm, and compute from its codec the token rate (tokens per second
of audio), the sequence length, and the step count that set its cost.
The worked examples of Section 4 do this for MusicGen, and a reading
guide does the same for the standard metrics. Sections 6 to 9 explain
the evidence relevant to three practical choices: between the token and
the latent lineage for a given deployment, between an adapter (a small
trained module inside a frozen model), a control branch, inference-time
optimization, and an explicit plan for a given control, and between a
large distilled model and a small purpose-built one for a given latency
budget. The evidence and the open trade-offs are stated at the end of
each of those sections. After Section 11, the reader can tell which
numbers in a system report’s evaluation have been checked against human
judgments, and can design and report a listening test. After Section 12,
the reader can read a model card for what it says about data, license,
and memorization. The same section says which of these statements the
dated legal record bears on.

That aim shapes how the text is built. The shared objectives and
sampling procedures are stated in one notation and shown at work in
representative systems, and the figures and tables are built so that
they can be read without the surrounding prose. The comparison tables
record the design and release information documented in papers, model
cards, repositories, and official product sources. Section 2 explains
the selection process, and Appendix 15 records the coverage and
observation dates. Missing information remains explicit, so readers can
distinguish a reported property from an inference.

Six questions recur across the systems, and we ask the reader to carry
them through the text, because each is a question of machine learning
before it is a question of music and each has a different answer in the
application classes of Sections 6 to 9.

Q1, representation.  
What information does the generated representation keep and discard, and
what does that decide about fidelity, sequence length, and control?
Codec tokens, continuous latents, and sketches answer differently
(Sections 3, 12, and 6).

Q2, cost.  
How do sequence length, token rate, and the generative paradigm set
training and inference cost, and what bounds latency? The objectives of
Section 4 make the costs explicit. Sections 6 and 9 report them.

Q3, conditioning.  
How do global conditions, time-varying conditions, and reference audio
enter a model, and what does each route cost in training and in
fidelity? Sections 14 and 8 answer.

Q4, planning.  
When should a plan be generated before the audio, what should the plan
contain, and what does the separation cost? Section 4 states the
planner-renderer objective. Sections 7 and 8 record where the separation
helped.

Q5, measurement.  
What can distribution-level metrics establish about generated music,
where do they fail, and how well do the alternative instruments agree
with human judgments? Section 11 answers.

Q6, artifacts.  
What changes when a model is a released artifact rather than a paper:
openness, provenance, memorization, and the legal record? Sections 16
and 12 answer.

These questions guide the comparisons in the application sections. The
comparison framework of Section 5 records the design choices behind Q1
to Q4, the application class in which each choice was made, and the
evidence about data, access, and release conditions that Q6 asks for.
Section 14 returns to the six questions with the answers the collected
systems support. The answers converge on one observation, stated there
with its limits: every generative paradigm in the tables was known by
2023, and the systems since differ in what they generate over, in what
follows their training, and in whether a planner precedes the renderer.
The tables cannot establish which of these changes account for the
reported gains, because the systems differ in data, scale, and
evaluation.

Figure 4 shows the structure of the monograph. Sections 2 to 5 teach the
foundations: the scope, the building blocks, and the taxonomy with its
notation and worked examples. Sections 6 to 10 review the systems by
application class. Sections 11 and 12 cover evaluation and data.
Sections 13 and 14 discuss and conclude. Appendix 15 documents the
search. The mathematical sections use vectors, conditional
probabilities, expectations, and gradients, and they take a neural
network to be a parameterized function fitted to training examples.
Audio signal processing is not assumed. Where transformers,
autoencoders, and other architectures appear, the text explains their
role in the pipeline and does not derive them. Four reading paths cover
the readers we have in mind. A reader familiar with machine learning but
new to music generation should take Sections 2 to 4 in order, then
Section 6 and one application section of interest, and consult Section 5
and the tables as needed. A reader building or choosing a system should
read Section 4, the section of the intended application class among
Sections 6 to 9 with its closing subsection of lessons and trade-offs,
and the comparison tables. Section 17 explains how to navigate them. A
musician or producer who wants the ideas before the formulas can read
the openings, running examples, and closing lessons of Sections 6 to 9,
with Section 32 on neural instruments and Section 31 on live interaction
as entry points, and then the evaluation and provenance discussion. The
formulas and the statistical protocol assume the stated background. A
reader concerned with evaluation, data, or policy can go to Sections 11
and 12 directly. They depend on the taxonomy only through the class
names.

#### Scope.

The monograph covers the generation of music audio conditioned on text,
lyrics, audio, symbolic signals, or video, including instrumental music,
songs with vocals, stems (the separate instrument or voice tracks of a
mix) and accompaniments, and edits of existing recordings. It treats
symbolic composition only where it meets audio pipelines or co-creation,
because its methods and its evaluation differ from those of audio
generation, and Section 36 says where the two meet. Singing-voice
synthesis from a score, speech synthesis, and sound-effect generation
are excluded unless a system also generates music. We report training
data, evaluation, and release status from the original sources. Many
industrial systems disclose only part of this information. Coverage and
source dates are documented in Section 2 and Appendix 15.

## Chapter 2 Background and Scope

The review focuses on music audio generation from MusicLM (Agostinelli
et al., 2023) onward, with earlier work included where it establishes
the methods used by these systems. We take MusicLM as the starting point
because it is the point at which text-to-music generation became
simultaneously high-fidelity, minute-scale, and specified in enough
detail to establish a reusable design. General text-to-audio models are
included when they are evaluated on music or serve as the basis for
music systems. The other limits of coverage are those of the Scope
paragraph in Section 1.

Sections 3 and 4 develop the representations and objectives, the
application sections connect them to musical tasks, and Section 11
explains the evaluation evidence. Section 12 separates the technical
questions of data and memorization from the dated account of cases and
settlements. Section 2 and Appendix 15 document the collection process
and its limits.

### 1 Earlier Foundations

The 2023 systems combined four older lines, each cited here for the
design it contributed. Autoregressive waveform models showed that a
neural network can synthesize audio sample by sample from a learned
distribution, at a cost of one sequential prediction per sample (van den
Oord et al., 2016; Mehri et al., 2017). The token language models of
Section 6 keep the autoregressive factorization and move it from samples
to codec tokens at a rate lower by two orders of magnitude. Two-stage
pipelines, in which one model generates an intermediate representation
and a second renders it, were established in text-to-speech (Shen et
al., 2018) and recur in every planner-renderer system of Section 7.
Transformers over symbolic tokens captured long-range musical structure
with relative attention (Huang et al., 2019), which is the model class
the token systems scaled, and Jukebox applied the same class to a
hierarchy of VQ-VAE audio codes, producing full songs with singing at
low fidelity and with sampling times measured in hours (Dhariwal et al.,
2020). Neural codecs with residual vector quantization (Zeghidour et
al., 2022; Défossez et al., 2023) and the semantic-plus-acoustic
decomposition of AudioLM (Borsos et al., 2023) are what made the
hierarchy fast enough to use. Adversarial synthesis demonstrated fast
spectral-domain generation at small scale (Engel et al., 2019), and its
discriminators return as the adversarial post-training of the current
diffusion models. Differentiable signal processing (Engel et al., 2020)
and the RAVE autoencoder (Caillon and Esling, 2021) started the
instrument line of Section 32 in parallel with, and independently of,
the text-to-music systems. Each of these techniques reappears in
modified form in the literature reviewed here.

The infrastructure behind the 2023 systems is covered here as building
blocks. First, neural audio codecs with residual vector quantization
compress waveforms into discrete token sequences that language models
can predict (Zeghidour et al., 2022; Défossez et al., 2023; Kumar et
al., 2023), with more recent tokenizers reducing token cost further (Ji
et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026). Second, contrastive audio-text
embeddings provide the conditioning signal that connects free-form
language to audio (Huang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2023b), and
self-supervised music encoders supply features for understanding and
evaluation (Li et al., 2024g; Zhu et al., 2025). Evaluation is treated
in Section 11, after the systems, and that section gives the metric and
its known limits for the claims about quality in Sections 6 to 10.

### 2 Literature Collection

The bibliography was built by three complementary methods, applied
throughout the collection period and closed when the classification was
fixed. Citation chasing followed references forward and backward from
the landmark systems and covered the proceedings of ISMIR, ICLR,
NeurIPS, ICML, ICASSP, Interspeech, AAAI, and IJCAI and the journals
TASLP, TISMIR, and TMLR. Structured searches queried the arXiv API with
fixed search strings in nineteen topic groups, from surveys and codecs
to provenance, preference alignment, neural synthesis instruments,
multi-agent composition, motion and games, and a group for the most
recent months. Over the collection period they returned 1,115 unique
candidates, of which 83 were already cited through citation chasing at
the time of the search. Every candidate was screened on title and
abstract. During the collection period 253 were added, 163 with a
dedicated sentence in the text and 90 in grouped citations. A second
reading of the abstracts of every candidate not then added, completed in
October 2026 after the classification was closed, found 13 further
candidates that meet the inclusion criteria and 137 that merit a grouped
mention. These additions are not included in the present classification
or its counts. Five of them predate the eligibility cutoff but were
identified after the classification had been finalized, and
Appendix 15.A names them.

Venue monitoring used the accepted-paper list of ISMIR 2026 (140
papers), classified 36 as generative music work and 38 as adjacent, and
located public versions for 62. Accepted papers without a public version
are not cited. A paper is included if it generates music audio or
directly supports it (codecs, embeddings, evaluation, provenance) and if
it introduces an architecture, control mechanism, training procedure,
evaluation method, or resource that subsequent work builds on. Work
available only as a preprint is included when it meets these criteria
and is marked as a preprint in the bibliography. Figure 1 shows the
composition of the resulting bibliography by year and category.

Candidates were de-duplicated against the bibliography and across groups
by arXiv identifier and by normalized title. A candidate was excluded at
screening if it generated no music audio and supported no such system,
if it was a duplicate or a later version of an included work, or if it
contributed no architecture, control mechanism, training procedure,
evaluation method, or resource of the kinds listed above. The decision
and its reason were recorded for each candidate. Where sources disagree,
the monograph follows a fixed order: a peer-reviewed version over a
preprint, a preprint over a model card, a model card over product
documentation, and product documentation over press or legal reports,
except that the openness, license, and withdrawal status of an artifact
are taken from its card or repository because they change after the
paper. The source is chosen for the claim: a methods paper for an
objective, a versioned model card for a checkpoint’s license, product
documentation for current product behavior, and the court record for a
judgment. Appendix 15 gives the search strings, the start date of each
group, the closing date of the searches, the per-group counts, and the
flow from candidates to included works (Figure 10).

The system comparison includes selected releases with an eligibility
cutoff at the end of September 2026. Appendix 15 distinguishes that
cutoff from the closing date of the structured searches and the dates on
which release information was observed. The bibliography also cites some
later work for context, and that work does not enter the tables or the
counts. The tables describe the documented versions included in this
review. They should not be read as continuously updated product
specifications.

## Chapter 3 Building Blocks

### 3 Codecs, Tokenizers, and Autoencoders

A waveform holds tens of thousands of samples for every second of sound,
so most systems describe it with a much shorter sequence. An encoder
produces that description, a decoder reconstructs audio from it, and the
generative model learns to produce new descriptions of the same kind.
The representation constrains how much information survives and how much
work the generator must do. The exceptions are the waveform-domain
cascades and the symbolic systems marked as such in the tables. On the
discrete side, SoundStream introduced neural codecs with residual vector
quantization (RVQ), turning waveforms into stacks of token
sequences (Zeghidour et al., 2022). EnCodec provided an open,
high-fidelity implementation and became the default tokenizer for music
language models (Défossez et al., 2023), and the Descript Audio Codec
improved fidelity per bit substantially (Kumar et al., 2023). AudioLM
added the now-standard split between semantic tokens, which carry
structure, and acoustic tokens, which carry detail (Borsos et al.,
2023). A diffusion decoder from tokens to waveform raised the fidelity
ceiling of a fixed token stream (San Roman et al., 2023).

Three properties of a codec matter to a generator, and each later
tokenizer can be read as an attempt to improve one of them. The first is
the token rate, the number of tokens a language model must predict per
second of audio, which sets the sequence length and therefore the cost
and the reachable duration. The worked example of Section 4 computes it
for MusicGen. The second is semantic content: whether the tokens carry
information about what is played and sung, which a language model can
learn to predict, or only about how it sounds. The third is graceful
degradation: whether dropping the finer codebooks of an RVQ stack
removes detail in a controlled way or breaks the signal. The cost of RVQ
stacks, several parallel token streams per frame, has motivated work
along all three.

On the token rate, single-codebook codecs reach very low rates (Ji et
al., 2025), music-specific codecs reach extreme compression (Xu et al.,
2025), spectral-domain codecs cover full-band stereo (Li et al., 2025c),
and two-dimensional mel-spectrogram tokenizers share one codebook across
frequency bands, and their authors argue that RVQ stacks are poorly
matched to autoregressive prediction (Cheng et al., 2026; Li et al.,
2025b). On semantic content, a codec trained for reconstruction alone
may encode information in a form that a language model cannot predict
efficiently, which X-Codec addresses by supervising the codec with a
semantic encoder (Ye et al., 2025). Source-aware dual-track tokenizers
give vocal and accompaniment language models tokens that reconstruct
well and remain predictable (Lin et al., 2025b). The most recent step is
a semantically supervised low-rate tokenizer: Qwen-Music trains a single
25 Hz codebook with transcription, chroma, and mel-reconstruction losses
so that the tokens keep lyric and pitch information, and leaves the
remaining acoustic detail to a downstream generative renderer (Xu et
al., 2026). On degradation, multi-scale quantizers place codebooks at
different temporal resolutions (Siuzdak et al., 2024), and
frequency-ordered training makes truncating an RVQ stack remove
predictable bands (Yang et al., 2026c). Codec benchmarks (Wu et al.,
2024a) and a systematic review of discrete audio tokenizers (Mousavi et
al., 2025) compare these designs. Universal low-frame-rate codecs,
semantic codecs for general sound, streaming dialogue codecs, and
transformer-only tokenizers trained at scale continue the
development (Hu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2024a; Défossez et al., 2024;
Gong et al., 2026b; He et al., 2026a), and fast stereo vocoders decode
compressed representations into audio (Zhu et al., 2024).

A discrete representation chooses entries from a finite vocabulary, and
a continuous representation uses vectors of real numbers. In both cases
the generator works in the compressed space and a decoder turns its
output into audio. The decoder is trained on reconstructions of real
recordings, and a separate generator learns to produce plausible new
sequences in the same space. A variational autoencoder is one way to
learn a continuous space whose distribution is regularized in training,
and what follows concerns the resulting representation and decoder
rather than the training objective. On the continuous side, diffusion
and flow models operate over VAE latents or spectrograms (Liu et al.,
2023a; Evans et al., 2024a). The design trend is toward autoencoders
that are compact, accurate in reconstruction, and semantically
structured at once. Highly compressed latents enable full-length songs
in one context (Evans et al., 2024b), and frame rates as low as 5 Hz
make block-wise generation tractable (Jiang et al., 2025).
Semantic-acoustic objectives structure the latent for downstream
generation and editing (Parker et al., 2026; Evans et al., 2026),
autoencoders trained with a consistency objective (Section 9)
reconstruct from the latent in a single step (Pasini et al., 2024b), and
complex-spectral autoencoders address the high-frequency, phase, and
stereo failures of high-compression decoders (Wang et al., 2026b).

### 4 Audio-Text Embeddings and Music Encoders

Conditioning is the information supplied to guide generation: a
description, a melody, lyrics, or an existing recording. An embedding
turns such information into numerical features the generator can use,
and when the same embedding also scores the output, the score inherits
the distinctions the embedding learned to make. A text prompt reaches a
generator through a text encoder or through a joint audio-text embedding
trained to place a recording and its description near each other, and
the joint embeddings also serve as prompt-adherence scores. MuLan,
trained contrastively on 44 million recordings, is the conditioning
backbone of MusicLM (Huang et al., 2022). CLAP is its open counterpart
and is also used as an automatic prompt-adherence score (Wu et al.,
2023b). Self-supervised music encoders provide features for
understanding, control, and evaluation: MERT is the common choice (Li et
al., 2024g), and MuQ uses Mel-RVQ token prediction, reports improvements
over MERT, and releases a paired music-text embedding (Zhu et al.,
2025). CLaMP 3 extends contrastive music-text pretraining across
symbolic, audio, and many languages (Wu et al., 2025a). Benchmarks for
these representations (Yuan et al., 2023) matter to generation research
because metrics, reward models, and planning schemes (Lam et al., 2025)
inherit the biases of the encoder they build on (Section 11).

### 5 Data

Training corpora are mostly undisclosed. We defer provenance and
licensing to Section 12. Two data problems recur on the technical level.
First, captions: paired music-text data is scarce, so systems generate
pseudo-captions with language models (Huang et al., 2023a) or parse
musical metadata out of rich captions (Melechovsky et al., 2024a).
Evaluation depends on small human-written caption sets (Agostinelli et
al., 2023; Manco et al., 2023). Open caption corpora are built by
captioning freely licensed music at scale (Roy et al., 2025) or by
rewriting expert album reviews (Alonso-Jiménez et al., 2026). Second,
quality: open corpora are noisy, and systems increasingly account for
recording quality during training, either by conditioning on per-clip
quality so that inference can be steered toward high-quality output (Li
et al., 2025a) or by filtering and rewarding with learned aesthetics
predictors (Tjandra et al., 2025).

## Chapter 4 Objectives, Sampling, and Metrics

This section gives a common description of music audio generation and
fixes the notation. It defines the training objectives of the main
generative and preference methods, explains sampling and acceleration,
and introduces the standard evaluation metrics. Section 5 then uses
these definitions to compare systems. Throughout, sampling means
producing an output from a learned probability model. An audio sample is
one numerical value of a waveform, while musical sampling means reusing
a recorded excerpt. The statements are the standard ones from the cited
method papers, presented in a shared notation and connected to worked
examples so that readers can interpret the system comparisons of
Sections 6 to 9. A reader who prefers the example to the formula can
start from the worked examples, which come before the formal statement
wherever the example can stand on its own.

### 6 The Generic Problem

Every system in this monograph generates music audio by filling four
slots.

1.  1.  _A representation._ A map from a waveform $`x`$ to the object the
        model generates, which is a sequence of codec tokens, a continuous
        latent, a symbolic score, or a sketch, together with a decoder that
        maps the object back to audio.
2.  2.  _A factorization_ of the distribution of that object given the
        condition $`c`$: one token at a time, many tokens in parallel, a
        chain of denoising steps, a vector field integrated from noise to
        data, or a plan followed by a rendering.
3.  3.  _A route for the condition._ The condition enters as a global
        embedding attended to at every step, as a sequence aligned in time
        with the representation, as reference audio that the model continues
        or inverts, or through the plan.
4.  4.  _A sampler_, with its number of network evaluations, and whatever
        reduces that number: fewer steps, a distilled student, a cache over
        blocks.

Section 5 uses the first three of these decisions as its comparison
questions, records the sampler’s consequences in the latency cells of
the tables, and adds the application and the release conditions as the
context in which a system’s results are read. To read a new system
report, identify the choices made in each of these four slots. Most of
the trade-offs in this monograph are trade-offs between slots. A lower
token rate in slot 1 shortens the sequence, which reduces decoding cost
in slot 4, usually at a cost in reconstruction quality. A plan in slot 2
can make structure explicit. It also introduces a second model and a
possible mismatch between the plans seen in training and the predicted
plans seen at inference. A time-aligned condition in slot 3 gives more
direct control, but supporting a new control often requires additional
training. Figure 2 draws slot 2 for the five paradigms, and Table 1
lists the symbols used below.

### 7 Representations

#### Example first.

MusicGen’s codec is an EnCodec model for 32 kHz monophonic audio (Copet
et al., 2023). It cuts the waveform into frames with a stride of 640
samples, so it produces $`32{,}000/640=50`$ frames per second, and it
describes each frame with four tokens, each chosen from a table of 2,048
entries. The language model behind MusicGen must therefore produce
$`50\cdot 4=200`$ tokens for every second of music, or $`6{,}000`$
tokens for a 30-second clip, and the codec’s bitrate is
$`50\cdot 4\cdot\log_{2}2048=2{,}200`$ bits per second. These numbers
help explain the duration and runtime trade-offs in the tables. Token
rate determines sequence length, while bitrate and codec design
constrain reconstruction quality. For a fixed context limit, a lower
token rate permits longer outputs and can reduce generation cost,
although it may reduce reconstruction quality. The tokenizer research of
Section 3 works on this trade-off. The rest of this subsection states
the general form of what the example computed.

We write $`x\in\mathbb{R}^{T}`$ for a waveform of $`T`$ samples and
$`c`$ for the conditioning input (text, lyrics, audio, symbolic signals,
or video). Most systems generate over a learned compression of $`x`$
produced by an encoder $`E`$ and inverted by a decoder $`D`$. The
waveform-domain cascades and the symbolic systems are the exceptions
marked as such in the tables.

A _neural codec_ with residual vector quantization (RVQ) (Zeghidour et
al., 2022; Défossez et al., 2023) maps $`x`$ to $`Q`$ parallel token
streams. The encoder produces frames
$`e_{1},\dots,e_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ at a frame rate
$`f_{\mathrm{fr}}`$, so that $`N=f_{\mathrm{fr}}\cdot T/f_{\mathrm{s}}`$
for a sample rate $`f_{\mathrm{s}}`$, and each frame is quantized in
$`Q`$ stages against codebooks
$`\mathcal{C}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{C}^{(Q)}`$ of size $`K`$:

```math
r^{(0)}_{n}=e_{n},\qquad z^{(q)}_{n}=\arg\min_{k}\big\lVert r^{(q-1)}_{n}-\mathcal{C}^{(q)}_{k}\big\rVert_{2},\qquad r^{(q)}_{n}=r^{(q-1)}_{n}-\mathcal{C}^{(q)}_{z^{(q)}_{n}}. \tag{1}
```

The codec is trained with a reconstruction loss on $`D(z)`$ against
$`x`$ (spectral and adversarial terms in the cited codecs), a commitment
loss that pulls each encoder residual toward the codebook entry chosen
for it, and a codebook update that moves the entries toward the
residuals assigned to them. The quantizer itself is not differentiable,
and the gradient is passed straight through it to the encoder. The first
stream carries most of the energy of the frame and the later streams
refine the residual, which is why a truncated stack still decodes and
why a language model can predict the streams in order of importance. In
musical terms, the stack is a sketch followed by finer and finer
corrections, and dropping the last corrections leaves a coarser but
recognizable rendering. The bitrate is
$`f_{\mathrm{fr}}\cdot Q\cdot\log_{2}K`$ bits per second, and the token
rate seen by a generator is $`f_{\mathrm{fr}}\cdot Q`$ tokens per
second. Both figures appear in the codec discussion of Section 3. A
_semantic tokenizer_ is trained to produce a single stream $`s_{1:M}`$
that keeps structure (pitch, lyrics, timing) and leaves the remaining
acoustic detail to a renderer. What it keeps is a property of its
supervision. The split between semantic and acoustic tokens is the one
introduced by AudioLM (Borsos et al., 2023). A _continuous autoencoder_
maps $`x`$ to a latent $`h\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}`$ with $`L\ll T`$,
trained with a reconstruction loss and, in the variational case, a
Kullback–Leibler penalty that keeps the latent close to a Gaussian
prior. These three outputs, with symbolic tokens as a fourth, are the
classes of the representation dimension D1. In the example,
$`f_{\mathrm{fr}}=50`$, $`Q=4`$, and $`K=2048`$.

#### Notation remark.

The codec papers write the number of codebooks as $`N_{q}`$
(SoundStream) or $`K`$ (EnCodec and MusicGen), and MusicGen’s paper
calls the parallel streams “codebook streams”. Our $`Q`$ is their number
of codebooks and our $`K`$ their codebook size. Our $`N`$ counts frames,
which EnCodec writes as $`T/\mathrm{stride}`$.

#### Warning: semantic and acoustic tokens are not two codecs.

Both are discrete streams at a frame rate, and a paper may call either
one “tokens”. A semantic stream is built so that a language model can
predict it (what it keeps depends on its training; the semantic
tokenizers reviewed here keep pitch, lyrics, and timing and drop timbre
and room), and it cannot be decoded to audio without a renderer. An
acoustic stream is built so that a decoder can reconstruct the waveform,
and it is harder to predict. A system that “generates tokens” may be
doing either, and the tables mark the difference in the D1 column
(semantic streams fall under the hybrid class when a renderer follows).

### 8 Generative Paradigms

Schematic diagrams of the five generative paradigms: autoregressive
token models; masked decoding; diffusion; flow matching; and
planner-renderer hybrids.

Figure 2: Data paths of the five generative paradigms (D2). Boxes are
the stages a sample passes through, from the condition to the audio, and
arrows give the order of computation. Notation follows Section 4.

Every paradigm models $`p(\cdot\mid c)`$ over one of the representations
above. Two time axes appear below and must be kept apart. The frames of
a representation follow the playback time of the music. The time
variable of a diffusion or flow sampler indexes the progress of
sampling: each step updates a representation of the whole excerpt, so
the number of frames sets the size of one update and the number of steps
sets how many updates are made.

#### Example first.

MusicGen predicts its four token streams in one left-to-right pass by
delaying stream $`q`$ by $`q-1`$ steps, so that at each step the model
emits one token of each stream and the later streams can see the earlier
ones of the same frame (Copet et al., 2023). The pattern is a canon:
each voice enters one step after the previous one and follows it.
Figure 3 draws the two schedules.

Figure 3: Two schedules for predicting four token streams of six frames
with one autoregressive model. Left: the flattened schedule predicts the
four tokens of each frame one after another, so a clip of $`N`$ frames
costs $`NQ`$ steps (here 24). Right: the delay schedule predicts one
token of every stream at each step, with stream $`q`$ delayed by $`q-1`$
frames, so the same clip costs $`N+Q-1`$ steps (here 9); the hatched
cells are the steps at which a delayed stream has nothing to predict
yet, and the numbers are frame indices. At each step of the delay
schedule the later streams see the earlier streams of the same frame,
which is what makes the single pass possible at a small cost in quality.

A 30-second clip at 50 frames per second therefore costs $`1{,}500`$
decoding steps plus three delay steps at the end, each a full
transformer pass over the growing context, which is the figure its paper
reports. Predicting the four streams one after another would cost
$`6{,}000`$ steps for the same clip, and predicting a semantic stream
first and the acoustic streams second, as MusicLM does, requires three
stages of different lengths: semantic, coarse acoustic, and fine
acoustic. The choice of pattern is therefore a choice of sequential step
count. Wall time also depends on the cost of each step, which grows with
the context even with key-value caching, and on model size, hardware,
and codec decoding. No autoregressive token model in Table 4 carries the
faster-than-real-time code. The sequential step count is one reason.
Another is that the code records only what each paper measured, and
these papers report no such measurement. The general form follows.

#### Autoregressive token language models.

An interleaving pattern assigns every token $`z^{(q)}_{n}`$ of the
$`Q\times N`$ grid to one of $`S`$ decoding steps, and the model
factorizes the joint distribution over steps,
$`p_{\theta}(z\mid c)=\prod_{s=1}^{S}\prod_{(q,n)\in P_{s}}p_{\theta}\big(z^{(q)}_{n}\mid z_{<s},c\big)`$,
where $`P_{s}`$ is the set of tokens predicted at step $`s`$ and
$`z_{<s}`$ are the tokens of the earlier steps. The tokens within one
step are predicted in parallel from the same context. The model is
trained by maximum likelihood,
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AR}}=-\sum_{s}\sum_{(q,n)\in P_{s}}\log p_{\theta}(z^{(q)}_{n}\mid z_{<s},c)`$.
When every step holds one token the factorization is the usual chain
rule over a flattened sequence $`u_{1:NQ}`$ and $`S=NQ`$. The
interleaving pattern decides which streams are predicted at each step: a
hierarchy predicts the semantic stream first and the acoustic streams
second, and the delay pattern of MusicGen offsets stream $`q`$ by
$`q-1`$ steps so that all $`Q`$ streams are predicted in one pass with a
controlled loss of quality (Copet et al., 2023). Sampling takes one
forward pass per step, so the number of sequential steps grows with the
token rate and the output length. Wall time also depends on model size,
hardware, and the cost of each pass. Key-value caching avoids
recomputing earlier states, but each new query still reads a cache that
grows with the context. Under the delay pattern $`P_{s}`$ holds one
token of each stream that has started, so a clip of $`N`$ frames costs
$`S=N+Q-1`$ steps rather than $`NQ`$, which is the arithmetic of the
example.

#### Masked and parallel decoding.

A masked model predicts a masked subset $`z_{\mathcal{M}}`$ from the
visible tokens,
$`p_{\theta}(z_{\mathcal{M}}\mid z_{\bar{\mathcal{M}}},c)`$, trained
with the cross-entropy on the masked positions for masks drawn from a
schedule. Generation starts from a fully masked sequence and unmasks in
$`S`$ iterations, keeping at each iteration the predictions of highest
confidence and re-masking the rest, so that $`S\ll NQ`$ forward passes
suffice (Chang et al., 2022; Garcia et al., 2023). Inpainting and
outpainting need no new model: the mask $`\mathcal{M}`$ is chosen to
cover the region to regenerate.

#### Diffusion.

With a latent $`h_{0}\sim p(h\mid c)`$, the forward process adds
Gaussian noise, $`h_{t}=\alpha_{t}h_{0}+\sigma_{t}\varepsilon`$ with
$`\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)`$ and a noise schedule
$`(\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t})`$, and a network $`\varepsilon_{\theta}`$ is
trained to predict the noise,

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM}}=\mathbb{E}_{t,h_{0},\varepsilon}\,\big\lVert\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{\theta}(h_{t},t,c)\big\rVert_{2}^{2}, \tag{2}
```

which is the denoising objective of DDPM (Ho et al., 2020). The
score-based view learns the score
$`\nabla_{h_{t}}\log p_{t}(h_{t}\mid c)`$ (the unconditional case drops
$`c`$), which equals $`-\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid h_{t},c]/\sigma_{t}`$
for this forward process, so the two parameterizations carry the same
information. It samples by integrating a reverse-time stochastic or
ordinary differential equation (Song et al., 2021). The EDM
parameterization gives the preconditioning and schedule choices that
several current audio latent diffusion models use (Karras et al., 2022).
Sampling integrates the reverse process over many steps, and each step
is a full network evaluation, so the number of sequential steps of
unaccelerated sampling is set by the schedule and does not grow with
output length, although the computation and memory of each step grow
with the latent sequence length, quadratically in the attention of a
diffusion transformer, whereas autoregressive decoding takes one
sequential step per token. Which of the two is slower for a given output
depends on the step count, the token rate, the model size, and the
hardware. The one comparison that holds data and architecture fixed
found no dominant paradigm (Tal et al., 2025). Conditioning is applied
with classifier-free guidance (Ho and Salimans, 2022): the network is
trained with the condition dropped at random, and at sampling time the
prediction is extrapolated,
$`\tilde{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon_{\theta}(h_{t},t,\varnothing)+w\,(\varepsilon_{\theta}(h_{t},t,c)-\varepsilon_{\theta}(h_{t},t,\varnothing))`$
with a guidance weight $`w>1`$, which trades diversity for adherence to
$`c`$.

#### Notation remark.

The three diffusion papers parameterize the same process differently.
DDPM writes the forward process with a variance schedule $`\beta_{t}`$
and the cumulative product $`\bar{\alpha}_{t}`$, so that our
$`\alpha_{t}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}`$ and
$`\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}`$, with time running over integer
steps $`1`$ to $`T`$. The score-based formulation uses continuous time
and a score network $`s_{\theta}`$, related to ours by
$`s_{\theta}(h_{t},t)=-\varepsilon_{\theta}(h_{t},t)/\sigma_{t}`$. EDM
sets $`\alpha_{t}=1`$ and indexes the process by the noise level
$`\sigma`$ alone, a convention that several audio latent diffusion
implementations follow. A paper’s “number of steps” refers to the
discretization of this continuous process at sampling time, which
training leaves open.

#### Flow matching.

Flow matching replaces the noise-prediction objective by regression onto
a velocity field along a prescribed path from noise to data. With the
linear path $`y_{t}=(1-t)\,\varepsilon+t\,h_{0}`$ for $`t\in[0,1]`$,
which runs from the noise $`y_{0}=\varepsilon`$ to the clean latent
$`y_{1}=h_{0}`$, the target velocity is $`h_{0}-\varepsilon`$ and the
objective is

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}=\mathbb{E}_{t,h_{0},\varepsilon}\,\big\lVert v_{\theta}(y_{t},t,c)-(h_{0}-\varepsilon)\big\rVert_{2}^{2}, \tag{3}
```

after which samples are drawn by integrating
$`\mathrm{d}y_{t}/\mathrm{d}t=v_{\theta}(y_{t},t,c)`$ from $`t=0`$ to
$`1`$ (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023c). The path is a choice:
flow matching admits a family of interpolations between noise and data
that includes the diffusion path, and the linear interpolation above is
the conditional optimal-transport path (Lipman et al., 2023). A straight
path between each sampled noise and data pair does not make the learned
marginal field straight, so the number of solver steps a model needs
depends on the learned geometry and on the solver. Reflow, which
retrains on the model’s own noise-to-sample pairs, straightens the
learned paths and is one optional procedure for reducing the step
count (Liu et al., 2023c). Diffusion and flow matching are the same
family in the sense that both learn a time-dependent vector field over
the latent. They differ in the path and the parameterization, which is
why the tables distinguish them as paradigms but Section 6 treats their
systems together.

#### Notation remark.

The subscript $`0`$ marks the clean latent $`h_{0}`$ throughout this
monograph, and the point on a path at time $`t`$ is written $`h_{t}`$
for diffusion and $`y_{t}`$ for flow matching, because the two run in
opposite directions: diffusion’s $`h_{t}`$ goes from data at $`t=0`$ to
noise, flow matching’s $`y_{t}`$ from noise at $`t=0`$ to data at
$`t=1`$. The flow-matching paper writes the data point $`x_{1}`$ and the
vector field $`u_{t}`$. Rectified flow writes the endpoints $`X_{0}`$
(noise) and $`X_{1}`$ (data) and the velocity $`X_{1}-X_{0}`$. These
conventions correspond to the target velocity $`h_{0}-\varepsilon`$
along the linear path used here.

#### Warning: flow matching, rectified flow, and consistency models are not one method.

Flow matching is a training objective (regression onto a velocity along
a chosen path). Rectified flow is that objective with the linear path.
Reflow is an optional later procedure that retrains on the model’s own
noise-to-sample pairs to straighten the learned paths. Consistency
models are a distillation or training method that maps any point on a
trajectory to its endpoint in one step, and they can be built on a
diffusion or a flow model. A system reporting “few-step” generation
should therefore state both its training objective and any acceleration
procedure. The post-training column of the tables records the procedures
that a paper reports.

#### Planner-renderer hybrids.

A hybrid factorizes
$`p(x\mid c)=\sum_{\pi}p(\pi\mid c)\,p(x\mid\pi,c)`$, where the plan
$`\pi`$ is a semantic token sequence, a sketch, a melody, or a
human-readable layout produced by an autoregressive language model, and
the renderer is a diffusion or flow model over a continuous latent. The
two stages are trained separately with the objectives above. At
inference the plan is sampled first and the renderer is conditioned on
it. The factorization moves long-range decisions, such as structure and
lyrics, into the discrete stage, where an autoregressive model handles
long contexts, and moves acoustic detail into the continuous stage,
where parallel sampling is cheap.

#### How the condition enters.

Three mechanisms carry $`c`$ into the networks above, and they are the
choices of slot 3. A global condition is encoded once, by a text encoder
or a contrastive audio-text model, into one or a few vectors that every
layer attends to through cross-attention, or that are added to the time
embedding of a diffusion network. A time-varying condition (chords,
beats, a melody curve, a reference stem) is encoded into a sequence
aligned with the frames of the representation. It enters as extra input
channels, as additional tokens in the context of a token model, or
through a trained branch. A ControlNet-style branch copies part of the
network, receives the control, and adds its outputs to the frozen main
network. An adapter is a small module inserted into a frozen network and
trained alone, so that the cost of a new control is the adapter’s
training run. Reference audio enters as the model’s own representation
of it, as a prefix the token model continues or as a latent the
diffusion model is conditioned on.

#### Inference-time control and inversion.

Two further routes change what a trained diffusion or flow model
produces without training it further. Inference-time optimization treats
the initial noise $`\varepsilon`$ as the free variable: for a feature
extractor $`g`$ and a target $`y^{\star}`$ (an intensity curve, a
melody, a structure), it solves
$`\min_{\varepsilon}\lVert g(\mathrm{Sample}_{\theta}(\varepsilon,c))-y^{\star}\rVert^{2}`$
by gradient descent through the sampler, which is the DITTO formulation
of Section 8. Each gradient step costs a sampling run and its backward
pass, with the memory that entails, and a distilled sampler made the
method cheap enough for interactive use in the cited work. Inversion
runs the sampler backwards: the deterministic
(ordinary-differential-equation) sampler maps noise to a latent, so
integrating it in the reverse direction from a given recording’s latent
recovers a noise $`\hat{\varepsilon}`$ that regenerates the recording,
and an edit is made by regenerating from $`\hat{\varepsilon}`$ under a
changed condition or with the attention of the network steered. Errors
accumulate along the reverse integration, which is the failure the
editing methods of Section 8 address, and inversion-free methods avoid
the reverse pass by constructing the edit from the difference between
the source-conditioned and target-conditioned predictions.

### 9 Sampling Acceleration

Sampling from a diffusion or flow model takes many network evaluations.
Several acceleration methods recur in Sections 6 to 9. _Step
distillation_ trains a student to reproduce in one step what the teacher
reaches in many. The consistency formulation (Song et al., 2023) trains
$`f_{\theta}(h_{t},t)`$ to map any point on a sampling trajectory to its
origin, with the consistency loss
$`\mathbb{E}\,d\big(f_{\theta}(h_{t_{n+1}},t_{n+1}),\,f_{\theta^{-}}(\hat{h}_{t_{n}},t_{n})\big)`$
between adjacent points, where $`\hat{h}_{t_{n}}`$ is obtained from
$`h_{t_{n+1}}`$ by one solver step and $`\theta^{-}`$ is a slowly
updated copy of $`\theta`$. The parameterization fixes
$`f_{\theta}(h_{t_{\min}},t_{\min})=h_{t_{\min}}`$ at the smallest noise
level $`t_{\min}`$, which rules out the constant function that the
agreement loss alone would admit, and the gradient is stopped through
the target branch. When the solver step uses a pretrained teacher the
procedure is consistency distillation, and when it is estimated from the
data alone it is consistency training (Song et al., 2023). _Adversarial
post-training_ adds a discriminator loss on few-step samples so that the
student’s outputs match the data distribution rather than the teacher’s
trajectory. _Layer distillation_ removes layers from the network and
fine-tunes the rest. The tables record the resulting runtime class and
whether a streaming procedure exists, because a fast batch sampler is
not a streaming one: a streaming system must emit audio for the first
chunk before the condition for later chunks is known. Consider a model
that computes each output chunk of duration $`\tau`$ in one pass, after
it has received the input it may condition on, and plays the chunks in
order. Its future visibility $`\phi`$ is the offset between the latest
input a chunk may condition on and the start of the output interval the
chunk accompanies (Wu et al., 2025e). Under this schedule the output
stream lags the input it accompanies by at least $`\phi`$ plus the
computation time of a chunk, and a new input is heard only when the next
chunk plays, up to $`\tau`$ later. Computing each chunk in less than
$`\tau`$ keeps the stream continuous. That is a throughput condition,
separate from the delay. Section 9 reports the trade-off.

#### Warning: real-time factor, latency, and future visibility measure three different things.

The real-time factor is the generation time divided by the duration of
the audio generated. A value below one says that a system produces audio
faster than the audio plays, in a batch, once the whole condition is
known. It says nothing about how soon the first sound arrives. Latency
is the time from an input to the audio that responds to it. For a
chunked streaming model it has two parts: the output stream lags the
input it accompanies by at least the future visibility $`\phi`$ plus the
computation time of a chunk, and a new input is heard only at the next
chunk boundary, up to the chunk duration $`\tau`$ later. Future
visibility is a design choice: a positive $`\phi`$ gives the model input
look-ahead at the price of a later stream, and a negative $`\phi`$ makes
it predict ahead to absorb system delay (Wu et al., 2025e). The tables
record the real-time factor where a paper reports one and mark streaming
separately, because live use requires output while the input is still
arriving, a separate property from batch speed.

### 10 Preference Alignment

Preference alignment is a post-training stage that can be applied to a
model of any paradigm, so we state it once. Given a reward $`r(x,c)`$
from human ratings or a learned judge, reinforcement learning
post-trains a generator $`p_{\theta}`$ initialized from a reference
$`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ by maximizing

```math
\mathbb{E}_{c}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c)}\big[r(x,c)\big]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c)\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid c)\big)\Big], \tag{4}
```

where $`\beta`$ controls how far the policy may move from the reference.
MusicRL applied this to a token language model with policy-gradient
optimization (Cideron et al., 2024b; Schulman et al., 2017). Direct
preference optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023) removes the
explicit reward: for a pair $`(x^{+},x^{-})`$ preferred and dispreferred
under $`c`$, the loss is

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x^{+}\mid c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x^{+}\mid c)}-\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x^{-}\mid c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x^{-}\mid c)}\right), \tag{5}
```

which diffusion models cannot evaluate directly, because their
likelihoods are not available in closed form. Diffusion-DPO derives a
surrogate from the evidence lower bound of the diffusion likelihood,
which reduces to a difference of denoising losses on the preferred and
the dispreferred sample (Wallace et al., 2024), and text-to-audio
alignment uses that construction (Majumder et al., 2024). Preference
methods for flow models use constructions of their own. The
post-training column of the tables records which of these stages a
system reports, and the evaluation section returns to the circularity
that arises when the judge that supplies $`r`$ is also the metric.

#### Notation remark.

The $`\beta`$ of the reinforcement objective and the $`\beta`$ of the
DPO loss play the same role, the weight of the pull toward the reference
model, and DPO uses the analytic relationship between a reward and the
optimal KL-regularized policy to write the pairwise preference
likelihood in terms of policy ratios, which gives a training objective
without an explicit reward model. The reference $`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ is
the model before alignment, in most music systems the supervised model
of the previous training stage. Papers write it $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$
when they borrow the language-model notation in which the generator is a
policy $`\pi`$, a symbol this monograph reserves for the plan.

#### Warning: preference optimization and adversarial post-training are different stages with similar names.

Both are applied after the main training run and both change what the
model produces, but they answer different questions. Preference
optimization moves the model toward outputs a judge or a listener
prefers, and needs ratings or a reward model. Adversarial post-training
moves a few-step sampler toward the data distribution, needs only data
and a discriminator, and is a speed method. The tables mark them with
different codes, and a system can report both, one, or neither.

### 11 Distribution-Level Metrics

The default automatic metric of the period, the Fréchet Audio
Distance (Kilgour et al., 2019), embeds generated and reference clips
with a fixed audio encoder, fits a Gaussian to each set, and reports the
Fréchet distance between the two Gaussians,

```math
\mathrm{FAD}=\lVert\mu_{g}-\mu_{r}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\mathrm{tr}\big(\Sigma_{g}+\Sigma_{r}-2(\Sigma_{g}\Sigma_{r})^{1/2}\big), \tag{6}
```

so it depends on the encoder, assumes Gaussian embeddings, and its
estimate from a small set is biased, generally upward, and noisy. The
sample covariances themselves are unbiased, but the distance is a
nonlinear function of the estimated means and covariances, and that
nonlinearity introduces the bias. The kernel alternative uses the
maximum mean discrepancy: for a kernel $`k`$ and the generated and
reference embedding distributions $`P_{g}`$ and $`P_{r}`$,
$`\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(P_{g},P_{r})=\mathbb{E}\,k(g,g^{\prime})+\mathbb{E}\,k(r,r^{\prime})-2\,\mathbb{E}\,k(g,r)`$,
and its unbiased estimator replaces each expectation by a U-statistic,
an average over distinct pairs that is unbiased for the expectation,
over the two sets (Gretton et al., 2012). On a finite sample this
unbiased estimate can be negative. The Kernel Audio Distance (KAD) is
this estimator over audio embeddings (Chung et al., 2025), unbiased in
expectation, without the Gaussian assumption, and still subject to
sampling variance. Section 11 reviews the evidence on how these
distances correlate with human judgments.

#### Interpreting a distribution distance.

Two consequences of the definitions decide how a comparison should be
read. First, both distances are computed in an embedding space, so two
papers that use different encoders, or the same encoder on different
reference sets, report numbers that cannot be compared. The tables of
Section 11 therefore name the encoder wherever a paper does. Second, the
Fréchet distance is a nonlinear function of the means and covariances
estimated from the samples, so with few generated clips the estimate is
biased, generally upward, and a system evaluated on a small set can be
penalized for the set size rather than its quality. The unbiased MMD
estimator removes that bias but not the variance, so a small evaluation
set still gives a noisy number. A reader who sees a distribution
distance reported without the encoder, the reference set, and the number
of clips has three questions to ask before believing a ranking.

### 12 Audio Representations

Models generate over one of four classes of representation. _Symbolic_
representations (MIDI, ABC) separate composition from rendering (Lu et
al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025c). _Discrete acoustic_ representations are
codec token sequences, typically from RVQ codecs (Zeghidour et al.,
2022; Défossez et al., 2023; Kumar et al., 2023), often arranged in a
semantic-then-acoustic hierarchy (Borsos et al., 2023; Agostinelli et
al., 2023). The cost of stacked codebooks has motivated single-codebook,
two-dimensional, semantically supervised, and multi-scale tokenizers (Ji
et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2025; Siuzdak et al.,
2024). _Continuous acoustic_ representations are VAE latents, mel
spectrograms, or waveforms, consumed by diffusion and flow models (Liu
et al., 2023a; Evans et al., 2024a). Recent autoencoders are designed to
be simultaneously compact, accurate in reconstruction, and semantically
structured (Evans et al., 2026). _Hybrid or sketch_ representations
insert an intermediate plan or learned sketch between the condition and
the audio, which symbolic layouts and programs can make interpretable: a
trajectory of audio-text embedding tokens (Lam et al., 2025), a coarse
semantic sketch (Yang et al., 2025a), a melody token sequence (Xu et
al., 2026), or a human-readable structural layout or program (Li et al.,
2026b; Wang et al., 2025b).

### 13 Generative Methods

The generative method is the factorization of the distribution over the
representation. Five principal paradigms account for most of the
literature (Figure 2). The paradigm code of a system follows the
training objective its paper states: a diffusion path with a noise or
v-prediction target is coded as diffusion, and the linear noise-to-data
path with the target $`h_{0}-\varepsilon`$ as flow matching, whichever
word the paper uses for its family. Both targets are called velocities
in some papers, although they differ. The post-training stages and the
order of generation are recorded separately, in the post-training,
latency, and streaming cells. The tables add four smaller groups
(inference-time optimization, language-model agents, adversarial
continuous generators, and autoencoder instruments), each described
where it occurs. The paradigms differ in how they factor the
distribution over a piece of music, and the factorization constrains
what a model can do cheaply. _Autoregressive token language models_
predict successive tokens or groups of tokens from earlier predictions,
which makes continuation and streaming natural and makes cost grow with
the token count (Agostinelli et al., 2023; Copet et al., 2023; Yang et
al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025). _Masked or parallel decoding_ predicts
many tokens at once and refines the result over a few rounds, trading
some quality for large latency reductions (Garcia et al., 2023; Ziv et
al., 2024; Parker et al., 2024). _Latent and cascaded diffusion_ starts
from noise and removes it in steps over a continuous representation, so
that every step sees the whole piece, which suits editing and
inference-time control (Huang et al., 2023a; Liu et al., 2023a; Evans et
al., 2024b; Li et al., 2025a). _Flow matching and rectified flow_
regress a velocity field along a chosen path from noise to data, and the
optimal-transport and rectified-flow variants used in music aim at
straight paths, so that fewer solver steps suffice (Prajwal et al.,
2024; Tal et al., 2024; Fei et al., 2024b), and combine with consistency
training, step and layer distillation, and adversarial post-training to
reach faster-than-real-time generation (Liu et al., 2024c; Novack et
al., 2025; Evans et al., 2026). Block-wise flow matching keeps the
objective and generates a song block by block, each block conditioned on
the ones before it (Jiang et al., 2025). _Planner-renderer hybrids_
split the task: a language model produces a plan, a sketch, a melody, or
a score, and a diffusion or flow model renders audio from it (Lam et
al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025a; Bai et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a; Xu
et al., 2026; Dai et al., 2026).

Orthogonal to all five, _preference alignment_ is an increasingly common
post-training stage, from reinforcement learning on human
feedback (Cideron et al., 2024b) to direct preference optimization
adapted to the denoising objective (Wallace et al., 2024; Majumder et
al., 2024), distributional reward optimization (Bai et al., 2025),
multi-axis preference alignment (Lei et al., 2025), multi-reward
optimization for flow matching (Ziv et al., 2026a), cross-pair schemes
that avoid model merging (Jiang et al., 2025), and diversity-preserving
distillation of guidance (Cideron et al., 2024a). A survey of alignment
methods for generative music summarizes the options and their open
problems (Herremans and Roy, 2026). A controlled comparison of the
autoregressive and flow-matching paradigms with data and architecture
held fixed finds trade-offs rather than a winner (Tal et al., 2025).

Several systems reviewed below combine paradigms to use their different
strengths. Autoregression contributes streaming and variable-length
generation. Diffusion contributes parallel refinement, editing by
inpainting, and inference-time control (Novack et al., 2024b; Evans et
al., 2026). Block-wise and chunked schemes now give diffusion models
autoregressive interfaces, including key-value caching, which keeps the
attention states of earlier chunks so they are not recomputed, for
streaming inference (Novack et al., 2026), and planner-renderer systems
give diffusion renderers the long-range structure of a language-model
plan (Li et al., 2026d).

### 14 Conditioning and Control

A condition can specify a whole piece in a few words or constrain every
beat, and the systems reviewed here span that range. The main
distinction is how the condition enters the model: a global condition
applies to the whole output and is represented by one vector or by a
sequence of embeddings that every step of generation attends to, a
time-varying condition is a sequence aligned with the audio, and a
reference recording enters either as an embedding or as audio the model
continues. _Global text_ is the default interface (Agostinelli et al.,
2023; Copet et al., 2023). _Lyrics and structure tags_ specify full
songs (Yuan et al., 2025; Ning et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026b), and
segment-level prompts allow different instructions at different points
in a song (Wang et al., 2026e). _Reference audio_ conveys melody, style,
or voice, including in-context learning from short excerpts (Copet et
al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2025; Tsai et al., 2024) and the melody of an
existing song for cover generation (Li et al., 2026c). _Time-varying
signals_ such as chords, rhythm, dynamics, intensity, and emotion curves
give bar-level and beat-level control (Wu et al., 2024b; Lin et al.,
2023; Lan et al., 2024; Tal et al., 2024; Melechovsky et al., 2024a; Mei
et al., 2025), with adapter and conditioner designs reducing the cost of
adding controls (Baker and Nistal, 2025; Tsai et al., 2025),
inference-time optimization avoiding retraining altogether (Novack et
al., 2024b; Novack et al., 2024a), and gestural interfaces accepting
hummed or sketched shapes (Garcia et al., 2025). _Structural plans_,
human-readable layouts or programs, control form directly (Li et al.,
2026b; Wang et al., 2025b). _Multimodal_ conditioning maps video or
images to music (Tian et al., 2025b; Li et al., 2024c; Lin et al.,
2026). _Interactive and streaming_ control operates while audio is being
generated, in live music models (Lyria Team et al., 2025; Novack et al., 2026) and causal accompaniment to live input (Pasini et al., 2026; Wu et
al., 2025e).

### 15 Application Classes

We distinguish six application classes, labeled T1 to T6 for brevity.
The labels index the classes and do not order them: T4 is not above T1,
and the numbering follows the order in which the classes are reviewed.
_T1, instrumental text-to-music_: clip- to track-level instrumental
generation (Copet et al., 2023; Evans et al., 2024b; Li et al., 2025a).
_T2, lyrics-to-song_: full songs with vocals (Suno, 2026b; Udio, 2026;
Yuan et al., 2025; Gong et al., 2025; Lei et al., 2025; Yang et al.,
2026b; Lei et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026). _T3, production co-creation_:
stems, accompaniment, inpainting, editing, and variations (Parker et
al., 2024; Rouard et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025b; Han et al., 2023;
Zhang et al., 2024b; Zhang et al., 2024a; Mariani et al., 2024; Nistal
et al., 2024a; Donahue et al., 2023). _T4, real-time and live
interaction_: streaming generation and accompaniment as an
instrument (Lyria Team et al., 2025; Novack et al., 2026; Pasini et al.,
2026; Novack et al., 2025). _T5, cross-modal scoring_: music for video,
images, and games (Tian et al., 2025b; Su et al., 2024; Ji et al.,
2026). _T6, unified understanding and generation_: systems that analyze,
discuss, and generate music in one interface, including audio-language
models and agents that control a generative backend (Liu et al., 2023b;
Yang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2026b).

The classes are defined by the task a system is built and evaluated for,
and a system can belong to more than one. Three rules fix the placement.
First, a system is tabulated once, in its primary class, which is the
task of its main evaluation. Secondary memberships are stated in the
text. Second, the boundaries follow the output and the condition rather
than the architecture. A model that generates vocals from lyrics is T2
whatever its paradigm. A model prompted with audio rather than text is
T3 even when it was built as a general generator. A model is T4 only if
it emits audio under a stated latency bound while receiving input.
Third, where two classes apply equally, the class with the stricter
constraint wins (T4 over T3, T2 over T1), so that the table records the
result under the more specific constraint. The hard cases show the rules
at work. SingSong generates accompaniment for a given vocal, so its main
evaluation is an audio-to-audio task and it is tabulated with the stem
and accompaniment systems of T3, although Section 7 keeps it as the
first decomposition of the song problem. VampNet is a general masked
generator prompted with audio, so it is tabulated with the editing
systems of T3 rather than with T1. Shao is trained with lyrics and
generates vocal songs, so it is tabulated in T2 although its design
question, a deep token hierarchy with super-resolution in the same token
space, belongs to T1. Presto is a distillation method whose paper
evaluates offline text-to-music generation, so it is tabulated in T1
although Section 9 cites it for the speed that live use needs. The six
classes were fixed before the structured searches of Section 2 were run,
on the basis of the landmark systems and the functional categories of
Table 2, and the boundary rules above were added after the first
screening pass exposed the cases just listed. No class was added or
merged afterwards. Most new systems addressed T1 in 2023 and T2 by 2025.
Figure 6 and Tables 4 to 6 show the distribution of the reviewed systems
across classes and methods.

### 18 Relation to Earlier Surveys

Several earlier taxonomies emphasized symbolic generation. We relate
their organizing questions to the audio systems reviewed here. The
five-dimension analysis of Briot, Hadjeres, and Pachet asks what is
generated, how it is represented, which architecture generates it, and
what challenges and strategies apply (Briot et al., 2020). Our D1 and D2
correspond to its representation and architecture dimensions, and D3 to
D5 replace its challenge and strategy dimensions with the axes that
distinguish released systems: what a system can be told, what it is for,
and what was released. The functional taxonomy of Herremans, Chuan, and
Chew indexes systems by the musical dimension they generate (melody,
harmony, rhythm, timbre, interaction, narrative, difficulty) (Herremans
et al., 2017). That index remains useful for systems that generate
particular musical dimensions, and its interaction category anticipates
our class T4. Full-mixture audio generators often combine melody,
harmony, rhythm, and timbre in one output, and we distinguish them by
input, paradigm, and artifact. The three-level organization of Ji, Luo,
and Yang (score, performance, audio) places all of our systems in its
audio level (Ji et al., 2020). The foundation-model survey of Ma et al.
organizes by pretraining paradigm and downstream task across
understanding and generation (Ma et al., 2024). Our D2 refines its
generative paradigms and our D4 and D5 add the application and artifact
axes.

The modality-centered survey of Li et al. organizes systems by guidance
modality, single, cross, and multi-modal, across symbolic and audio
output, and records for each representative method its modeling
approach, modal encoding, fusion, and architecture (Li et al., 2026a).
Our D3 covers modality as one of five dimensions, and Tables 4 to 6
record the released artifact (D5) rather than the fusion design. The
video-to-music survey of Ji et al. decomposes that branch into
conditioning input construction, conditioning mechanism, and generation
framework, and tabulates demos, generation lengths, model sizes, and
training resources for its systems (Ji et al., 2026). The three
components map onto our D3 and D2, and its table is the branch-level
counterpart of ours. The evaluation survey of Lerch et al. groups
evaluation targets into system output and model use and reviews
subjective, objective, and HCI methodologies (Lerch et al., 2025).
Section 11 points to it for methodology and covers only the instruments
the reviewed systems report. The functional grouping of Table 2 is the
bridge between these organizations and ours.

## Chapter 6 Instrumental Text-to-Music

Instrumental text-to-music (class T1) was the task addressed by the 2023
systems, and it is the class in which the paradigms can be compared
without the added problems of vocals and lyrics. A description specifies
the music and the system produces an excerpt, so the comparison asks how
each method handles sequence length, sampling cost, and control.
Reported quality also depends on training data and evaluation, and
cannot be attributed to the model family alone. Table 4 lists the
systems reviewed in this section with their taxonomy placement and
released-artifact attributes. Figure 5 places the landmark systems of
this and the next class on a common time axis.

### 19 Autoregressive Token Language Models

MusicLM established a hierarchical codec-token approach to text-to-music
generation, and the token-based systems of Tables 4 and 5 keep its main
elements (Agostinelli et al., 2023). A MuLan embedding of the text
prompt conditions a first language model that predicts semantic tokens
(in the original, units from w2v-BERT, a self-supervised speech
encoder), a second model predicts the coarse SoundStream codebooks given
the semantic tokens, and a third predicts the fine codebooks. The
SoundStream decoder renders audio. The hierarchy separates long-range
structure, which the semantic stream carries, from acoustic detail, and
it lets each stage run at its own sequence length. MusicLM also released
MusicCaps, the caption set that became the default text-to-music
benchmark. The model was not released.

MusicGen then showed that the three-stage hierarchy can be replaced by a
single model (Copet et al., 2023). A single transformer predicts EnCodec
tokens under one of several codebook interleaving patterns. The delay
pattern, which offsets each codebook by one step, lets one
autoregressive pass predict all codebooks with a small quality cost
relative to fully sequential decoding. The model adds melody
conditioning through a chromagram (a per-frame profile of energy over
the twelve pitch classes) of a reference recording, was released with
weights and code, and is the most common open baseline among the papers
reviewed here: it appears as a baseline or a backbone in the control,
editing, stem, and adaptation work of Section 8 (Lin et al., 2023; Lan
et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024a; Rouard et al., 2025; Lin et al.,
2024; Mehta et al., 2025a).

The systems discussed next illustrate two approaches to acoustic detail,
and the choice between them is the planning question Q4 in its earliest
form. The first keeps the language model for what it does well,
long-range structure, and delegates acoustic detail to a separate
renderer. MeLoDy reduced sampling cost by rendering semantic tokens with
a dual-path diffusion model (Lam et al., 2023), and InspireMusic pairs
an autoregressive model with a flow-matching super-resolution stage for
long-form 48 kHz output (Zhang et al., 2025a). MusiCoT has the model
generate an analyzable structural sketch, a trajectory of audio-text
embedding tokens, before the audio tokens, which its authors report
improves global structure. The method is the basis of a commercial
system (Lam et al., 2025). The second direction keeps everything in one
acoustic token space and scales it, accepting long sequences for the
sake of a single model. UniAudio includes music in a single language
model with universal token input and output across audio tasks (Yang et
al., 2024). Shao builds a deep RVQ hierarchy and generates coarse tokens
for the whole track before a super-resolution model completes the finer
codebooks within the same token space. It is trained with lyrics and
generates vocal songs, so it appears in Table 5, but its design question
is the one of this section (Liu et al., 2026a). Codec design constrains
what a token model can express: codecs trained for reconstruction alone
may represent semantic information in a form that language models
predict inefficiently, which motivated semantically supervised codecs
(Section 3) (Ye et al., 2025), and codecs with frequency-ordered or
multi-scale codebooks make truncating the codebook stack a controlled
loss (Yang et al., 2026c; Siuzdak et al., 2024). A controlled comparison
that holds data and architecture fixed and varies only the modeling
paradigm found that autoregressive and flow-matching models trade off
differently on quality, speed, and controllability, so the choice
between them depends on the deployment target rather than on one being
dominant (Tal et al., 2025).

### 20 Masked and Parallel Decoding

Masked token modeling generates all positions iteratively rather than
left to right. VampNet showed that one masked model covers generation,
inpainting, outpainting, and variations by changing the mask (Garcia et
al., 2023). Because it is prompted with audio rather than text, it is
tabulated with the editing systems in Table 6. MAGNeT reached
near-autoregressive quality at substantially lower latency with a pure
non-autoregressive schedule over EnCodec codebooks (Ziv et al., 2024).
The approach was extended to accompaniment, where StemGen generates a
stem conditioned on an existing mix (Parker et al., 2024). Masked
decoding has not been used for full songs in the systems reviewed here
(Figure 6). The song systems that need parallel generation use diffusion
or flow matching over continuous latents instead.

### 21 Diffusion and Flow Matching

Diffusion models reached music by two routes, waveform cascades at
industrial scale and compressed latent representations, which reduced
the computation needed and supported several open implementations.
Noise2Music demonstrated text-to-music with cascaded waveform diffusion
at industrial scale, with pseudo-captions generated by a language
model (Huang et al., 2023a). AudioLDM diffuses over mel-VAE latents with
CLAP conditioning and renders with a vocoder (Liu et al., 2023a). Other
latent models released in this period included Make-An-Audio with prompt
enhancement (Huang et al., 2023b), ERNIE-Music with weakly supervised
text-music pairs (Zhu et al., 2023a), AudioLDM 2 with one latent for
speech, sound, and music (Liu et al., 2024b), Moûsai with long contexts
under strong compression (Schneider et al., 2024), MusicLDM with
beat-synchronous mixup (Chen et al., 2024b), and JEN-1 at 48 kHz
stereo (Li et al., 2024a).

The Stable Audio series exemplifies the design that several later
systems use: a diffusion transformer over a highly compressed continuous
latent, with the same or a derived autoencoder (Ning et al., 2025; Wang
et al., 2026e; Chen et al., 2026a). Timing conditioning informs the
model of its position within a track, enabling structured 95-second
stereo pieces (Evans et al., 2024a). A diffusion transformer over a
compressed latent extended this to full-length tracks with coherent
long-range structure (Evans et al., 2024b). Stable Audio Open is an
open-weights reference model with documented Creative Commons training
sources (Evans et al., 2025). Stable Audio 3 added a semantic-acoustic
autoencoder, variable-length generation, inpainting-based editing, and
adversarial post-training for few-step inference, with open small and
medium weights and a commercial tier (Evans et al., 2026; Stability AI,
2026). Orthogonal improvements target data quality, with quality-aware
masked diffusion transformers steerable toward high-quality output from
noisy corpora (Li et al., 2025a). The latent autoencoder is a separate
design decision: decoders at high compression lose high frequencies,
phase coherence, and stereo image, and complex-spectral autoencoders
with per-band correction address these failures (Wang et al., 2026b).

Flow matching replaces the diffusion objective with a velocity
regression along a chosen path, and the variants used in music choose
the linear path and reach few solver steps with reflow or distillation.
MusicFlow reached comparable quality with smaller, faster cascaded
models and native infilling (Prajwal et al., 2024), FluxMusic adapted
the rectified-flow transformer design from image generation (Fei et al.,
2024b), and JASCO used flow matching for temporally precise joint
conditioning (Tal et al., 2024). Sampling cost falls further with
consistency and distillation methods: consistency training reaches
few-step music generation (Fei et al., 2024a), latent consistency models
do the same for text-to-audio (Liu et al., 2024c), flow matching with
preference optimization gives fast and faithful text-to-audio
generation (Hung et al., 2024), and Presto distills both sampling steps
and network layers, reaching faster-than-real-time latent
diffusion (Novack et al., 2025). Instrument-level synthesis with
explicit playing techniques (Tian et al., 2026) and unified
anything-to-audio generation (Tian et al., 2025a) are neighboring tasks
that share these architectures.

### 22 Lessons and Open Trade-offs

This class poses the simplest form of the generation problem: produce
seconds to minutes of instrumental audio from a global text condition in
the format of a studio recording. It is the class in which the
representation and cost questions are answered most directly. The
tabulated answers are autoregressive language models over codec tokens,
masked token models, and diffusion or flow matching over continuous
latents, with planner-renderer designs as a fourth once MeLoDy delegated
rendering to a diffusion model (Lam et al., 2023). The evidence that
separates them is uneven. Reported runtime gives the most concrete
distinction, although the reported values depend on the hardware and
configuration of each measurement. Six rows of Table 4 carry the
faster-than-real-time code: four diffusion or flow models, with or
without distillation (Evans et al., 2024a; Evans et al., 2024b; Novack
et al., 2025; Evans et al., 2026), one planner-renderer design with a
diffusion renderer (Lam et al., 2023), and one masked token model,
MAGNeT, whose paper gives its speed relative to autoregressive
decoding (Ziv et al., 2024). No autoregressive token model reports such
a measurement. On control the reviewed corpus shows a pattern rather
than a general result: adapters were built on token models, largely
because MusicGen’s weights were available, which is why much of the
control literature of Section 8 was built on it (Copet et al., 2023),
and gradient-based control at inference time was built on diffusion
models (Novack et al., 2024b). Neither route is exclusive to its
paradigm. On quality the evidence is weakest: the one study that holds
data and architecture fixed and varies only the paradigm found no
dominant choice (Tal et al., 2025), and every other comparison in this
section confounds the paradigm with the training set, the codec or
autoencoder, the model size, and the metric, none of which is held
constant across papers. Output quality and the effect of training data
cannot be compared from the table at all. The systems report different
metrics over different embeddings (Section 11), and five of the 22 rows
carry only the data code U, with no named source and no license, so a
difference between two rows may be a difference in data.

A reader who starts from a practical constraint can take three rules
from the table. For an open backbone to fine-tune or adapt, MusicGen
remains the common choice because the adapters and controls that exist
were built for it. For long-form stereo output with a documented
training set, the Stable Audio reports provide examples with documented
training-source statements, from the Creative Commons model (Evans et
al., 2025) to the licensed and screened corpus of its third
generation (Evans et al., 2026). For few-step or faster-than-real-time
generation, several diffusion or flow systems reach these speeds through
distillation or adversarial post-training. The table also includes
faster-than-real-time masked and hybrid systems. The open trade-off of
the class is the autoencoder. Compression sets the latent sequence
length and with it part of the sampling cost, and the losses at high
compression, in high frequencies, phase coherence, and stereo image, are
documented (Wang et al., 2026b) but have not been traded against
sampling cost in a controlled study. The choice between a codec with
semantic supervision (Ye et al., 2025) and one with frequency-ordered
codebooks (Yang et al., 2026c) is likewise made per system and not
compared.

## Chapter 7 Song Generation

Full songs with vocals (class T2) became the most active class of this
collection in 2024 and 2025. The task adds lyric-to-vocal alignment and
coordination between vocals and accompaniment. These relationships must
remain coherent over a full song, often several minutes long. The words
must align with the singing, the voice must stay consistent, and the
accompaniment and the sections must support the same song, so longer
output is only part of the problem. The systems differ in how they
divide these responsibilities between representations, generation
stages, and conditioning. Table 5 lists the systems reviewed here.

### 23 Commercial Systems and Compositional Precursors

The early commercial song generators disclosed little about their
architectures. Suno and Udio demonstrated full-song generation without
technical reports (Suno, 2026b; Udio, 2026). Seed-Music documented an
industrial framework that mixes token language models and diffusion and
adds vocal cloning and note-level editing (Bai et al., 2024). Mureka
offers the chain-of-thought planning of MusiCoT as a product (Lam et
al., 2025). Academic work reached songs by a different route: it
decomposed the song into parts that existing models could handle, and
the decompositions are instructive because later single-model systems
had to solve the same sub-problems internally. One decomposition took
the vocals as given and generated the accompaniment: SingSong did this
with an AudioLM-style token stack (Donahue et al., 2023), and FastSAG
replaced its autoregressive stages with non-autoregressive diffusion
over mel spectrograms to make it fast (Chen et al., 2024a). Another
generated the vocals first and the accompaniment second. Two-stage
text-to-song did so with a tri-tower contrastive pretraining that
supplies the text representation for the accompaniment stage (Hong et
al., 2024). MelodyLM put a MIDI melody before the vocals, so that the
text controls an explicit melody representation before audio rendering,
and rendered the accompaniment with latent diffusion (Li et al., 2024b).
The Chord-Conditioned Song Generator fed extracted chords into an
end-to-end model through a cross-attention mechanism robust to
extraction errors (Gao et al., 2024). The decomposition then moved
inside one model: SongCreator modeled vocals and accompaniment as two
interacting token sequences in one language model, covering generation,
continuation, and editing (Lei et al., 2024), and SongEditor added
segment-wise and track-wise editing of lyrics, vocals, and accompaniment
to a language-model song generator (Yang et al., 2025b).

### 24 Open Systems

Open models reached the same task in 2025, within a year of the
commercial products, and some of them provided inspectable and
extensible baselines. They split along the two lineages of Section 6. On
the token side, YuE scaled a LLaMA-style language model to five-minute
songs with track-decoupled next-token prediction, in which vocals and
accompaniment are predicted as separate token tracks rather than as one
mixed track, and added in-context style transfer and a memorization
analysis. Its authors present it as the first open lyrics-to-song
foundation model (Yuan et al., 2025). SongGen reached single-stage
generation by interleaving vocal and accompaniment tokens, with mixed
and dual-track output modes (Liu et al., 2025d). On the continuous side,
DiffRhythm generated full songs end to end in seconds with flow matching
over a continuous latent, which is possible because every sampling step
treats the whole song at once (Ning et al., 2025). DiffRhythm+ added
style control and preference optimization (Chen et al., 2025). ACE-Step
released an open flow-matching model for songs, which its successor in
Section 25 extends (Gong et al., 2025). VersBand decoupled the vocal and
accompaniment models under prompt-based control (Zhang et al., 2025e).
LeVo showed what post-training adds: it aligned a token language model
to several human preference axes with direct preference
optimization (Lei et al., 2025), and its successor targets stability and
melodic quality through hierarchical representation modeling and
progressive post-training (Lei et al., 2026). Several projects also make
reproducibility an explicit goal: Muse releases a song generator
together with a licensed synthetic training set and the training and
evaluation pipeline (Jiang et al., 2026), and the ICME 2026 grand
challenge on academic text-to-music generation requires training from
scratch on a Creative Commons subset of MTG-Jamendo, for ten-second
instrumental clips (Hsieh et al., 2026).

### 25 Planning, Scale, and Post-Training

The two lineages, token language models and latent diffusion, were
combined most often in this class, and the combinations show what each
side contributes: the token model supplies a sketch of the whole song,
and the diffusion or flow model supplies the acoustic detail that is
costly to predict in long token sequences. SongBloom interleaves
autoregressive sketching with patch-wise diffusion refinement, so that
each sketched segment is rendered before the next is sketched (Yang et
al., 2025a). DiffRhythm 2 is semi-autoregressive block flow matching:
the song is generated block by block, each block by flow matching
conditioned on the blocks before it, which recovers lyric alignment
without external labels. The same work introduces cross-pair
optimization for multi-preference alignment (Jiang et al., 2025).
SketchSong plans a song-level sketch along time and models the parts
separately along the track axis (Duan et al., 2026a). Two results show
the range of scale and openness: Jam shows that a small flow-matching
model suffices for full songs with word-level lyric control (Liu et al.,
2025c), and HeartMuLa releases its model, codec, and training procedure,
with multilingual conditioning and a reinforcement-refined release (Yang
et al., 2026b). The YuE group released YuE2 on 9 September 2026,
followed by a technical report on 27 September (Multimodal Art
Projection, 2026; Yuan et al., 2026). Its autoregressive and
non-autoregressive mixture-of-transformers backbone generates an
editable score and semantic tokens, which condition a flow-matching
renderer for 48 kHz stereo audio. The weights were released under CC
BY-NC 4.0.

Among the 2026 industrial reports in Table 5, ACE-Step 1.5, Qwen-Music,
and the eight-codebook report describe a planner and a renderer, and
WanSong describes a single flow model without a planner. The three
planner-renderer systems differ in the plan, the renderer, and the
training procedure, and Qwen-Music reports the largest training corpus,
in hours, of any row in Table 5. ACE-Step 1.5 extended the open ACE-Step
line and put a language-model planner in front of its generator (Gong et
al., 2026a). Qwen-Music pairs a 3B semantic-token language model with a
diffusion-transformer renderer, trained over more than five million
hours of multilingual audio. Its addition is a melody-token chain of
thought, a melody sequence the planner writes before the song, which
plans the vocal contour and also serves as an interface for melody
cloning and cover generation. The authors report blind expert tests in
which it matches the strongest closed commercial systems (Xu et al.,
2026). A second report describes a semantic-aware eight-codebook
tokenizer, a hybrid language-model planner, and a FullDiT renderer
covering lyrics-to-song, instrumental, and cover generation (Dai et al.,
2026). Its renderer is described separately, and that description states
the central difficulty of the planner-renderer design (Figure 7): in
training the renderer sees clean token plans, at inference it sees the
planner’s imperfect ones, and a renderer trained without regard to this
exposure bias degrades. The proposed remedy is to train the renderer for
full-context generation from imperfect plans (Li et al., 2026d). WanSong
instead generates the song directly with one rectified-flow model and
produces multilingual songs of several minutes with separate vocal and
accompaniment stems in one pass. It relies on step distillation for
speed (Chen et al., 2026a).

Figure 7: The planner-renderer data path and where its training and
inference differ. In training (top), the planner learns to predict a
plan $`\pi`$ (semantic tokens, a melody, or a score) from the condition,
and the renderer learns to produce audio from plans taken from the data,
which are clean. At inference (bottom), the renderer receives the
planner’s own prediction $`\hat{\pi}`$, which contains errors the
renderer never saw in training. This mismatch is the exposure bias that
the full-context renderer of Section 25 is trained against. It is the
cost of the separation that gives the design its long-range structure.

Once a system can generate a whole song, the next problem is controlling
what happens inside it, and much of the recent work on this class
addresses that problem with the conditioning forms of Section 14 applied
along the song’s timeline. SegTune broadcasts segment-level prompts to
their time windows, so that a verse and a chorus can receive different
descriptions, and predicts sentence-level lyric timestamps with a
language model (Wang et al., 2026e). Cover-song generation conditions on
the melody of an existing song and regenerates vocals and
accompaniment (Li et al., 2026c), with explicit phoneme conditioning
reducing lyric errors (Lee et al., 2026b). MIDI-informed accompaniment
lets songwriters retain direct control over the melody they supply by
conditioning on vocal MIDI, chords, and structure (Tsai et al., 2026),
and UniSinger unifies speaker-cloning song generation with singing voice
conversion in one multimodal diffusion transformer (Zhang et al.,
2026c). Score-first pipelines through editable bar-level notation (Wang
et al., 2026d) and video-synchronized song generation (Wang et al.,
2026c) lie at the boundary of the class.

Preference alignment has become common in this class: 11 of the 25 song
systems of Table 5 first released in 2025 and 2026 report such a stage
(the tables date a system by its first public release, which can precede
the year of the cited version). The stage suits songs because errors in
lyrics, unstable vocals, and unsatisfactory melodies can be easier for
listeners to recognize than to specify in a training loss. Of the
systems in Table 5, DiffRhythm+, LeVo, LeVo 2, DiffRhythm 2, Jam,
SegTune, HeartMuLa, WanSong, Qwen-Music, the Dai et al. framework, and
ACE-Step 1.5 report a preference or reinforcement stage, as does the
2024 Seed-Music framework, which is outside the count of 11, while YuE,
SongGen, DiffRhythm, ACE-Step, SongBloom, SketchSong, Muse, and Shao
report none (the table marks adversarial post-training separately).
Several reports combine supervised initialization, offline preference
optimization, and online policy optimization in sequence (Xu et al.,
2026; Lei et al., 2026; Dai et al., 2026). Alignment targets have
widened from overall preference to specific failure modes:
phoneme-error-based preference data against lyric hallucination (Zhang
et al., 2025b), multiple off-the-shelf rewards for text alignment,
production quality, and semantic consistency in flow-matching
generators (Ziv et al., 2026a), distributional rewards that match a
target distribution rather than a scalar (Bai et al., 2025), and open
pairwise preference metrics built for alignment (Kim et al., 2026d).
Alignment can also reduce output diversity, which makes diversity
preservation a separate design concern (Cideron et al., 2024a).

### 26 Lessons and Open Trade-offs

Song generation adds constraints to the instrumental problem: the vocals
must follow the lyrics, the accompaniment and vocals must remain
coordinated, and these relations must hold over a full song. Three
answers are tabulated:

- •
  a language model over interleaved or decoupled vocal and accompaniment
  tokens (Yuan et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025d);
- •
  end-to-end diffusion or flow over the whole song (Ning et al., 2025;
  Gong et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026a), within which step distillation
  is a sampling choice rather than a different model (Chen et al.,
  2026a), and semi-autoregressive block generation a variant that
  decodes the song in order (Jiang et al., 2025);
- •
  planner-renderer hybrids in which a token model writes a sketch, a
  melody, or a score and a diffusion or flow model renders it (Yang et
  al., 2025a; Xu et al., 2026; Dai et al., 2026; Multimodal Art
  Projection, 2026).

Errors in lyric alignment are a useful point of comparison between the
paradigms: token models carry the lyrics in the sequence, diffusion
models need the alignment supplied or recovered, DiffRhythm 2 recovers
it without labels (Jiang et al., 2025), and lyric hallucination has
become a target of preference data in both lineages (Zhang et al.,
2025b). Sampling cost separates them as in Section 6: the flow-matching
systems report full songs in seconds (Ning et al., 2025; Gong et al.,
2025), and the sketch-then-render system that reports a real-time factor
is slower than real time at full size (Yang et al., 2025a). Structure
separates the planner-renderer designs from the rest, and it is the main
response of this class to the planning question: several of the 2026
industrial reports use an explicit plan (Xu et al., 2026; Dai et al.,
2026), and the exposure bias between the clean plans of training and the
predicted plans of inference is the cost that one of them trains
against (Dai et al., 2026). The limits of cross-system comparison are
especially pronounced for songs. The industrial systems are trained on
undisclosed corpora of five million hours and more and the academic
systems on tens of thousands of hours or on a released synthetic
corpus (Jiang et al., 2026), so a quality difference between rows may
partly reflect a data difference. The evaluation batteries differ by
report. Differences in evaluation protocols further limit independent
comparison. For ten of the 33 rows of Table 5 the paper states nothing
about released weights.

The practical choices follow the constraint a reader starts from. For
training from scratch at academic scale, Muse releases a song pipeline
and a corpus (Jiang et al., 2026). The ICME challenge fixes a comparable
setting for ten-second instrumental clips (Hsieh et al., 2026).
Fine-tuning an open backbone has ACE-Step and YuE as the common bases
and HeartMuLa as the family that releases codec and training procedure
as well as weights (Gong et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Yang et al.,
2026b). The commercial systems of Table 5 are closed, and the tables do
not compare their quality with that of the open ones. Preference
alignment can also reduce output diversity, making diversity
preservation a separate design concern (Cideron et al., 2024a). Which
signals the alignment reward should contain remains an open question.
The contents of the plan are chosen per system, a melody token
stream (Xu et al., 2026), an editable score (Multimodal Art Projection,
2026), or a semantic sketch (Yang et al., 2025a), without a comparison
of what each gives the renderer and the user.

## Chapter 8 Control, Editing, and Co-Creation

Production use (class T3) requires more than a prompt: time-varying
control, targeted edits, and stem-level operations. A request such as
“change the instrument but keep the melody” specifies a change and a
constraint, and a useful method must satisfy both. This section compares
how controls enter a generator, how an existing recording is represented
during editing, and where the cost is paid: in additional training, in
per-edit computation, or in limits on what the model can change. Table 6
lists the control, editing, stem, and live systems of this section and
of Section 9.

### 27 Time-Varying Control

Time-varying controls specify how a piece should change over time: a
chord sequence, a melody line, or a dynamics curve. A generator trained
with text conditioning aligns such a signal with its audio output
through an additional mechanism, and the first approach learns that
mechanism during training. Music ControlNet adapted the ControlNet
design to music: a control branch receives the time-varying signal and
feeds the diffusion generator, giving melody, dynamics, and rhythm
controls (Wu et al., 2024b). For token language models, Coco-Mulla adds
chord and drum conditioning with a parameter-efficient adapter on a
frozen backbone (Lin et al., 2023), MusiConGen reaches practical rhythm
and chord control by fine-tuning MusicGen (Lan et al., 2024), and a
heterogeneous adapter with a masking scheme gives MusicGen inpainting
together with frame-level content controls (Lin et al., 2024). JASCO
conditions flow matching jointly on symbolic signals and audio stems
with temporal precision (Tal et al., 2024), and Mustango takes a
different route to the same end, providing control through the prompt
itself by predicting chords, beats, tempo, and key from rich
captions (Melechovsky et al., 2024a). Several designs reduce the
additional training needed to support a control. LiLAC replaces
ControlNet-style branches with lightweight modules (Baker and Nistal,
2025), and MuseControlLite reaches multiple conditions with small
conditioners built on positionally enhanced cross attention (Tsai et
al., 2025). AP-Adapter adds audio-prompt control to a frozen diffusion
model (Tsai et al., 2024), and adapter studies compare architecture,
placement, and size for low-resource adaptation (Mehta et al., 2025a;
Zou et al., 2024).

The second approach keeps the pretrained generator fixed: DITTO
optimizes the initial diffusion noise at inference time, by gradient
descent through the sampler, to improve agreement with an intensity,
melody, or structure target (Novack et al., 2024b), and DITTO-2 reduces
the cost of this optimization by separately training a distilled
sampler (Novack et al., 2024a). Sketch2Sound extends the interface to
vocal imitations and sketched control curves (Garcia et al., 2025), and
LARA-Gen adds continuous valence-arousal emotion control by aligning
hidden states with a music understanding model (Mei et al., 2025). A
third approach modifies generation without updating the generator’s
weights or optimizing each output: once a concept direction has been
identified inside a pretrained generator, activation steering applies it
directly, although finding the direction may itself require training a
sparse autoencoder or an activation-patching analysis (Singh et al.,
2025; Facchiano et al., 2025; Narashiman et al., 2025; Zhao et al.,
2025).

#### Running example.

Several MusicGen variants illustrate the different costs of adding
control. Coco-Mulla keeps MusicGen frozen and trains an adapter that
reads chords and drums (Lin et al., 2023). MusiConGen fine-tunes the
backbone itself for rhythm and chords (Lan et al., 2024).
Instruct-MusicGen adds instruction following to it (Zhang et al.,
2024a). A heterogeneous adapter gives it inpainting together with
frame-level controls (Lin et al., 2024). MusicGen’s melody-conditioned
variant accepts a chromagram as a control input. The variants differ in
where they introduce the control sequence and in which parameters they
update: the adapter methods keep MusicGen frozen, and the adapter itself
still needs a training run, which is the cost the later lightweight
conditioners of this section reduce.

A global text prompt can ask for a chorus or a returning motif but gives
little direct control over their timing, and two recent systems provide
more explicit structural control by generating a plan before the audio.
MusicLayout generates a time-aligned layout of sections, textures,
repetitions, and arrangement before the audio (Li et al., 2026b), and
MusicWeaver predicts a multi-level song program encoding form, motif
recurrence, and bar-level attributes, with edits formalized as typed
operations on the program (Wang et al., 2025b). Both are
planner-renderer systems in the sense of Section 13, with a
human-readable plan.

### 28 Editing and Instruction Following

Editing an existing recording, rather than generating a new one, is
approached by three families of methods, which differ in how they obtain
the requested change while preserving the source recording. Some learn
editing from examples or train additional modules. Others use a
pretrained generator to reconstruct or transform the input at inference,
by inversion into the model’s generation process, by a directly
constructed editing trajectory, or by an intervention on attention. The
routes overlap, so the comparison below states what is trained, how the
source enters, and what is changed during generation. The first family
learns editing from examples: instruction-tuned models are trained on
triplets of a recording, an instruction, and the edited result. AUDIT
introduced the formulation for general audio (Wang et al., 2023),
InstructME frames editing and remixing as instruction following over
latent diffusion, covering stem addition, removal, and replacement (Han
et al., 2023), and Instruct-MusicGen brings instruction tuning to token
language models (Zhang et al., 2024a).

A second family uses inversion. The recording is mapped back to the
latent or the noise from which a pretrained generator would have
produced it. Generation is then steered toward the requested edit. These
methods can use a pretrained generator without task-specific editor
training. MusicMagus changes instrument, genre, or mood by steering
latents (Zhang et al., 2024b). DDPM inversion gives text-based and
unsupervised editing directions (Manor and Michaeli, 2024). MEDIC
disentangles the inversion to reduce the error that accumulates when a
recording is inverted and regenerated (Liu et al., 2025b). MelodyFlow
adapts latent inversion to flow matching over a stereo latent codec (Le
Lan et al., 2024). MusRec and FlowSonic invert real recordings with
rectified-flow diffusion transformers (Boudaghi and Zare, 2025; Boudaghi
and Zare, 2026). SteerMusic uses score distillation for consistency (Niu
et al., 2026). Melodia and AnchorSteer intervene on attention maps and
on self-discovered concept vectors to preserve rhythm and melody while
changing attributes (Yang et al., 2026d; Chang et al., 2026).

A third family edits without updating the generator’s weights or first
inverting the recording. Inversion-free methods construct the editing
path directly from the difference between source- and target-conditioned
predictions (Fu et al., 2026; Ge et al., 2026), and EditGen steers the
cross-attention of a frozen autoregressive model (Sioros et al., 2025).
Two capabilities cut across the families. Inpainting-capable generators
cover targeted regeneration and continuation directly, by regenerating
only a masked region (Garcia et al., 2023; Evans et al., 2026), and
similarity-guided posterior sampling extends inpainting to multi-second
gaps (Turland et al., 2025). What an edit should leave unchanged has its
own evaluation framework (Vishe et al., 2025), and instruction-based
editing across audio modalities has a benchmark (Ma et al., 2026b).

### 29 Stems and Accompaniment

A stem is an audio track containing one instrument, voice, or group of
parts from a mix. Stem-level generation asks a model to produce one part
given the others, or all parts so that they can be mixed separately.
Early examples include SingSong’s vocal-conditioned
accompaniment (Donahue et al., 2023) and StemGen’s context-aware
stems (Parker et al., 2024). Later systems approach the task in three
ways. The first design generates and edits named stems: MusicGen-Stem
extends one autoregressive model to drums, bass, and other stems (Rouard
et al., 2025), and JEN-1 Composer generates or completes multiple tracks
jointly on a diffusion stack (Yao et al., 2025b). The second treats the
stems as parallel channels of one distribution, so that generation,
separation, and accompaniment are all the same conditional inference.
Multi-source diffusion unifies generation, source separation, and
partial accompaniment over parallel stems (Mariani et al., 2024), and
Multi-Track MusicLDM extends a latent diffusion model to
arrangement-aware multi-track generation (Karchkhadze et al., 2025).
MGE-LDM learns a joint latent distribution over mixtures, submixtures,
and stems, so that generation, imputation, and text-conditioned
extraction are all conditional inpainting (Chae and Lee, 2025). The
third design generates one accompaniment track conditioned on the
remaining mix, as in Diff-A-Riff (Nistal et al., 2024a). Diff-A-Riff is
trained on licensed data (Nistal et al., 2024a). The three designs
differ in the data they need. In Table 6, the systems that treat stems
as parallel channels are trained on public research datasets, and the
named-stem and mix-conditioned systems on in-house or licensed catalogs,
so the design a reader can reproduce depends on the multi-track data
available.

Beyond generation, the production steps themselves are being modeled:
Diff2Mix controls mixing with diffusion models and differentiable
effects (Zong et al., 2026), and RIME formalizes agentic
post-production, in which individual aspects of a song are targeted and
refined in sequence (Schaffer and Singh, 2026). All stems of a track can
also be generated at once with flexible conditioning (Wu et al., 2026b),
hierarchical autoregressive foundation models target accompaniment
generation as a task in its own right (Zhu et al., 2026), and
MIDI-to-music synthesis with optional prompt MIDI unifies rendering and
local editing (Jing et al., 2026).

A language model can also sit above these tools and call them in
sequence on the user’s behalf. M2UGen connects music encoders and
generators to a large language model for chat-style understanding and
generation (Liu et al., 2023b), the T6 pattern of Section 5. MusiChat
separates lyric-aligned structure generation from audio generation so
that a conversation can evolve a piece rather than regenerate it (Liao
et al., 2026). Loop Copilot uses a language model to sequence
specialized models in an iterative generate-and-edit loop (Zhang et al.,
2023; Zhang, 2024). Section 10 returns to these systems as the unified
class.

### 30 Lessons and Open Trade-offs

Adding control requires choices about what to train, what to compute for
each edit, and how closely to preserve the source. Five routes are
reviewed above:

- •
  conditioning added at training time, through a control branch (Wu et
  al., 2024b), an adapter on a frozen backbone (Lin et al., 2023),
  fine-tuning (Lan et al., 2024), or instruction triplets (Han et al.,
  2023; Zhang et al., 2024a);
- •
  optimization at inference time against a target (Novack et al.,
  2024b);
- •
  inversion of a recording into the generator’s latent (Zhang et al.,
  2024b; Liu et al., 2025b), or an editing path that needs no
  inversion (Fu et al., 2026; Ge et al., 2026);
- •
  an explicit plan between intent and audio (Li et al., 2026b; Wang et
  al., 2025b);
- •
  steering of concepts found inside the model (Singh et al., 2025;
  Facchiano et al., 2025).

The routes trade additional training against inference cost and against
preservation of the source. Control branches and adapters learn a new
conditioning signal, and some keep the generator fixed while others
update it. The cost of this route has fallen from a full control branch
to lightweight modules (Baker and Nistal, 2025; Tsai et al., 2025).
Inference-time optimization avoids parameter updates and adds
computation to every output, which distillation made affordable (Novack
et al., 2024a). Inversion-based and inversion-free editors differ in how
they retain the source. Later methods seek to reduce errors introduced
during inversion and regeneration (Liu et al., 2025b). Explicit plans
make structure over minutes easier to specify and revise. The papers
report different training costs, inference costs, and preservation
measures, and their protocols do not support a ranking: one framework
defines preservation (Vishe et al., 2025), one benchmark covers
instruction-based editing across audio modalities (Ma et al., 2026b),
and otherwise each paper measures its own control against its own
target. For a reader with a constraint, the routes sort as follows. An
adapter adds a control to a token generator with its weights fixed,
although the adapter itself is trained. A control branch or
inference-time optimization adds one to a diffusion generator.
Inversion-based or inversion-free editing uses a pretrained generator
without additional training for the edit. A plan is useful when the user
must specify or revise structure over minutes. The five routes have not
been compared under one protocol, and Section 13 returns to this gap.
How best to support multiple controls within a common training and
evaluation framework remains open.

## Chapter 9 Real-Time and Interactive Generation

Whether a system can be played like an instrument depends on its
latency, the time between an input and the audio that responds to it. A
generator can produce a minute of audio in less than a minute and still
respond too slowly for live use. A performer needs to know how much
recent input the model can hear, when an output becomes available, and
how soon a change in playing affects it. These timing questions separate
fast batch generation, streaming generation, and participation in a live
interaction. Offline generation serves production. Live use (class T4)
requires streaming output under continuous control, which for most of
the period only small models could attempt. An early survey of music
generation in the context of interaction predates the audio systems
reviewed here and frames the evaluation problem the class still
has (Agchar et al., 2024).

### 31 Streaming Generation and Live Accompaniment

Interactive speed has been reached by two routes, one that makes a large
offline model cheaper to sample and one that changes how it generates.
The first reduces the computation needed for sampling: step and layer
distillation makes latent diffusion faster than real time (Novack et
al., 2025), distilled inference-time control makes steering usable
live (Novack et al., 2024a), adversarial post-training gives few-step,
high-fidelity sampling in current open models (Evans et al., 2026), and
a dependency-free runtime runs such a model on CPUs and single-board
computers with quantization (Spanio and Rodà, 2026). The second is
architectural streaming. Live music models generate continuously
promptable audio with a fixed delay, built on autoregressive token
modeling, and were designed with performance and jamming as the target
use (Lyria Team et al., 2025). Live Music Diffusion Models then showed
that open diffusion models can be repurposed into streaming generators:
block-wise generation with key-value caching matches, and then improves
on, the inference cost of the discrete autoregressive live models, on
consumer hardware (Novack et al., 2026).

#### Running example.

MusicGen’s arithmetic shows why offline models do not stream by default.
At 50 frames per second under the delay pattern, playing in real time
would require about 50 decoding steps every second, each a transformer
pass over a growing context, before any computation time for the
condition. The live token models of this section take the same route and
make it work by fixing a chunk, bounding the context, and generating
each chunk at least as fast as it plays. The block-wise diffusion models
take the other route and generate a chunk at a time with a cache over
the previous ones. Future visibility $`\phi`$ is the offset between the
latest input a chunk may condition on and the start of the output
interval it accompanies: a positive value gives the model look-ahead at
the price of a later stream, and a negative value makes it predict ahead
to absorb system delay (Wu et al., 2025e). Figure 8 draws the schedule.

Accompaniment to a live musician adds a causality constraint: the model
hears an input stream and must emit a coherent output stream without
seeing the future. LiveBand generates causal accompaniment to a
musician’s audio in real time, with training and inference computation
matched by design (Pasini et al., 2026). A study of streaming
accompaniment, evaluated in simulated streaming, varies future
visibility and output chunk duration and examines their effects on
perceived latency and coherence (Wu et al., 2025e). ReaLchords learns
adaptive chord accompaniment for live interaction with reinforcement
learning (Wu et al., 2025d), and reinforcement-learning post-training
for live jamming exposes a further problem, reward hacking toward
repetitive but coherent output, which adversarial post-training
mitigates (Wu et al., 2025c). System reports describe the architecture
of real-time human-AI jamming systems (Liu et al., 2026c) and
low-latency gesture-driven generation (Jeyaraj et al., 2025).
Performance interfaces that map gesture to control signals for real-time
symbolic generation show how such models are used in performance (Wu et
al., 2026c), and a design space built from 184 live music agents across
the literature and video organizes the field by usage context,
interaction, technology, and ecosystem (Kim et al., 2026c). In live
coding, an agent generates TidalCycles patterns and steers them toward
target affective states with reinforcement learning (Wilson et al.,
2024).

### 32 Neural Audio Synthesis as an Instrument

A second line of real-time work predates the text-to-music systems above
and comes from computer music research. Here the goal is a playable
instrument: a small model that a musician controls through a latent
space, a synthesizer panel, or a live audio input. Sounds are
represented by compact sets of numbers, and the model turns points in
this latent space into audio. For a performer, the practical questions
are what a gesture or a control movement changes, and how quickly the
sound responds. The systems below are trained on hours rather than tens
of thousands of hours of audio and run on a laptop CPU or on embedded
hardware. Early contributions from IRCAM explored learned timbre spaces,
controllable synthesis, and neural instruments (Esling et al., 2018;
Esling et al., 2019; Caillon and Esling, 2021). Generative timbre spaces
regularize the latent space of a variational autoencoder with perceptual
dissimilarity ratings, encouraging movements through the latent space to
correspond to perceptually meaningful changes in timbre (Esling et al.,
2018). A modulated variant performs many-to-many timbre transfer with
one encoder and one decoder (Bitton et al., 2018). FlowSynth maps such
an organized latent audio space invertibly to synthesizer parameters
with normalizing flows, so that one model gives both parameter inference
from audio and macro-controls over the synthesizer (Esling et al.,
2019). A conditional autoencoder over drum spectrograms ships as a Max
for Live plugin for real-time generation on a CPU (Aouameur et al.,
2019). Neural granular synthesis replaces the descriptor space over
grains with a learned continuous latent (Bitton et al., 2020).
Structured lottery-ticket pruning makes deep generative audio models fit
embedded hardware with real-time constraints (Esling et al., 2020).

RAVE combined these elements into the model that most of the later
instrument work builds on. It is a variational autoencoder with a
two-stage training procedure, representation learning followed by
adversarial fine-tuning, a multiband decomposition of the waveform, and
a post-training analysis that compacts the latent space. The paper
reports 48 kHz synthesis running twenty times faster than real time on a
laptop CPU (Caillon and Esling, 2021). A companion method produces
non-causal streaming models. It executes offline-trained convolutional
models in the buffer-based computation of real-time audio software while
preserving their non-causal context, at the cost of additional buffering
latency (Caillon and Esling, 2022). Control then moved from latent
traversal to explicit attributes: a lightweight model whose latent space
is adversarially stripped of a salient descriptor turns that descriptor
into a continuous control, small enough to embed in a hardware
synthesizer (Devis et al., 2023). Demerlé et al. bring the
latent-diffusion machinery of Sections 6 to 8 into this setting:
diffusion-autoencoder features separate local structure from global
timbre, and one model serves both explicit control and example-based
style transfer, at faster than real-time speed on a GPU (Demerlé et al.,
2024). Two position papers state the goals of this line of work.
Chemla–Romeu-Santos and Esling (2022a); Chemla–Romeu-Santos and Esling
(2022b) argue that creative exploration can benefit from departures from
the training distribution, which likelihood objectives and
distribution-matching metrics may discourage. Douwes et al. (2021) argue
that generative audio models should be scored on quality and energy
consumption together. Later work extracts task-specific subnetworks from
large audio models for consumer devices (Genova et al., 2025) and learns
composable representations for diffusion-based synthesis (Bindi and
Esling, 2024).

Other groups arrived at the same framing, a small controllable model
that runs in real time, from different starting points. Differentiable
digital signal processing starts from the synthesizer: it places
interpretable components, oscillators, filters, and reverberation,
inside the network, so that the network controls a synthesizer rather
than producing samples, which gives controllable synthesis with small
models (Engel et al., 2020). It was extended to hierarchical note,
expression, and synthesis modeling (Wu et al., 2021), made lightweight
through neural waveshaping (Hayes et al., 2021), packaged as a real-time
plugin (Ganis et al., 2021), and reviewed as a family (Hayes et al.,
2024). Musika starts from the data budget: it trains on hundreds of
hours with one consumer GPU and generates arbitrary-length music faster
than real time on a CPU (Pasini and Schlüter, 2022). The same group’s
later work connects the instrument line to the systems of Sections 6
and 8: Music2Latent is a consistency autoencoder with single-step
reconstruction (Pasini et al., 2024b; Pasini et al., 2025), noise
augmentation lets continuous autoregressive models avoid error
accumulation over long sequences (Pasini et al., 2024c), and Diff-A-Riff
2 adds a stereo autoencoder, a diffusion transformer, and consistency
training to accompaniment generation (Nistal et al., 2024b; Pasini et
al., 2024a). Deployment has its own papers: the Neutone SDK wraps
PyTorch models as real-time and offline plugins for digital audio
workstations (Mitcheltree et al., 2025), and Caspe et al. analyze the
sources of latency and jitter in interactive neural synthesizers, with
RAVE-based timbre transfer as the case, and derive a design procedure
for low-latency interaction (Caspe et al., 2025). Related work covers
novelty search over a RAVE generator (Guo et al., 2024), timbre transfer
by inference-time editing of a pretrained latent diffusion model (Lee et
al., 2026a), and an HCI framing of neural audio models as design
materials for musicians (Zheng et al., 2026).

In the taxonomy these systems fall under audio and gesture control (D3)
and classes T3 and T4 (D4). In the D5 attributes, these examples include
compact models trained on relatively small corpora, released weights,
and CPU or embedded deployment, although not every example has every
property. Their evaluation runs through performance and practice, with
little use of the distribution metrics of Section 11. The two lines
address the same latency budget in opposite orders. The instrument line
starts with small models and adds control and quality. The streaming
models of Section 31 start from large offline models and reduce their
cost. Demerlé et al. bring latent diffusion into the instrument
setting (Demerlé et al., 2024), and the live models bring text
conditioning to the instrument’s latency budget (Lyria Team et al.,
2025; Novack et al., 2026).

The large text-conditioned streaming models are recent, and the
interactive neural instruments have the longer history. Both must be
evaluated on response latency and on the musical interaction as well as
on output quality, and the available computation bounds the model that
can meet a given latency budget (Novack et al., 2026).

### 33 Lessons and Open Trade-offs

This class asks how to emit audio under the latency budget of Section 4
while receiving input, with whatever model capacity the bound allows.
Live use requires the generator to meet a throughput budget and a
response-delay budget at once. Two routes are reviewed above, and they
approach the bound from opposite sides. The first reduces the cost of a
large offline model, by step and layer distillation (Novack et al.,
2025), adversarial post-training (Evans et al., 2026), or block-wise
generation with key-value caching (Novack et al., 2026), or builds a
token model for continuous prompting (Lyria Team et al., 2025). The
second builds a small model for the purpose: the autoencoder instruments
of Section 32, which run on a laptop CPU (Caillon and Esling, 2021) or
inside a hardware synthesizer (Devis et al., 2023). The evidence
separates them on hardware and on input. The reviewed streaming
text-to-music models typically run on a GPU and are prompted with text.
The instruments typically run on a CPU and are played with audio or
gesture. The one study that varies the streaming parameters shows that
future visibility and chunk duration trade perceived latency against
coherence inside the first route (Wu et al., 2025e). Accompaniment to a
live musician is a third problem, causal by construction (Pasini et al.,
2026), and reinforcement post-training for it has a documented failure,
reward hacking toward repetitive output (Wu et al., 2025c). The
evaluations are difficult to compare. The instrument line is assessed
through performance and practice, and the streaming models through
distribution metrics and user studies. Latency figures are reported on
different hardware without a shared procedure, although the sources of
latency and jitter have been analyzed for one system class (Caspe et
al., 2025).

For CPU or embedded deployment with audio or gesture control, the
neural-instrument literature provides the starting points and the
deployment tools (Mitcheltree et al., 2025). Text-conditioned generation
on a GPU has the live models and the block-wise models. Accompaniment to
a live input needs a causal design. The remaining trade-offs concern
latency, output quality, energy use, and hardware requirements, and this
class reports embedded deployment and energy consumption where the other
classes do not (Esling et al., 2020; Douwes et al., 2021). Evaluation
also needs to examine the musical interaction as well as individual
outputs.

## Chapter 10 Boundaries and Adjacent Areas

Neighboring areas intersect the audio-generation literature: music
generated for video and images (class T5), systems that unify
understanding and generation behind a language interface (class T6), and
symbolic generation where it feeds audio pipelines or co-creation. This
section marks where the scope of the monograph ends. We cover selected
examples that show how each area connects to music audio generation. The
structured searches of Section 2 had no topic group for symbolic
generation. The branch surveys cited in each subsection give fuller
treatments.

### 34 Cross-Modal Scoring

Video-to-music generation conditions on visual content and must satisfy
two alignments at once: semantic (mood, genre, setting) and temporal
(cuts, motion, rhythm). Existing surveys cover the history, datasets,
and metrics of video-to-music generation in greater detail (Ji et al.,
2026; Wang et al., 2025d). An early system of the branch generated
symbolic accompaniment, chords and MIDI, from a video’s semantic,
motion, and emotion features with an affective multimodal transformer
and released the paired MuVi-Sync dataset (Kang et al., 2024). Within
our scope the systems extend the conditioning dimension D3 without
changing the paradigms. The examples below illustrate changes in
conditioning and synchronization within the generative paradigms
introduced earlier. V2Meow learned globally aligned video-acoustic
signatures with a multi-stage autoregressive model over paired music
videos (Su et al., 2024). VidMuse combined local and global visual cues
in a long-short-term model and released a large video-music
dataset (Tian et al., 2025b), MuVi added contrastive music-visual
pretraining for rhythmic synchronization (Li et al., 2024c), Diff-BGM
brought diffusion and an annotated dataset with retrieval-based
alignment metrics (Li et al., 2024e), and GVMGen used hierarchical
spatial and temporal attention (Zuo et al., 2025). Recent systems
address these alignments in different ways. V2M-Zero captures temporal
structure through event curves computed within each modality and needs
no paired video-music data at training time (Lin et al., 2026).
Video-Robin and VIBE combine autoregressive planning with diffusion
synthesis, the first adding text conditioning for semantic
control (Lokegaonkar et al., 2026) and the second reward modeling for
verifiable constraints such as tempo and key (Bhosale et al., 2026).
Reproducibility is a stated concern here as elsewhere: a corpus of
public-domain film clips replaces crawled video links and enables the
study of dialogue as a conditioning signal (Kim et al., 2026a).
Video-synchronized generation of audio, speech, and song in one
diffusion transformer marks the boundary with general audio
generation (Wang et al., 2026c).

Dance-to-music generation is the motion counterpart of the task, and its
designs follow the same conditioning routes. One system inverts the
rhythm and genre of a dance sequence into the text space of a
text-to-music model (Li et al., 2024d). Others condition a latent
diffusion model on positive and negative rhythmic cues from the
video (Sun et al., 2025) or have a diffusion transformer extract
genre-adaptive rhythm from joint motion and align it to the music
latents (Wang et al., 2025a). Visual features in place of single-dancer
pose extend the task to multiple and non-human dancers (Im et al.,
2026), and beat-guided contrastive pretraining on unpaired motion and
music reduces the need for paired data (Kimura et al., 2026). Procedural
music for games, where generation must react to play, has a survey of
its own (Luo and Reiss, 2025).

### 35 Unified Understanding and Generation

Systems in class T6 put a language model between the user and one or
more generators. Two designs recur. In the first design, the language
model routes requests to specialized tools. MusicAgent decomposes a
request into tasks and calls specialized tools (Yu et al., 2023), and
M2UGen and MuMu-LLaMA connect music, image, and video encoders to a
language model and route generation to MusicGen or AudioLDM 2 (Liu et
al., 2023b; Liu et al., 2026b). WeaveMuse is an open multi-agent system
covering understanding, symbolic composition, and audio
synthesis (Karystinaios, 2025), and MusiChat uses the same pattern for
iterative refinement of a composition (Liao et al., 2026). In the
second, one model does both: UniAudio treated music as one task among
many in a universal token language model (Yang et al., 2024), and
UniAudio 2.0 factorizes its codec into text-aligned reasoning tokens and
reconstruction tokens so that understanding and hierarchical generation
share a representation (Yang et al., 2026a). AudioChat unifies
storytelling, editing, and understanding for general audio in one
model (Chen et al., 2026b), and any-to-any multimodal language models
include music among their output modalities (Wu et al., 2023a; Zhan et
al., 2024).

A third design distributes composition itself over several agents, so
far mostly in the symbolic domain. ComposerX assigns roles to
language-model agents and reports that the multi-agent arrangement
improves the compositions of a single model (Deng et al., 2024).
CoComposer builds five agents on the stages of a composition workflow,
evaluates them with the Audiobox aesthetics predictor, and reports
better MusicLM performance under its evaluation protocol (Xing et al.,
2025). Specialized agents generate higher-voice harmony for a given
melody (Ganapathy and Shaja, 2025). MusicSwarm removes the central
coordinator. Identical frozen foundation models act as bar-wise agents
that exchange harmonic, rhythmic, and structural cues, adapt a
short-term memory, and reach consensus without updating model weights.
In the reported experiments, the decentralized swarm outperformed a
centralized multi-agent system with a global critic on quality,
diversity, and structural variety, in symbolic, audio, and
graph-theoretic analyses (Buehler, 2025). Libretto gives such agents an
inspectable object to work on, a symbolic grammar of onset slots,
voices, and bars with a corpus-calibrated statistical space for
diagnosis and self-revision (Xu, 2026), and a self-supervised world
model over piano rolls is proposed as the representation co-creation
agents need (Hawley, 2026).

On the audio side, training-free multi-agent frameworks orchestrate
existing generators for complex scenes: AudioGenie pairs a generation
team with a supervisor team for multimodal-to-multiaudio
generation (Rong et al., 2025), and Audio-Oscar coordinates specialist
agents for voice design, speech, timeline planning, model selection,
non-speech generation, and post-production, with a benchmark of scene
descriptions (Duan et al., 2026b). A language model can also analyze a
listener’s preferences and write prompts for a commercial
generator (Pandey et al., 2025), and an agent-built music-theory lexicon
is reported to improve symbolic and audio generators (Hu et al., 2025).
In the taxonomy these systems are language-model agents (D2) whose
generation step is symbolic or delegated to the generators of Sections 6
to 8. The claim that coordination rules can substitute for parameter
updates has been tested on symbolic output and remains open for audio.

Music-language models also support captioning and question answering,
the understanding side of the class: MU-LLaMA performs both from MERT
features (Liu et al., 2024d), and Music Flamingo and the Audio Flamingo
family extend song and sound understanding in audio-language
models (Ghosh et al., 2025; Ghosh et al., 2026). Benchmarks test what
these models know: multiple-choice music understanding (Weck et al.,
2024), factual comprehension (Lin et al., 2025a), multi-step reasoning
in symbolic music (Zhou et al., 2024), and competence outside Western
tonal music (Kader et al., 2026). Surveys of foundation models for
understanding (Li et al., 2024f) and of large audio models (Latif et
al., 2023) cover this class’s understanding side in depth.

### 36 Symbolic Generation at the Boundary

Symbolic generation remains relevant to audio pipelines for two reasons.
First, as an intermediate control layer: MuseCoco maps text to musical
attributes to symbolic music, letting users steer composition before
rendering (Lu et al., 2023). Symbolic signals such as chords and melody
are the control inputs of several audio systems (Tal et al., 2024; Lin
et al., 2023). MIDI intermediates carry the melody in text-to-song
systems (Li et al., 2024b; Tsai et al., 2026). Text-to-MIDI generation
with adapted or purpose-built language models can produce such
intermediate representations (Wu et al., 2025b; Bhandari et al., 2025;
Ding et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2024; Qu et al., 2024), supported by
captioned MIDI datasets (Melechovsky et al., 2024b). Second, as an area
of active co-creation research: anticipatory music transformers model
asynchronous human-machine composition with strong infilling (Thickstun
et al., 2024), Composer’s Assistant brings multi-track infilling into a
DAW-style workflow (Malandro, 2023), NotaGen applies the full
language-model pipeline of pretraining, fine-tuning, and reinforcement
learning to sheet music (Wang et al., 2025c), and hierarchical
orchestral generation with a controllable harmony skeleton extends the
approach to large ensembles (He et al., 2026b). The symbolic and audio
threads are converging through hybrid sketch representations
(Section 12), where an intermediate plan or learned sketch is placed
between the condition and the sound (Lam et al., 2025; Yang et al.,
2025a; Li et al., 2026b). A survey of language-model methods for
symbolic music covers this branch in full (Le et al., 2025).

### 37 Distribution-Level Metrics

For most of the period, the default automatic metric was the Fréchet
Audio Distance (FAD), a distribution-level distance in an embedding
space (Kilgour et al., 2019). It was adopted for three reasons. It needs
no paired reference for each generated clip, it is cheap to compute, and
the original paper showed that it tracks human ratings of distorted
music better than the signal-based measures it replaced. Two lines of
evidence then showed its limits as a measure of generated music. First,
FAD scores vary substantially with the choice of embedding model and
reference set, so results are not comparable across papers (Gui et al.,
2024). The correlation of FAD with human ratings depends on the
embedding even for environmental audio (Tailleur et al., 2024), and the
training task of the encoder shapes which acoustic properties the metric
can detect (Jeong, 2026). Second, FAD correlates weakly with human
preferences on modern text-to-music output (Huang et al., 2025). The
same study released MusicPrefs, a corpus of pairwise human preferences
over generated music, and proposed the MAUVE Audio Divergence (MAD),
which computes the MAUVE divergence over self-supervised MERT
embeddings. The metric and the embedding were selected with synthetic
degradations and then validated against MusicPrefs, where MAD’s system
rankings agreed with listeners better than FAD’s (Huang et al., 2025).
Kernel-based alternatives avoid the Gaussian approximation (Chung et
al., 2025). Squared MMD admits an unbiased estimator, although estimates
remain variable at finite sample sizes (Chung et al., 2025). A
comparative study of five systems found inconsistencies across these
metrics and human ratings (Zhang et al., 2025c), and a study on codec
embeddings found FAD ahead of MMD against listening tests (Biswas and
Villemoes, 2026). Reference-free metrics remove the reference set
altogether by reading quality from a generative backbone (Ziv et al.,
2026b). Complementary text-audio alignment scores based on contrastive
embeddings (Wu et al., 2023b) remain in use and measure prompt adherence
rather than quality. A counterfactual protocol shows that prompted
attribute agreement overstates control, because a requested attribute
may appear because it is common in the model’s outputs (Wang, 2026).

#### Running example.

The MusicGen paper used four forms of evaluation: the Fréchet Audio
Distance with the VGGish encoder, a Kullback–Leibler divergence between
label distributions predicted on generated and reference clips, a CLAP
score for prompt adherence, and a listening test with ratings of overall
quality and of relevance to the prompt (Copet et al., 2023). Read with
the evidence of this section, the first number depends on an encoder
trained on general audio, the third shares the blind spots of the
embedding it uses, and the listening test is the result a reader should
weigh most, provided the report states the raters and the items. The
protocol of Section 39 is the list of what such a test should state.

Evaluation begins with the claim being made. A distribution metric asks
whether collections of generated and reference recordings resemble one
another in a chosen representation, a prompt-adherence score asks
whether an output matches its description, and listening tests and
task-specific measures ask more particular questions about lyrics,
structure, edits, or preference. These instruments give different
evidence and are not interchangeable measures of quality. The expansion
of generation capabilities over the period reviewed has not been matched
by widely adopted evaluation protocols for full songs, editing, and live
interaction, and much of the recent evaluation literature revisits the
automatic metrics of the earlier system reports. Table 7 lists the
metrics, judges, benchmarks, and protocols in use, what each measures,
and whether the paper that introduced or studied it reports evidence of
agreement with human judgments. Dedicated evaluation surveys cover
methodology across subjective and objective methods (Xiong et al.,
2023), metrics for audio and symbolic generation (Kader and Karmaker,
2025), and the evaluation of generative models in music as a whole,
including listening studies and their validity (Lerch et al., 2025).
This section covers what a reader needs to interpret the claims of the
systems in Sections 6 to 10. For each instrument those systems report,
it says what the instrument measures and whether human-agreement
evidence stands behind it.

Human judgments are being collected at larger scale and in more settings
than the listening tests of individual papers. Live arena platforms
collect pairwise votes from real users across systems, providing
continually updated preference data and a public leaderboard (Kim et

music therapists, including a songwriting tool for Deaf and

does not capture (Sun et al., 2024; Choi et al., 2026). Listener studies

In parallel, expert-annotated corpora support the training and testing
of learned evaluators, models that predict a rating from the audio so
that a system can be scored without a new listening test. The corpora
differ in what was rated and by whom: ratings of generated music (Liu et
al., 2025a), full-song aesthetics annotations built for the
lyrics-to-song era (Yao et al., 2025a), a large aesthetics dataset rated
across ten perceptual dimensions by trained annotators (Zhang et al.,
2025d), fine-grained multi-aspect song-quality benchmarks scoring
melody, arrangement, vocals, mixing, and structure separately (Wu et
al., 2026a), and stem-aware song judges (Lv et al., 2026). General audio
aesthetics predictors trained on such ratings are also used as reward
models and data filters inside training pipelines (Tjandra et al.,
2025), the dual use that Section 40 returns to. Reward models for songs
now produce a written critique before a score (Zhuang et al., 2026), and
a benchmark tests reward models under compositional text, lyric, and
audio instructions (Ma et al., 2026a). Mean-opinion-score predictors
learn quality and text alignment directly (Emon et al., 2025; Wang et
al., 2026a). The judges have known failure modes, and a reader should
expect them: aesthetic scorers trained on SongEval learn genre as a
shortcut for quality and overestimate pop music (Zhang et al., 2026b),
and popularity on generative platforms is predictable from aesthetic
features, which reflects the platforms’ audiences as well as the
music (Husain and Herremans, 2026). Cleaner caption datasets improve the
text side of text-to-music evaluation (Manco et al., 2023),
representation benchmarks support comparisons of the encoders that
metrics are built on (Yuan et al., 2023), and an academic challenge with
fixed data and compute makes systems comparable at the protocol
level (Hsieh et al., 2026).

A multi-stimulus test with a hidden reference and anchors (MUSHRA), the

### 40 Gaps and a Reporting Recommendation

Gaps remain after these developments, and each limits what a system
report can claim. There is no accepted protocol for full songs, where
lyric intelligibility, vocal quality, arrangement, and structure each
need assessment. Practice is converging on batteries of learned judges
reported side by side, as in the 2026 industrial reports (Xu et al., 2026) and in song benchmarks (Wu et al., 2026a), but no battery is yet
standard and each judge is validated against small rater pools.
Long-range structure has no standard metric: system reports inspect it
through self-similarity matrices of generated and real music (Evans et
al., 2024b), a song judge reports rank correlation with expert structure
scores (Wu et al., 2026a), but no accepted measure distinguishes a
coherent three-minute form from locally plausible drift, and
structure-preserving editing has an evaluation framework (Vishe et al., 2025) that generation does not. Symbolic generation has its own
distribution metrics (Retkowski et al., 2024), which audio systems with
a symbolic plan could reuse. And the use of learned aesthetics
predictors as both training rewards and evaluation metrics creates a
circularity risk: a system post-trained against a judge can score well
on that judge without listeners agreeing. Reward hacking of this kind
has been observed in live interaction models (Wu et al., 2025c).

## Chapter 12 Data, Provenance, and the Legal Record

Music generation should serve the musicians, songwriters, performers,
and communities whose work makes it possible. We support research and
tools that respect their creative agency, obtain meaningful consent for
the use of their work and voices, recognize their contributions, and
provide fair compensation. These are commitments of this monograph.
Technical quality, commercial adoption, or a licensing announcement does
not by itself demonstrate that they have been met. Our discussion of
Suno, Udio, and other systems does not endorse their training practices
or imply that creators’ objections have been resolved.

Artist and industry organizations have raised concerns about
authorization for training, the use of performers’ voices and
identities, transparency, and the economic consequences for creative
work. The Human Artistry Campaign calls for permission, appropriate
compensation, and traceable uses of protected material (Human Artistry
Campaign, 2026). A joint letter of June 2026 from artist, songwriter,
and manager organizations challenged default participation and pressure
to accept AI uses without clear terms, and it called for meaningful
consent, remuneration, and transparency (European Music Managers
Alliance and others, 2026). These statements document the positions of
their signatories. They should not be treated as evidence that all
artists or all music companies have the same interests.

The evidence answers several different questions. Training-data
provenance records where material came from, how it was obtained and
processed, and what permissions or licenses are documented. Attribution
methods estimate or record relationships between an output and
particular inputs or training items. Memorization tests examine whether
a model can reproduce training material. Watermarks and detectors
provide evidence about an output’s origin under specified conditions.
Each kind of evidence answers its own question: a data statement
describes the corpus, a memorization test measures what the model
reproduces, an attribution estimate apportions influence, and a detector
identifies generated audio. Each leaves the other questions open.
Table 8 lists what each kind of evidence can support and the inference
to avoid.

| Evidence                             | What it can support                                         | Inference to avoid                                                                       |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Source and processing records        | An account of the material and its handling                 | A complete legal clearance, or proof that every creator participated willingly           |
| A stated license or agreement        | Permissions described by its parties and terms              | Authorization for all past uses, every right, or every model                             |
| Input records or attribution methods | Recorded or estimated relationships in a specified workflow | A universal causal allocation of creative contribution or payment entitlement            |
| Memorization tests                   | Evidence of reproduction under a particular protocol        | Proof that no protected material can ever be reproduced                                  |
| A watermark                          | A tested origin claim under defined conditions              | A complete training history or proof of permission                                       |
| A binary generated-audio detector    | Fallible evidence about generated versus recorded audio     | Identification of the generator or its training recordings from that binary result alone |

Of everything a system report describes, the training data is the
component described least. In the data column of Tables 4 to 6, 43 of
the 105 rows carry only the code U: the source is undisclosed, in-house,
or web-collected, with or without an hours figure, and the two
commercial products are among them. Twenty rows carry the code L or CC,
a license or a Creative Commons corpus, 11 are inference-time methods
that add no training data of their own, and the remaining rows name a
public or synthetic corpus, with one exception that states nothing. Many
academic and most industrial models do not disclose their corpora, and
dataset opacity has been an ethical concern since before the current
generation of systems (Barnett, 2023). The Qwen-Music technical report
states corpus scale, upward of five million hours, without
provenance (Xu et al., 2026). The datasets that are documented are
skewed: a study of music generation datasets and papers found that only
a small fraction of the total hours come from non-Western genres, with
disparate model performance across genres as a consequence (Mehta et
al., 2025b).

These data codes describe what the sources disclose. U is not a finding
of unlawful use, and L or CC is not an independent audit of every
relevant right or of the terms on which creators participated.

The legal record, discussed in Section 41, is read here for its
consequences for machine learning. First, the question the Munich
judgments turned on is whether training items can be reproduced from the
model, while the United States cases also concern how the recordings
were acquired, the copies made during training, circumvention, market
effect, and fair use. Memorization is therefore a measurement that a
release should report, with the protocol stated (Section 42). Second,
licensing agreements should be reported with their parties and stated
scope. The systems with documented sources below are reference points
for the systems with undisclosed data. The causal effect of licensed
data would need a controlled comparison. Third, attribution and
origin-verification tools (Section 43) can support accountability, but
they do not determine who authorized a use, who is entitled to payment,
or whether payment occurred. The technical measurements and the legal
record therefore need to be read together, with their different limits
kept explicit.

Fourth, how creators should be paid when their catalogs train models is
being formalized as a market design problem with data-attribution scores
at its center (Zhang et al., 2026a), and one proposed agent architecture
makes attribution part of the generation workflow, with music organized
into retrievable blocks whose every use triggers an attribution
event (Kim et al., 2025a). The same group then tested what such a record
can establish, in a study published after the eligibility cutoff and
cited here for context. A stem generator conditioned only on audio of
known provenance records every input, and the outputs are audited for
reproductions of training tracks absent from the record with a
version-identification model. In their experiments the recorded inputs
shaped the outputs as intended (timbre followed the prompt audio and
harmony followed the context), reproductions outside the record were
rare, and on human-judged cases the audit’s detector had higher
precision and recall than the other memorization detectors tested (Kim
et al., 2026b). In this workflow, an input record and an output audit
provide complementary evidence. The record identifies supplied material,
while the audit tests for reproductions that the record alone would not
show. Neither gives a complete account of training-data influence or
creators’ entitlements.

A minority of systems document the sources and licenses of their
training data. Stable Audio Open generates from Creative Commons audio
with open weights (Evans et al., 2025), and Diff-A-Riff is a production
tool trained on a licensed catalog (Nistal et al., 2024a). Stable Audio
3 reports that its medium and large models are trained on licensed
AudioSparx material and Creative Commons recordings from Freesound, and
that the final training stages of its small music model use AudioSparx
only. An audio tagger (PANNs) flagged music-like Freesound recordings,
which an unnamed content-detection company then checked, and the authors
removed the recordings that the service identified as copyrighted
content (Evans et al., 2026). This describes a source and filtering
procedure. It does not establish that the corpus is free of copyright or
that every relevant right has been cleared. A Creative Commons license
is a copyright license and does not guarantee clearance of all other
rights (Creative Commons, 2026). Muse is an open song generator trained
on a released licensed synthetic corpus (Jiang et al., 2026), and the
ICME challenge fixes a Creative Commons training set for every
participant (Hsieh et al., 2026). Open caption corpora over freely
licensed music support the same goal (Roy et al., 2025). These systems
are the reference points for comparisons with undisclosed-data models.

#### Running example.

MusicGen’s data statement is the kind a reader should look for: about
20,000 hours of licensed music, named by source, with the weights
released under a non-commercial license and the code under a permissive
one (Copet et al., 2023). The statement answers the first question of
this section, what the model was trained on. On the second, whether the
model reproduces any of it, the paper reports a prompted-continuation
experiment: it prompts the model with excerpts of training items and
counts exact and partial matches of the first-codebook tokens in the
continuation. The scope of that test is narrower than a general test of
whether generated music reproduces protected material, and a release
that answered the question would state the protocol with the
measurement.

### 41 Litigation, Licensing, and Creators’ Claims

The selected legal sources discussed here were checked on 23 October 2026. This account distinguishes court decisions from litigants’
positions and company announcements. The coverage of the system
comparison remains defined in Section 2.

In June 2024, major record labels sued the two leading song generation
companies over training on copyrighted recordings (Recording Industry
Association of America, 2024; UMG Recordings, Inc. et al. v. Suno, Inc.,
2024; UMG Recordings, Inc. et al. v. Uncharted Labs, Inc., 2024).
Particular settlements have since coexisted with litigation. Universal
Music Group and Udio announced a settlement and licensing agreements on
29 October 2025 (Universal Music Group, 2025). Warner Music Group
announced an agreement with Udio on 19 November 2025 (Warner Music
Group, 2025b) and with Suno on 25 November 2025 (Warner Music Group,
2025a; Malik, 2025). These announcements concern the identified parties
and their proposed licensed products. They do not establish
authorization for every prior use, resolve other parties’ claims, or
demonstrate consent and payment for all affected creators.

Creators’ interests cannot be inferred from agreements between platforms
and record companies. In June 2026, the American Federation of Musicians
brought an action against Universal, Atlantic, and Warner entities,
alleging breaches of obligations to notify the union and compensate
musicians when recordings were licensed to Suno and Udio (American
Federation of Musicians of the United States and Canada, 2026). The
union’s account states its allegations and its interpretation of the
collective agreement, and no court has adjudicated them. The action
illustrates why the existence of a label license and the treatment of
the musicians whose performances were licensed are separate questions.

On 9 September 2026, Suno announced v6, named industry partners
including Warner Music Group, BMG, and Believe, and said it would retire
earlier models during the rollout (Suno, 2026a). The announcement does
not provide an independent audit of the complete training corpus. On 18
September 2026, Universal and Sony entities filed a second United States
action concerning 60,202 recordings (UMG Recordings, Inc. and others,
2026; Ingham, 2026). The complaint alleges continuing unauthorized
exploitation in developing v6, including through outputs and preference
signals associated with earlier models. These allegations have not been
adjudicated, and announcing a new model does not resolve them. In its
September 2026 answer in the earlier action, Suno asserted fair use as a
defense (Suno, Inc., 2026). That pleading states the company’s legal
position and does not record a judicial approval of its practices.

The Munich proceedings produced findings on particular works and models.
On 11 November 2025, the Munich Regional Court held that the lyrics at
issue were reproduced in OpenAI’s models and prompted outputs, and that
the text-and-data-mining exception did not cover those
reproductions (Landgericht München I, 2025). On 31 July 2026, the same
court found infringement concerning six compositions in Suno’s v3.5 and
v4 models, addressing training copies, memorization, and outputs. It
applied United States law to the training copies made in the United
States and rejected the fair-use defense on those facts. The court
substantially granted claims for injunctive relief, information, and
damages (Landgericht München I, 2026). The court’s announcements state
that both judgments are not final, and their holdings should not be
generalized to every model or training use.

The United States Copyright Office’s 2025 training report provides legal
and policy analysis and is not a judgment in these proceedings. It
treats fair use as dependent on the works, their acquisition, the
purpose of training, output controls, and market effects. The report
regards large-scale commercial use of copyrighted material to generate
competing expressive output, especially following illegal access, as
exceeding established fair-use limits. The cited version is the Office’s
pre-publication report (United States Copyright Office, 2025).

### 42 Memorization

Whether a model reproduces its training data is a measurable property,
but few system authors measure it. Among major releases, YuE is an
exception in reporting a memorization analysis alongside the model (Yuan
et al., 2025), and training-time mitigations such as beat-synchronous
mixup were proposed early (Chen et al., 2024b). Independent studies
supply the measurements. Memorization in audio latent diffusion models
grows as training sets shrink, and mel-spectrogram similarity detects it
more reliably than embedding retrieval (Bralios et al., 2024).
Anti-memorization guidance at sampling time reduces replication in an
open text-to-audio model (Messina et al., 2026). Phonetic prompting
attacks show that lyric filters in commercial and open song generators
can be bypassed by homophonic rewrites, because the models bind
sub-lexical sound patterns to memorized songs (Roh et al., 2025).
Melody-aware similarity models built for plagiarism detection are the
complementary tool on the output side (Lu et al., 2025; Go and Kim,
2026).

### 43 Watermarking and Detection

Watermarking and detection provide different forms of evidence about an
output’s origin. A watermark inserted during generation can support an
origin claim within the conditions in which it has been tested. A binary
classifier trained to distinguish generated from recorded music provides
fallible evidence for that distinction. By itself, such a classifier
identifies neither the generating model nor its training recordings.
Generator attribution is a separate task, studied with datasets that
distinguish outputs from different models (Comanducci et al., 2025).
Neither task establishes training-data provenance or permission to use a
recording. Watermarking is the first route. Localized watermarking with
sample-level detection, developed for generated speech, is the reference
design (San Roman et al., 2024), but a benchmark of watermarking methods
under realistic attacks finds neural codec resynthesis the hardest case,
even for methods trained against it (Özer et al., 2025). Generative
watermarking embedded during generation is one proposed approach (Yun et
al., 2026). Watermarking can also be applied to the training data, so
that use of a catalog is detectable in a model’s outputs (Epple et al.,
2024), and audio embeddings can attribute a generated piece to
influential training recordings (Barnett et al., 2024).

Detection is the second route, and its literature grew quickly during
the period reviewed. An early study showed that a music deepfake
detector is easy to train to high accuracy on reconstructions and argued
that the test score is not the end of the problem (Afchar et al., 2024;
Afchar et al., 2025). An overview organizes detection methods by the
trace they exploit, at the signal, feature, watermark, or
semantic-consistency level (Li et al., 2024h). Benchmarks and datasets
followed: classifiers and benchmarks for fully synthetic songs (Rahman
et al., 2025), datasets for attributing output to the generating
model (Comanducci et al., 2025), a multi-generator, multi-domain,
multi-lingual database (Li et al., 2026e), a semantically aligned
dataset across ten generation services built to prevent shortcut
learning (Pascu et al., 2026), and systematic evaluations with
explanations (Li et al., 2026f). The 2026 work addresses deployment
conditions. Detectors that rely on codec artifacts collapse under speed
and pitch manipulation, and a frequency-scaling-invariant design
restores robustness (Dugelay et al., 2026). Hybrid tracks that mix human
and AI-generated stems defeat separation-then-detection
pipelines (Rigaud et al., 2026), and the task is reformulated as
estimating the proportion of AI-generated content (Cruz et al., 2026).
Broadcast monitoring, where music is short and masked by speech,
degrades detectors trained on clean audio (López-Ayala et al., 2026a;
López-Ayala et al., 2026b). Zero-shot detection for generators unseen at
training time is approached by one-class modeling of real music (Afchar
and Hennequin, 2026; Han et al., 2026). Further work covers hard
negatives, detector architectures, forensic residuals, and
representation choices (Morosanu et al., 2026; Batra et al., 2025; Oh,
2026; Kim and Go, 2025; Park et al., 2026). Detection lags generation,
as analyses of the interaction between the two describe (Cros Vila et
al., 2025).

## Chapter 13 Discussion: Trends and Open Challenges

### 44 Trends Across Classes

Read together, the tables and the sections above show the following
patterns in the reviewed systems. First, song generation became more
prominent in this collection: Table 5 holds no system from 2023 and 25
from 2025 and 2026, while Table 4 holds six from 2023 and five from 2025
and 2026. Second, planner-renderer designs became common, from MeLoDy
and MusiCoT through SongBloom and DiffRhythm 2 to the 2026 industrial
reports (Lam et al., 2023; Lam et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025a; Jiang
et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026; Dai et al., 2026), alongside single-stage
flow models (Chen et al., 2026a) and single-stage token models (Liu et
al., 2026a). Third, flow matching, consistency training, distillation,
and adversarial post-training reduced sampling cost to real-time and
streaming rates (Prajwal et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024c; Novack et al.,
2025; Evans et al., 2026; Lyria Team et al., 2025; Novack et al., 2026).
Fourth, preference alignment spread from a few systems in 2024 (Cideron
et al., 2024b; Majumder et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2024) to 11 of the 25
song systems of 2025 and 2026 in Table 5, with alignment targets
widening from overall preference to lyric fidelity and multiple
rewards (Zhang et al., 2025b; Ziv et al., 2026a). Fifth, open weights
followed two prominent commercial capabilities: open song generation
within a year of the commercial products (Yuan et al., 2025), open
streaming generation within a year of the first live models (Novack et
al., 2026). Openness is not permanent: the repository and weights that
the LeVo papers name were withdrawn by July 2026. For this reason, the
openness cells follow the dated artifact checks documented in
Appendix 15.A and are not taken from the papers. Sixth, evaluation
practice changed after the default metric was shown to correlate weakly
with human preference, toward metrics validated against human
preferences, learned judges, and arenas (Table 7). Seventh, the legal
context in the United States moved from lawsuits alone to settlements
and licenses alongside continuing suits (Recording Industry Association
of America, 2024; Malik, 2025). In Germany, the Munich Regional Court
issued judgments concerning song lyrics in OpenAI’s language models and
six compositions in Suno’s models (Section 41) (Landgericht München I,
2025; Landgericht München I, 2026). Section 12 treats provenance,
detection, and compensation as research topics in their own right.

### 45 Open Challenges

The following problems remain open. They are ordered by the question of
Section 1 they belong to, and for each we state what a solution would
need.

_Live interaction (Q2, cost and latency)._ Streaming models have made
generation fast enough for live performance (Lyria Team et al., 2025;
Novack et al., 2026; Pasini et al., 2026). Latency suitable for the
intended performance task, stability under reinforcement
post-training (Wu et al., 2025c), and interfaces developed with
musicians (Wu et al., 2025e; Wu et al., 2026c) remain design priorities.
The instrument line of Section 32 adds two criteria the text-to-music
systems rarely report, embedded deployment and energy
consumption (Esling et al., 2020; Douwes et al., 2021).

_Controllability (Q3, conditioning)._ Fine-grained control still trades
against fidelity or requires per-control training (Wu et al., 2024b; Tal
et al., 2024). Adapters, light conditioners, and inference-time
optimization reduce the cost (Baker and Nistal, 2025; Tsai et al., 2025;
Novack et al., 2024b), and editing methods preserve structure with
increasing reliability (Chang et al., 2026; Fu et al., 2026), but
unifying the adapter, conditioner, inference-time, and plan-based
approaches remains open.

_Long-range structure (Q4, planning)._ Models produce full-length songs,
and explicit plans, layouts, and programs give users control over
form (Lam et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2025b; Duan et
al., 2026a), with segment-level prompts extending that control along
time (Wang et al., 2026e). The existing measures score aspects of
structure: a song judge’s structure axis, which reports rank correlation
with expert structure scores (Table 7) (Wu et al., 2026a), structure
preservation in editing (Vishe et al., 2025), and a structural error
rate against the target lyric structure reported with one system (Yang
et al., 2025a). No widely adopted protocol evaluates long-range form,
motif development, and tension over minutes and across genres, so the
success of plan-based control can be scored only in part. A solution
would need such a protocol validated against expert judgments and a
demonstration that plan-conditioned generation improves it.

_Evaluation (Q5, measurement)._ Evaluation uses a growing range of
preference-validated metrics, arenas, and learned judges in place of a
default metric with weak human correlation (Huang et al., 2025).
Full-song judges (Yao et al., 2025a; Wu et al., 2026a; Zhuang et al., 2026) address several musical aspects, but no shared protocol is widely
adopted. Reusing an aesthetics predictor as both a training reward and
an evaluation metric creates a risk of overfitting to that
judge (Tjandra et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025c). Judges inherit dataset
biases (Zhang et al., 2026b), and controllability claims need
counterfactual protocols (Wang, 2026). A solution would need a shared
song-evaluation battery with documented listener recruitment and
evaluators that were not used as training rewards for the systems
compared.

_Data and memorization (Q6, artifacts)._ Reproducible research requires
open, licensed corpora at scale (Evans et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2026;
Hsieh et al., 2026), and memorization reporting should become a release
norm (Yuan et al., 2025; Bralios et al., 2024). Phonetic memorization
attacks show that lexical filters are not sufficient (Roh et al., 2025),
and detection degrades under manipulation, mixing, and broadcast
conditions (Dugelay et al., 2026; Rigaud et al., 2026; López-Ayala et
al., 2026b).

_Coverage (Q6, artifacts and data)._ Song models concentrate on English
and Mandarin popular music, most of the world’s musical traditions are
under-represented in training data (Mehta et al., 2025b), an evaluation
of language models on South Asian music reports gaps in their
performance (Kader et al., 2026), and the vocabulary people use to
describe and prompt generated music differs across cultures (Park and
Arthur, 2026).

_Interpretability (Q1 and Q3, representation and control)._ Little is
known about the internal structure of music models, leaving control and
safety interventions empirical. Early work discovers concepts with
sparse autoencoders and steers activations in audio generators (Singh et
al., 2025; Facchiano et al., 2025; Paek et al., 2025; Mariotte et al.,
2026), probes and patches symbolic generators (Poćwiardowski and
Modrzejewski, 2026), and uses the same findings for editing (Chang et
al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026d). A solution would connect these findings
to controllable and auditable generation.

### 46 Recommendations

The evidence of Sections 6 to 12 supports a short list of
recommendations, each addressed to the reader who can act on it.

_To authors of system reports._ State the four slots of the generic
problem (representation, factorization, conditioning route, sampler) and
the attributes of the box in Section 16, so that the system can be
placed and compared without inference. Report the evaluation as the box
in Section 40 asks, with the encoder, the reference set, and the sample
count of every distribution metric, and a listening test that follows
Section 39. If a learned judge supplied a training reward, say so and
add a human comparison on the same items.

_To evaluators and benchmark builders._ Validate measures used to
support perceptual-quality claims against listeners, and report the
scope of that validation. Report listener recruitment and
characteristics, and use evaluators that were not used as training
rewards for the systems being compared. Test controllability with
counterfactual prompts rather than with agreement on requested
attributes. A useful next step is a shared full-song battery with
separate lyric, vocal, arrangement, and structure assessments,
documented listener recruitment, and judges independent of the systems’
training.

_To those who release models and data._ State the training corpus by
source, hours, and license, and keep the model card current when weights
are withdrawn or replaced, because the tables of this monograph record
withdrawals found only by checking cards after publication. Report a
memorization measurement with its protocol at release. Report any
watermark or other origin-verification mechanism, the conditions under
which it was tested, and its limitations. Where inputs are recorded,
keep the record, since input records and output audits are complementary
evidence for attribution.

_To readers choosing a system._ For an open backbone to adapt, MusicGen
is the common choice among token models and ACE-Step and Stable Audio
Open among latent models. For long-form stereo output, the Stable Audio
reports provide examples with documented training-source statements. For
training a song generator from scratch at academic scale, Muse supplies
a released song-generation pipeline for training and evaluation (Jiang
et al., 2026). The ICME challenge provides a complementary setting for
instrumental generation under fixed data and compute constraints (Hsieh
et al., 2026). For fine-tuning a song generator, ACE-Step, YuE, and
HeartMuLa are open bases. The commercial song systems are closed, and
the tables do not compare their quality. For a control added to a
trained generator, an adapter keeps a token model’s weights fixed, a
control branch or inference-time optimization suits a diffusion model,
and a plan is useful when structure over minutes must be specified or
revised. For CPU or embedded deployment with audio or gesture control,
Section 32 provides starting points and deployment tools (Mitcheltree et
al., 2025). Section 31 covers text-conditioned streaming models and
causal accompaniment to live input. The closing subsections of
Sections 6 to 9 give the evidence behind each line.

## Chapter 14 Conclusion and Future Work

This monograph has connected the musical requirements of generation,
editing, and performance to the representations, objectives, and
sampling procedures used by music audio models. The six questions
introduced at the outset provide a way to read a system report: what it
represents, how it generates, what controls it accepts, whether it plans
before rendering, how its outputs are evaluated, and what is documented
about its release.

Representation constrains sequence length, reconstruction, and the
controls a model can support. Codec tokens make audio tractable for
language modeling, while continuous latents support diffusion and
flow-based generation. Intermediate plans and sketches separate
decisions about musical structure from the production of audio detail.
Runtime depends on the generation schedule as well as representation,
model size, hardware, and implementation. Streaming systems must meet
both a throughput requirement and a response-delay requirement.

Control enters through several routes, including additional training,
adapters, inference-time optimization, inversion, and explicit plans.
These routes differ in their computational cost and in how they preserve
an existing recording (Section 30). Several song generators use a
planner-renderer design to make structure easier to specify and revise.
Single-stage systems remain important alternatives. The choice of plan
and the reliability with which a renderer follows it remain open design
questions.

Distribution-level scores, prompt-adherence measures, learned judges,
and listening studies provide different evidence (Table 7). Depending on
the method, results may depend on the representation, reference
material, evaluation protocol, or population studied. A learned judge
used during training cannot by itself provide independent evidence of
improvement. Comparisons therefore need to state both what was measured
and what conclusions the measurement supports.

Data and release conditions are equally consequential. The comparison
tables expose substantial gaps in source documentation, and a stated
license, an attribution estimate, a memorization test, and an output
detector answer different questions. The legal record in Section 12
shows why the scope and authority of each claim matter. Our position is
that progress should support musicians’ and artists’ creative agency,
meaningful consent and the ability to decline participation,
recognition, and fair compensation. Commercial adoption and technical
quality do not establish those outcomes.

The reviewed systems combine established generative methods with changes
in representations, planning, and post-training. The tables cannot
isolate the contribution of each change because systems also differ in
data, scale, and evaluation, as Section 22 states for the one class in
which the paradigms can be compared. Readers assessing new releases
should therefore examine codecs, autoencoders, conditioning mechanisms,
and training feedback as closely as the generator family.

Practical priorities follow. Full-song evaluation needs a shared
protocol that reports lyrics, vocals, arrangement, and structure
separately, with documented listener recruitment and evaluators
independent of model training. Control and editing need comparisons that
measure the requested change, preservation of the source, added
training, and per-edit cost on the backbones to which each method
applies. Releases need an auditable account of sources, permissions,
memorization measurements, and the conditions under which any
origin-verification mechanism works (Section 16). For interactive
systems, these requirements also include response latency, stable
behavior, and evaluation with musicians in use.

Broader coverage of musical traditions and languages, together with a
clearer connection between internal representations and controllable
generation, remains part of the agenda of Section 13. The comparison
framework can accommodate new systems under the rules of Section 15.

identifier, title, authors, submission and update dates, categories,
journal reference, and digital object identifier, the expressions that
matched it, and whether it was already in the bibliography. Each

reason: a dedicated sentence in the text, a grouped citation, or
exclusion. The candidates not added during the collection period were
