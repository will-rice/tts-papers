###### Abstract.

This paper addresses fine-grained prosody control for
large-language-model (LLM) based text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis of
Mandarin Chinese, motivated by multimedia content-creation
scenarios—film dubbing, audiobook production, and interactive voice
interfaces—in which prosodic adjustments must be placed on specific
characters rather than applied uniformly to whole utterances. We present
a data-driven framework in which fine-grained prosodic markers (pitch,
duration, energy, and pause) are automatically extracted from raw audio
at scale and embedded into the text stream as out-of-vocabulary Unicode
symbols. The LLM of an existing TTS system is then fine-tuned to
interpret these markers while its architecture remains unchanged,
yielding character-level temporal control that complements existing
utterance-level style control. An optional text-LLM front-end can
translate natural-language instructions into marker sequences.
Experiments on 2.15M Mandarin utterances (5,858 hours) with CosyVoice2
as the backbone demonstrate reliable control of character duration,
pitch, pause duration, and global speaking rate, with perceptual quality
essentially preserved. A cross-tokenizer analysis further indicates that
the ASR-optimized tokenizer of the backbone attenuates prosodic
variation relative to reconstruction-oriented alternatives—an
observation we report as hypothesis-generating evidence for
prosody-sensitive system design.

## 1. Introduction

The proliferation of AI-generated content (AIGC) has created strong
demand for controllable speech synthesis in multimedia production. Film
dubbing requires precise emphasis on specific words, audiobook narration
requires rhythmic variation to sustain listener engagement, and
interactive gaming and virtual-reality applications require prosodic
adjustments in response to user actions. While recent LLM-based TTS
systems (e.g., CosyVoice (Du et al., 2024), Spark-TTS (Wang et al.,
2025a), Seed-TTS (Anastassiou et al., 2024)) produce highly natural
speech with utterance-level style control, they lack _surgical_ prosodic
manipulation at the character level. Scope. This work focuses on
Mandarin Chinese: our marker inventory, alignment pipeline, and all
experiments are designed for and validated on Mandarin speech, in which
characters constitute a natural prosodic control unit. Generalization to
other languages is discussed as future work (Section 5).

Existing control mechanisms fall into two categories, each with distinct
limitations (Xie et al., 2025). Utterance-level control via
natural-language descriptions (e.g., “speak sadly”) or reference speech
provides global style transfer but cannot specify _which_ syllable
should rise in pitch or _where_ exactly to pause (Yang et al., 2024;
Leng et al., 2024). Fine-grained symbolic control via SSML,
ToBI (Silverman et al., 1992), or learned word/phoneme-level
tags (Korotkova and others, 2024; Guo and others, 2022; Mohan and
others, 2021; Vioni and others, 2021) offers local precision but
typically requires expert annotation, language-specific rules, or
modification of the acoustic model, which limits scalability and
prevents direct application to pre-trained LLM-based TTS systems.

We bridge this gap with a data-driven framework for character-level
prosody control of Mandarin TTS. Prosodic patterns are automatically
extracted from raw audio via forced alignment and statistical analysis
and embedded into text as out-of-vocabulary Unicode markers; the LLM of
an existing TTS system then learns to interpret these markers through
fine-tuning alone. We note that automatic prosody annotation from
audio–text pairs has a substantial history, from HMM-era simultaneous
modeling of spectrum, F0, and duration (Yoshimura et al., 1999) to
recent pre-trained-model-based annotation (Dai and others, 2022; Tilson
and others, 2024); our contribution is therefore _not_ automatic
extraction per se, but (i) a character-level Unicode marker design
tailored to Mandarin and (ii) the finding that a frozen-architecture
TTS-LLM can be taught to follow such markers, establishing an
interpretable, text-side control channel. An optional front-end that
translates natural-language instructions into marker sequences is
summarized in Section 3.3.

This work makes three contributions:

- •
  Automated character-level prosody markup at scale. A pipeline that
  extracts pitch, duration, energy, and pause markers from raw audio
  without manual annotation, demonstrated on 2.15M Mandarin utterances
  (5,858 hours).
- •
  Architecture-preserving marker interpretation. Evidence that the LLM
  of an existing TTS system (CosyVoice2) can be fine-tuned to realize
  discrete character-level markers as acoustic variation without any
  structural change, providing control that is complementary to
  utterance-level style conditioning.
- •
  Cross-tokenizer sensitivity analysis. Measurements suggesting that
  ASR-optimized tokenizers attenuate prosodic perturbations relative to
  reconstruction-oriented ones, reported as hypothesis-generating
  evidence for prosody-sensitive tokenizer design.

Research objective. The goal of this work is _not_ to outperform
existing systems in overall naturalness, but to demonstrate that
character-level prosodic markers can be automatically obtained and
reliably interpreted by an existing TTS-LLM. We treat _naturalness_ and
_realism_ as distinct perceptual dimensions: human speech frequently
exhibits intentional prosodic deviations (e.g., irregular lengthening in
nervous speech, deliberate pitch exaggeration in dramatic performance)
that depart from neutral naturalness yet are realistic and desirable for
expressive content. Our evaluation therefore focuses on feasibility,
controllability, and multi-dimensional perceptual quality, and the
framework should be viewed as a complement to utterance-level control
methods.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1. Utterance-Level Prosody Control

Modern LLM-based TTS systems primarily offer utterance-level control.
PromptTTS2 (Leng et al., 2024; Shimizu et al., 2024), PromptStyle (Liu
et al., 2023), and InstructTTS (Yang et al., 2024) use natural-language
descriptions for global style, which inherently lacks temporal precision
for intra-sentence variation. Reference-speech methods
(Seed-TTS (Anastassiou et al., 2024), Mega-TTS 2 (Jiang et al., 2024),
NaturalSpeech 2/3 (Shen et al., 2024; Ju et al., 2024), Style
Tokens (Wang et al., 2018), EmoCtrl-TTS (Wu et al., 2024)) transfer
acoustic style holistically. These approaches are complementary to ours:
they set global style, while our markers adjust local prosody.

### 2.2. Fine-Grained Symbolic and Markup Control

SSML and ToBI (Silverman et al., 1992) provide character- or
phoneme-level control through explicit markup, but require expert
annotation and language-specific rules. Several neural approaches reduce
this cost: Ctrl-P (Mohan and others, 2021) enables temporal control of
prosodic variation, Vioni et al. (Vioni and others, 2021) derive
phoneme-level control from prosodic clustering, Guo et al. (Guo and
others, 2022) perform unsupervised word-level prosody tagging, and
Korotkova et al. (Korotkova and others, 2024) introduce word-level text
markup trained with automatic annotation. FastSpeech 2 (Ren et al., 2021) predicts phoneme-level duration and pitch directly, but requires
modifying the acoustic model. Our work differs in scope and mechanism:
markers operate on Mandarin _characters_, are inserted as
out-of-vocabulary Unicode symbols into the text stream, and are realized
by an off-the-shelf LLM-based TTS whose architecture is left intact.

### 2.3. Automatic Prosody Annotation

Extracting prosodic structure from speech has been studied since
HMM-based synthesis jointly modeled spectrum, F0, and
duration (Yoshimura et al., 1999). More recently, Dai et al. (Dai and
others, 2022) annotated prosody automatically with a pre-trained
text–speech model, and Tilson et al. (Tilson and others, 2024) moved
toward real-time word-based prosody recognition. We build on this line
of work: our extraction pipeline is an engineering contribution aimed at
scale and Mandarin character granularity, rather than a claim that
automatic prosody extraction is itself new.

### 2.4. LLM-Based Synthesis and Inline Control Tokens

VALL-E (Chen et al., 2025) and subsequent systems (CosyVoice (Du et al.,
2024), Spark-TTS (Wang et al., 2025a), IndexTTS (Deng et al., 2025),
MsEmoTTS (Lei et al., 2022)) treat TTS as conditional language modeling
but lack fine-grained temporal control mechanisms. BatonVoice (Wang et
al., 2026) uses an external LLM as a “conductor” producing phrase-level
vocal plans, requiring a specialized TTS trained from scratch;
NVSpeech (Liao et al., 2025) inserts discrete inline tokens for
paralinguistic events (e.g., \[Laughter\]), a form of content
augmentation that expands the vocabulary and sequence. Our markers
instead modulate _existing_ lexical content at the acoustic-attribute
level, without introducing new lexical items or task-specific vocabulary
entries; the approaches are complementary.

## 3. Methodology

converts raw audio–text pairs into prosodically marked text (Figure 2),
and (2) an _acoustic interpreter_: the LLM of an existing TTS system,
fine-tuned to render the markers into acoustic tokens (Figure 1). An
optional natural-language front-end is described in Section 3.3.

### 3.1. Automated Character-Level Prosody Extraction

The pipeline (Figure 2) extracts prosodic markers via statistical
analysis of aligned audio–text pairs, replacing manual markup with
automatic, data-driven annotation.

Flowchart of the extraction pipeline: audio and text are force aligned
by two systems whose boundary estimates are fused; per-character pitch,
duration, energy, and inter-phrase pause features are then computed and
converted to Unicode markers inserted around characters.

Figure 2. Character-level prosody extraction. Forced alignment provides
temporal boundaries for feature extraction and marker
insertion.Flowchart of the extraction pipeline: audio and text are force
aligned by two systems whose boundary estimates are fused; per-character
pitch, duration, energy, and inter-phrase pause features are then
computed and converted to Unicode markers inserted around characters.

Forced alignment and segmentation. We combine MFA (McAuliffe et al., 2017) (acoustic precision) and WhisperX (Bain et al., 2023) (robustness
to spontaneous speech) to obtain frame-level character timestamps.
Samples for which the two systems disagree by more than 150 ms are
discarded. For retained samples, boundary estimates are fused by
confidence-weighted averaging:

```math
\hat{t}=\frac{w_{\text{W}}\,t_{\text{W}}+w_{\text{M}}\,t_{\text{M}}}{w_{\text{W}}+w_{\text{M}}} \tag{1}
```

where $`w_{\text{W}}`$ and $`w_{\text{M}}`$ are normalized confidence
scores derived from WhisperX’s CTC posterior probabilities (Graves et
al., 2006) and MFA’s acoustic log-likelihoods, respectively. Long
utterances are then segmented into phrases at the fused boundaries.

Prosodic feature extraction. For each phrase and character we extract:

- •
  Pause duration. Inter-phrase pauses are quantized into four levels
  (_short_, _moderate_, _long_, _very long_); the corresponding duration
  ranges are given in Table 4.
- •
  Character duration. Per-character durations are derived from alignment
  timestamps. Thresholds are applied phrase-locally: annotation is
  performed only within phrases whose duration variance exceeds a
  threshold, where characters above the upper boundary ($`t>430`$ ms)
  are labeled _lengthened_ and those within the lower band
  ($`30<t<50`$ ms) _shortened_. The boundaries are corpus-derived
  constants, corresponding to the 95th percentile (430 ms) and the
  lowest 5% band (30–50 ms) of the per-character duration distribution
  of the aligned corpus.
- •
  Character energy. Analogously, within high-variance phrases,
  characters with local maximum and minimum energy are labeled
  _stressed_ and _weak_, respectively.
- •
  Pitch (F0). F0 is estimated with RMVPE (Wei et al., 2023). Within a
  phrase, a character whose mean F0 deviates from the _phrase mean_ by
  more than $`\pm 100`$ Hz is labeled _rising_ or _falling_, provided
  that (i) the character’s _internal_ F0 range stays below 35 Hz—so the
  label reflects a clean level shift relative to the phrase rather than
  intra-character movement—and (ii) its duration exceeds 150 ms. We use
  absolute Hz thresholds because marker granularity is defined over the
  corpus distribution; relative (log-scale/semitone) thresholds are a
  natural refinement left to future work.

distribution; the resulting boundaries are reported in Section 4.1.

Pitch-extraction reliability. Because alignment quality directly
controls marker correctness, we ensemble two aligners and filter by
agreement (above). For pitch we compared PYIN (Mauch and Dixon, 2014),
CREPE (Kim et al., 2018), and RMVPE (Wei et al., 2023) and adopted RMVPE

multiple pitch trackers, analogously to the alignment stage, is a
worthwhile extension.

### 3.2. Acoustic Interpreter: Teaching a Frozen-Architecture TTS-LLM to Follow Markers

The core of the framework is the observation that the LLM inside an
existing TTS system can be fine-tuned to interpret the injected markers
as explicit, time-aligned prosodic targets, mapping the discrete
symbolic sequence to corresponding acoustic token sequences. Training
pairs are produced entirely by the automatic pipeline of Section 3.1:
the input is marked text and the target is the acoustic token sequence
of the corresponding utterance. The base architecture—tokenizer, LLM,
and decoder—is kept intact; only the input distribution changes. Prosody
conditioning therefore enters through the text stream, which makes the
approach applicable to deployed LLM-based TTS systems in principle,
although our empirical validation is limited to a single backbone
(Section 4).

### 3.3. Optional Natural-Language Front-End

For usability, markers can also be generated from free-form instructions
rather than extracted from reference audio. We construct triplets
$`\{\text{text},\text{prompt},\text{marked text}\}`$ by _inverse prosody
captioning_ with a multimodal LLM (Qwen3-Omni (Xu et al., 2025)) and
fine-tune a text LLM (Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025), LoRA, one epoch,
learning rate $`10^{-4}`$) to map descriptions to marked text. The
training set comprises approximately 210,000 instances curated from
three scenarios with distinct prosodic profiles—TV dramas (60.75%),
lectures (34.97%), and live streaming (4.29%)—spanning seven emotion
categories, and is split 7:2:1 into training, validation, and test
partitions. On the held-out test set, fine-tuning reduces the normalized
edit distance (NED; character-level Levenshtein distance (Levenshtein, 1966) normalized by reference length and averaged per sequence) between
generated and reference marked text from 7.91 (zero-shot) to 4.61, where
reference annotations are produced by the automatic pipeline of
Section 3.1. We stress that this front-end is an optional usability
layer: the contributions of this paper do not depend on it, and a full
end-to-end instruction-to-speech evaluation is left to future work.

## 4. Experiments

We instantiate the framework on CosyVoice2 (Du et al., 2025) as a
representative LLM-based TTS, chosen for its fully open-source training
and inference stack. The experiments validate (1) whether automatically
extracted markers are learned by an existing TTS-LLM, and (2) whether
the resulting conditioning enables character-level manipulation while
perceptual quality is preserved. All baselines labeled _Base_ refer to
the unmodified, publicly released CosyVoice2 model.

### 4.1. Dataset and Setup

Dataset. The Mandarin subset of VoxBox (Wang et al., 2025a) is processed
with the pipeline of Section 3.1. Utterances of 3–25 s and at most 60
characters are retained, and samples without any prosodic marker are
filtered out, yielding 2,150,447 utterances (5,858 hours). We re-split
the retained corpus 9:1 into training and validation sets, stratified by
speaker so that no speaker appears in both splits; the split seeds and
file lists will be released upon publication.

Configuration. Long/short characters are defined as $`t>430`$ ms and
$`30<t<50`$ ms. Speaking-rate levels (mean character duration) are:
_very slow_ ($`>275.22`$ ms), _slow_ ($`223.75`$–$`275.22`$ ms),
_normal_ ($`167.82`$–$`223.75`$ ms), _fast_ ($`147.4`$–$`167.82`$ ms),
and _very fast_ ($`<147.4`$ ms). Pitch labeling follows Section 3.1
($`\pm 100`$ Hz level shift, $`<35`$ Hz internal range, duration
$`>150`$ ms).

Training. The TTS-LLM is fine-tuned on 8$`\times`$B200 GPUs for 20
epochs (Adam, $`\beta=(0.9,0.999)`$, lr $`=10^{-5}`$, linear warmup with
cosine annealing). The three checkpoints with the lowest validation loss
are fused by uniform weight averaging before evaluation.

### 4.2. Character-Level Controllability

We synthesize 11 manually composed test sentences designed to cover all
marker types, with target characters manually annotated, across 12
speaker identities, using both the base model and the marker-conditioned
model. F0 and duration of the target characters are measured with the
same extraction stack used at training time. We acknowledge that reusing
the training-time extractor for evaluation could mask systematic
extraction errors; however, since base and conditioned models are scored
identically, any such bias affects both conditions equally, and the
_relative_ changes in Table 3 remain meaningful. Absolute calibration
against human annotation is left to future work.

### 4.3. Perceptual Evaluation

We conducted a listening study with 24 listeners (volunteers, all
self-reported native Mandarin speakers with normal hearing). Each
listener rated 24 stimuli (12 per system, randomly interleaved and
balanced across listeners) on 5-point scales along three dimensions:
_naturalness_, _realism_ (prosodic plausibility and similarity to the
original utterance), and _speaker similarity_. Reference recordings of
the original utterances were provided during the realism and
speaker-similarity blocks; no MOS anchor stimuli were used. The study
took approximately 20 minutes per participant.

Table 5 reports mean scores on emotionally charged content. The
marker-conditioned model tracks the base model closely on naturalness
(4.44 vs. 4.46) and scores marginally higher on realism (4.15 vs. 4.10)
and speaker similarity (4.00 vs. 3.95). All differences are within
fractions of a scale point; we report them _descriptively_ and do not
claim statistical significance, as the study was not powered or analyzed
for inferential testing. Recent methodological analyses of MOS-based
evaluation (Kirkland and others, 2023; Cooper and Yamagishi, 2023; Le
Maguer and others, 2024; Camp and others, 2023) recommend comparative
protocols (AB/CMOS, BWS) over absolute MOS for small system
differences (Wells and others, 2024); adopting such a protocol, together
with confidence intervals, is planned follow-up work.

Setup. We perturb 1,000 audio samples (volume $`\pm 5/10`$ dB, F0
$`\pm 1/3`$ semitones, additive noise at $`-45/-40`$ dB relative level,
i.e., SNR $`\approx+45/+40`$ dB) and measure the NED (normalized edit
distance (Levenshtein, 1966)) between the original and perturbed token
sequences for four tokenizers: CosyVoice2 (ASR-optimized) and the
reconstruction-oriented BiCodec (Wang et al., 2025a), DualCodec (Li et
al., 2025), and TaDiCodec (Wang et al., 2025b).

Results. CosyVoice2 exhibits the lowest NED across all perturbations,
and is particularly insensitive to energy variation—consistent with our
decision not to report loudness control. TaDiCodec nearly rewrites the
token sequence under strong perturbations (NED$`\approx`$1.0), while
DualCodec lies between the extremes. Because only one ASR-optimized
tokenizer is included and the pattern is graded rather than categorical,
we present these results as _hypothesis-generating_: they are consistent
with a trade-off in which ASR objectives favor linguistic content at the
expense of prosodic detail, but they do not by themselves demonstrate
such a mechanism.

Relation to the backbone choice. This observation does not contradict
the controllability results above: our markers inject prosodic targets
through the _text_ stream, and the fine-tuned LLM learns to realize them
despite tokenizer-level attenuation. Rather, the analysis suggests that
reconstruction-oriented tokenizers may offer larger headroom for
marker-based control—a direction we leave to future work.

## 5. Limitations and Future Work

Language and backbone scope. Validation is limited to Mandarin Chinese
and a single backbone (CosyVoice2). Character-level alignment does not
transfer directly to non-tonal or agglutinative languages, and extension
to other tokenizers and architectures—reconstruction-oriented codecs
such as EnCodec (Défossez et al., 2023) or SoundStream (Zeghidour et
al., 2022), and non-LLM architectures—remains future work.

Marker design. Thresholds are fixed constants; relative or
log-scale/semitone F0 thresholds, and ensembling of pitch trackers, may
improve robustness. Extraction quality depends on forced-alignment
accuracy and may degrade for spontaneous or atypical speech.

Evaluation scope. The perceptual study is descriptive (24 listeners, no
inferential testing) and controllability results are reported as
condition means; comparative protocols with confidence intervals and
distributional analysis are needed. Marker quality was not compared
against human annotation, and the optional instruction front-end was
evaluated only at the text level, not end-to-end. Evaluation of the
TTS-LLM uses a held-out validation partition; a fully independent test
set with disjoint recording conditions is left to future work. Strong
marker combinations can trade small amounts of neutral naturalness for
realism in expressive contexts; quantifying this trade-off with
comparative listening tests is planned.

## 6. Conclusion

We presented a data-driven framework for character-level prosody control
of Mandarin LLM-based TTS. Prosodic markers are extracted automatically
from raw audio and embedded as Unicode symbols, and an existing TTS-LLM
learns to interpret them through fine-tuning alone, providing
fine-grained temporal control complementary to utterance-level methods
without architectural changes. Experiments at the scale of 2.15M
utterances demonstrate reliable control of duration, pitch, pauses, and
speaking rate, and a cross-tokenizer analysis offers
hypothesis-generating evidence that ASR-optimized tokenizers attenuate
prosodic information. Future work will extend validation across
languages and backbones, adopt comparative perceptual protocols, and
evaluate the full instruction-to-markers-to-speech pathway end to end.

## Ethics and Privacy Statement

Fine-grained prosody control lowers the effort needed to produce
expressive synthetic speech, which could be misused for deceptive or
impersonating audio content. Our experiments use a publicly released
corpus and open-source models, and the listening study involved
volunteer raters evaluating synthetic stimuli. We advocate pairing such
control techniques with synthetic-speech disclosure and detection
mechanisms in deployed multimedia applications.
