# RSE-TTS: Compact Text-to-Speech through Shared Text-Aware Reference Encoding and Generation

###### Abstract

Can a speech generator also serve as an effective voice encoder? We show
that a pretrained autoregressive text-to-speech (TTS) backbone can be
repurposed for reference compression, improving speaker similarity in
subsequent synthesis. Jointly encoding reference speech and its
transcript further strengthens this capability. Controlled ablations and
activation interventions trace the text benefit through intermediate
acoustic representations into the compressed vectors that condition
generation. Building on these findings, we introduce RSE-TTS, whose
Reconstructive Speaker Encoder (RSE) shares the generation backbone and
compresses paired reference text and speech into eight continuous
vectors. Sharing reduces encoder–generator parameters by 48% relative to
independent pretrained encoding. Large-scale English training yields a
118M-parameter generator that improves both word error rate and speaker
similarity over the corresponding official MOSS-TTS-Nano cloning modes
on all three English benchmarks. Combined with acoustic continuation,
RSE-TTS approaches the speaker similarity of substantially larger
systems while achieving lower WER than the evaluated CosyVoice 3 and
MOSS-TTS-Local models. Audio samples are available at
[https://voxnexusai.github.io/RSE-TTS/](https://voxnexusai.github.io/RSE-TTS/).

## 1 Introduction

Scaling model capacity and training data has driven substantial advances
in text-to-speech synthesis, improving pronunciation accuracy, speaker
similarity, and expressive naturalness. These gains have been
demonstrated across a broad range of speech generation architectures (Hu
et al., 2026; Jia et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024;
Chen et al., 2024; Du et al., 2025). However, bringing these
capabilities to compact models remains challenging: a small generator
must accurately realize new linguistic content while preserving the
identity and delivery of a reference speaker. This makes effective use
of reference speech a central design consideration for compact TTS.

Acoustic continuation (prompting) provides a direct way to preserve rich
voice information by treating reference speech as part of the generation
history (Wang et al., 2023a). This access, however, couples target
synthesis to the reference utterance. Longer prompts consume more
generation context, while recording artifacts, atypical pauses, and
mismatches between reference speech and its transcript can complicate
synthesis (Zhang et al., 2025). The acoustic history therefore carries
both useful voice cues and reference-specific content and conditions
that need not be reproduced.

A reference encoder separates reference processing from target
generation by summarizing the recording into a fixed-size condition
(Zhang et al., 2025). This interface supplies explicit voice cues from
the onset of synthesis without retaining the full reference history. Its
central challenge is preserving sufficient speaker identity and
expressive detail through compression (Zhou et al., 2022). To address
this challenge, we investigate whether a pretrained TTS generator’s own
text–speech modeling capabilities can be reused to construct richer
reference representations.

We find that pretrained generation capabilities transfer to reference
compression: initializing an independent encoder from the TTS backbone
substantially improves generated speaker similarity over random
initialization. Paired reference text further improves both independent
and shared encoders. To understand this benefit, we disrupt text–speech
correspondence and intervene on internal states. Restoring intermediate
reference-audio or later query representations recovers generated
speaker similarity, linking reference understanding to voice
reconstruction.

We turn this capability into a compact system, RSE-TTS. Its
Reconstructive Speaker Encoder (RSE) jointly compresses reference text
and speech into eight vectors. Encoding and generation share one
backbone, avoiding a second Transformer and reducing their combined
parameter count by 48%. We instantiate the design in MOSS-TTS-Nano
(OpenMOSS, 2026) and train it through target speech prediction.

Training on a large-scale in-house English speech corpus yields a
118M-parameter generator that improves both intelligibility and speaker
similarity over official MOSS-TTS-Nano across three English benchmarks.
Acoustic continuation further improves similarity, bringing the compact
system closer to substantially larger models. Our contributions are:

- •
  Generative capabilities for voice compression. We demonstrate transfer
  from pretrained AR TTS to reference encoding, with paired text further
  improving speaker similarity in both independent and shared encoders.
- •
  Mechanistic understanding of text-aware reference encoding. Transcript
  perturbations and activation interventions reveal how reference text
  affects acoustic representations and compressed query states that
  support voice reconstruction.
- •
  Parameter-efficient reference encoding and synthesis. RSE-TTS shares
  the encoding and generation backbone, reducing their combined
  parameter count by 48% relative to independent encoding. Large-scale
  training demonstrates the design’s effectiveness in a compact TTS
  system, with acoustic continuation providing a further improvement in
  speaker similarity.

## 2 Related Work

Reference conditioning for zero-shot TTS. Speaker-verification encoders
transfer identity representations to TTS (Jia et al., 2018), while
MiniMax-Speech, IndexTTS2, and XTTS learn synthesis-oriented reference
conditioners (Zhang et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026; Casanova et al.,
2024). VALL-E retains reference speech as acoustic context (Wang et al.,
2023a); Koel-TTS compares speaker embeddings, decoder-context
conditioning, and a separate context encoder (Hussain et al., 2025). RSE
reuses the generation backbone in a separate text-aware encoding pass,
sharing its learned capabilities while compressing the reference into
eight vectors for target generation.

Text-aware voice and style representations. Prosody encoders transfer
acoustic expression (Skerry-Ryan et al., 2018), while global style
tokens summarize expressive variation (Wang et al., 2018).
Content-dependent speaker embeddings select reference features relevant
to target phonemes (Zhou et al., 2022), and Mega-TTS 2 models timbre and
prosody with longer and multiple references (Jiang et al., 2024).
MMCE-Qformer uses separate Q-Formers to summarize historical text and
speech, with the current synthesis text guiding acoustic-context
extraction for context-aware TTS (Xue et al., 2024). RSE jointly encodes
a reference recording and its own transcript with a pretrained TTS
backbone, using its learned text–speech relationships to construct a
target-independent voice condition. Continuous context compression has
also been explored in text language models (Chevalier et al., 2023; Ge
et al., 2024). RSE applies this idea to paired text and speech,
preserving a speaker’s voice across changes in linguistic content.

## 3 Reconstructive Speaker Encoder

### 3.1 Shared Global–Local speech model

We build on MOSS-TTS-Nano, a compact autoregressive codec TTS model with
a Global–Local transformer architecture (OpenMOSS, 2026). The Global
transformer models the sequence of text and acoustic frames using 12
layers with a hidden dimension of 768, while the single-layer Local
transformer autoregressively generates the codebook indices within each
frame. The frozen codec represents speech at 12.5 frames per second
using 16 codebooks and reconstructs audio at 48 kHz. Codec
reconstruction preserves speaker similarity well (Appendix H),
motivating our focus on reference encoding and speech generation.

Let $`(\mathbf{a}_{r},\mathbf{x}_{r})`$ denote a reference recording and
its transcript, and let $`(\mathbf{a}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t})`$ denote a
target utterance from the same speaker.¹¹ 1 Bold lowercase and uppercase
letters denote vectors and matrices, respectively. The codec maps a
recording to a sequence of frame-level codebook tuples,
$`C(\mathbf{a})=(\mathbf{c}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{T})`$. Each
$`\mathbf{c}_{i}`$ contains the 16 codec indices at frame $`i`$, and
$`E(\mathbf{c}_{i})`$ denotes its continuous input embedding. The
embeddings of the codec tokens within each frame are summed to form a
single frame-level input to Global. Let $`F_{\theta}`$ denote the shared
Global transformer and
$`\mathbf{Q}=[\mathbf{q}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{q}_{8}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 8}`$
the matrix of eight learned query embeddings, with $`d=768`$. The
reference recording yields $`R`$ frames, embedded as
$`E(\mathbf{c}^{r}_{1}),\ldots,E(\mathbf{c}^{r}_{R})`$.

### 3.2 Encoding the reference

The reference input sequence $`\mathbf{S}_{r}`$ consists, in order, of
the reference text embeddings, reference speech embeddings, an
end-of-speech marker (EOS), and the eight learned queries. Writing the
embedded sequence as columns of
$`\mathbf{S}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N_{r}}`$, where $`N_{r}`$ is its
length, we obtain hidden states
$`\mathbf{H}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N_{r}}`$ with the shared
backbone:

```math
\mathbf{H}_{r}=F_{\theta}(\mathbf{S}_{r}).
```

Each query attends to the preceding reference text and speech under the
causal mask. All eight query outputs are computed in one transformer
pass. Let $`\mathbf{h}^{r}_{q,j}`$ denote the output at query position
$`j`$. Layer normalization and a learned linear bridge produce the
compressed vectors:

```math
\mathbf{z}_{j}=B_{\phi}\bigl(\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf{h}^{r}_{q,j})\bigr),\qquad j=1,\ldots,8.
```

Here,
$`\mathbf{Z}=[\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{8}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 8}`$
stacks the compressed reference vectors as columns. We retain the
existing 192-dimensional speaker-conditioning path, using a frozen CAM++
extractor (Wang et al., 2023b). If $`\mathbf{s}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{192}`$
is the reference speaker embedding, its normalized projection is added
to each vector:

```math
\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{j}=\mathbf{z}_{j}+\mathbf{W}_{s}\frac{\mathbf{s}_{r}}{\|\mathbf{s}_{r}\|_{2}}.
```

Here, $`\mathbf{W}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 192}`$. The resulting
condition is
$`\bar{\mathbf{Z}}=[\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{8}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 8}`$.
We study the information provided by $`\mathbf{Z}`$ beyond this
auxiliary speaker condition in Section 5.

### 3.3 Reconstructing same-speaker speech

The target branch uses the same Global transformer $`F_{\theta}`$. Its
input begins with $`\bar{\mathbf{Z}}`$, followed by the complete target
text and a speech-start marker, labeled BOS in Figure 1. Target
generation receives the compressed condition rather than the full
reference history.

At each generation step, the model predicts whether to continue or stop.
When continuing, the Local transformer generates the next frame’s codec
tokens autoregressively, conditioned on the Global output. The resulting
frame is embedded and fed back to Global for the next step. Generation
ends when the model predicts EOS, and the codec decoder converts the
generated frames into speech.

### 3.4 Training objective and computational implications

We train with teacher forcing using the autoregressive cross-entropy of
the target speech, including its end-of-speech prediction. This single
objective jointly trains reference encoding and target generation:
gradients flow from the target predictions through $`\bar{\mathbf{Z}}`$
into the query readout and shared backbone. Loss weights are specified
in Appendix A.

The same-speaker pairing requires the compressed representation to
support a different utterance from the reference. We share the backbone
to reduce the model’s parameter footprint, avoiding a second full
transformer for reference encoding. Longer references increase the work
of the reference pass, while target generation always receives the same
eight-vector interface.

## 4 Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Training data and model variants

We construct same-speaker utterance pairs from an in-house English
speech corpus, selecting target utterances based on acoustic quality and
transcription accuracy. Reference utterances are sampled from the same
speaker. Complete reference and target sequences are retained. We apply
IPA augmentation to both reference and target transcripts by
probabilistically replacing unambiguous words with their phonetic
transcriptions.

We train five variants for one epoch on the same 20k-hour subset,
separating initialization, reference text, and parameter sharing. All
generators start from the same English TTS checkpoint, pretrained with a
small convolutional speaker encoder. We replace that encoder with a
Transformer matching Global:

1.  1.  Independent audio (random): a randomly initialized reference
        backbone receives speech alone and trains independently of the
        generator.
2.  2.  Independent audio (pretrained): the reference backbone copies the
        pretrained generator’s weights before training, then updates
        independently.
3.  3.  Independent text + audio: the pretrained independent encoder
        additionally receives the paired reference transcript.
4.  4.  Shared audio: the reference encoder uses the generator’s parameters
        and receives speech alone.
5.  5.  Shared text + audio (RSE): the shared backbone receives the paired
        transcript and recording.

All variants use the same data order seed, learning-rate schedule, eight
queries, auxiliary speaker condition, and target-only objective.

Beyond the controlled ablations, we train the full RSE-TTS system on a
large-scale in-house English speech corpus and compare it with existing
TTS systems. Controlled-ablation training and inference settings are
provided in Appendix A.

### 4.2 Evaluation

We evaluate on the English subsets of Seed-TTS (Anastassiou et al.,
2024), CV3-eval, and MiniMax, comprising 1,088, 500, and 100 target
utterances, respectively. Within each benchmark comparison, all systems
use the same target texts and reference recordings. References are
processed with RE-USE enhancement (Fu et al., 2026), VAD-based trimming
of leading and trailing silence, and loudness normalization. Speaker
similarity (SIM) is measured as the cosine similarity between
WavLM-based speaker embeddings of the processed reference and generated
speech (Chen et al., 2021). Both waveforms undergo the same loudness
normalization before SIM scoring. Appendix D additionally reports SIM
against the original references.

Intelligibility is measured using word error rate (WER), with
transcriptions produced by Whisper large-v3 (Radford et al., 2023). We
apply the same text normalization to the target text and ASR
transcription before computing WER. Competing models use their official
inference implementations and default sampling settings. All systems are
scored using the same ASR and speaker-verification models. Appendix A
provides the preprocessing, scoring, and inference parameters.

For reference studies, we use 30 identities from EARS (Richter et al.,
2024), with four target texts per identity. Similarity is measured
against a different real recording of the same person, rather than
against the reference used to compute $`\mathbf{Z}`$. Reference studies
isolate changes in conditioning within each identity.

## 5 From Speech Generation to Reference Encoding

### 5.1 Transferring generative capabilities to reference encoding

A pretrained TTS generator provides an effective foundation for
reference encoding. Table 1 separates the effects of pretrained
initialization, reference text, and weight sharing, showing that the
gains arise from reusing text–speech modeling capabilities rather than
weight sharing alone.

Pretraining transfers useful encoding capabilities. With the
architecture and training setup fixed, initializing the independent
reference encoder from the TTS backbone raises SIM from 0.400 to 0.530
on Seed-TTS, from 0.342 to 0.469 on CV3-eval, and from 0.391 to 0.592 on
MiniMax. Because this encoder is trained independently of the generator,
the improvement demonstrates that pretrained generative representations
can be repurposed for voice compression without requiring continued
weight sharing.

Reference text strengthens this transfer. Adding the paired transcript
improves SIM on every benchmark for both independent and shared
encoders. The benefit therefore extends beyond a particular
parameter-sharing arrangement: the pretrained backbone becomes a more
effective reference encoder when it receives both what was said and how
it was spoken. Section 6 investigates how this correspondence shapes the
representations used for voice reconstruction.

Sharing makes this capability compact. The independent text-and-audio
encoder achieves the highest SIM, while RSE retains most of this benefit
with 118.2M rather than 228.5M encoding-and-generation parameters—a 48%
reduction. We therefore adopt sharing to reuse the backbone’s
capabilities without maintaining a second Transformer. Together, these
results support text-aware encoding with a pretrained TTS backbone, with
sharing providing a parameter-efficient realization.

We next examine what the learned representation captures, using the same
frozen full RSE-TTS model throughout Sections 5.2–6.

### 5.2 Eight vectors can benefit from more speech

Can eight vectors preserve the additional voice information available in
a longer recording? We study 30 speakers from EARS (Richter et al.,
2024). For each speaker, we take progressively longer excerpts from the
start of the same recording, approximately 3, 6, 12, and 24 seconds
long. Each excerpt and its corresponding transcript are encoded into
eight vectors, which condition synthesis of the same four target texts
across all reference lengths. The auxiliary 192-dimensional speaker
embedding is always extracted from the 24-second reference and held
fixed. We compute SIM against a separate real recording of the same
speaker. Mean SIM increases from 0.378 to 0.448, 0.518, and 0.569 as the
reference grows longer (Figure 2).

Figure 2: Longer reference speech improves similarity even though target
generation always receives eight compressed vectors. Points show
measured means. The auxiliary speaker vector is fixed across durations.

Extending the reference from 3 to 12 seconds increases SIM by 0.139 (95%
CI: \[0.112, 0.165\]), with gains for 28 of 30 identities. All 30
improve at 24 seconds.

These results show that the eight-vector representation captures
additional speaker information from longer references, improving speaker
similarity without increasing the reference context used during
generation.

### 5.3 The vectors carry delivery information

Do the eight vectors also preserve how a speaker talks, beyond their
voice identity? We use parallel recordings from EARS in which the same
speaker reads the same passage in regular, fast, slow, and high-pitched
styles. Each recording is encoded with the same reference transcript,
and the resulting eight vectors condition synthesis of the same four new
sentences. The auxiliary speaker embedding is fixed to that extracted
from the speaker’s regular reading, so only the reference recording
supplied to the encoder changes.

Compared with conditioning on the regular reading, fast references
produce outputs that are 7.0% shorter, while slow references produce
outputs that are 13.4% longer. These durations include pauses.
High-pitched references raise the generated speech’s pitch by an average
of 6.01 semitones.

The eight-vector representation transfers speaking-rate and pitch
variations to new sentences even when the auxiliary speaker embedding is
fixed.

### 5.4 Complementary information beyond a conventional speaker embedding

RSE-TTS conditions generation on both the eight reference vectors and a
conventional 192-dimensional speaker embedding. Do these representations
provide complementary voice information, or does one simply duplicate
the other? We test how well each representation can predict a speaker’s
voice characteristics in a different recording.

We construct reference–target pairs for 316 speakers by extending
Seed-TTS references with same-speaker recordings from Common Voice. A
regularized linear predictor estimates the target recording’s WavLM
embedding from the conventional speaker embedding, eight reference
vectors, or their concatenation. We evaluate cosine similarity on 63
held-out speakers.

## 6 How Reference Text Improves Voice Encoding

Reference text improves speaker similarity in both pretrained encoder
designs (Table 1). We now investigate how it shapes reference-speech
representations in RSE and whether these changes reach the eight vectors
that condition generation. All experiments in this section use the
frozen full RSE-TTS model and 30 EARS speakers, with four target texts
per speaker. Reference audio, target text, auxiliary speaker embeddings,
and generation seeds are held fixed within each comparison.

### 6.1 Disrupting the reference transcript reduces speaker similarity

We first encode each reference recording twice: once with its correct
transcript and once with the same transcript tokens in reverse order.
Each encoding produces eight vectors, which we use to synthesize the
same new sentences. This changes the order of the reference text without
changing the recording or the set of text tokens.

Mean SIM falls from 0.538 with correct text to 0.497 with reversed text.
Thus, providing the same tokens is not enough: their order matters for
voice reconstruction. Reversal preserves the token inventory while
disrupting textual coherence and transcript–audio correspondence. We use
this perturbation to trace how reference text affects the
representations used for generation.

### 6.2 Locating text-dependent changes in reference encoding

Attention reveals interactions between reference speech and text. In
layer 3, several heads show clearer sequential audio-to-text attention
patterns with the correct transcript; these patterns weaken or disperse
when the transcript is reversed (Appendix G, Figure 4). These
observations motivate interventions on reference-audio states to test
their influence on the generated voice.

SIM gradients identify positions for intervention. We estimate the
sensitivity of generated speaker similarity to internal encoder states.
The eight query positions show high per-position sensitivity throughout
the encoder, while reference-audio positions remain sensitive through
intermediate layers (Appendix G). These observations motivate
interventions on both groups at layers 3, 6, and 9. We then test whether
replacing their states transfers the benefit of correct reference text
to generation.

### 6.3 Restoring internal representations recovers speaker similarity

Building on the distinction between decodable speaker information and
its causal influence on synthesis (Zheng et al., 2026), we test whether
restoring text-dependent reference states recovers the generated voice.
We replace hidden vectors between the correct- and reversed-text
encoding runs using activation patching (Zhang and Nanda, 2024). After
each replacement, the encoder continues normally, and its final eight
vectors condition ordinary autoregressive speech generation.

For example, to restore layer-3 reference-audio representations, we run
the encoder with reversed text through layer 3, then replace the hidden
vectors at reference-audio positions with those from the correct-text
run. Text and query positions are left unchanged. This tests whether
correcting the representation of the recording can recover voice
similarity even though the transcript remains reversed. We also perform
the opposite replacement: inserting reversed-text representations into
the correct-text run to test whether similarity deteriorates.

Correcting reference-audio representations restores the voice. Replacing
layer-3 audio states raises SIM from 0.497 to 0.545, recovering the loss
caused by transcript reversal. The reverse replacement lowers SIM from
0.538 to 0.504. Layer-6 audio replacements show the same direction,
while layer-9 audio replacements have little effect (Table 3). The
layer-3 restoration gain is 0.048 (95% CI: \[0.032, 0.065\]). These
results show that text-dependent changes in reference-audio
representations influence the voice subsequently generated.

Useful information is present in the later query representations.
Replacing the eight query states after layer 9 raises SIM from 0.497 to
0.542; the reverse replacement lowers it from 0.538 to 0.507. The
restoration gain is 0.045 (95% CI: \[0.031, 0.060\]). Query replacements
at earlier layers are less effective.

Random perturbations of matched magnitude do not reproduce these
restorations. The bidirectional layer-3 audio-state effect also
replicates with free-form reference recordings (Appendix E).

The interventions reveal a layer-dependent contrast. At layers 3 and 6,
restoring reference-audio states recovers speaker similarity, whereas
restoring query states has a smaller effect. At layer 9, this pattern
reverses: restoring audio states has little effect, while restoring the
eight query states recovers similarity. Together, these results show
that the benefit of reference text is expressed in intermediate acoustic
representations and subsequently captured in the compact representation
used for generation.

## 7 What Can a Compact Generator Achieve?

Compact conditioning versus continuation. A continuation-only model,
trained with the same initialization, data, and schedule but without
reference-encoder or speaker-embedding conditioning, achieves higher
SIM. RSE yields lower WER on all three benchmarks (Table 4), using
fixed-size reference conditioning instead of the full acoustic history.

acoustic continuation, it achieves lower WER than CosyVoice 3 and

speaker similarity. Its generator and waveform decoder total 129M
parameters—6.6$`\times`$ and 30.5$`\times`$ smaller than those two
systems, respectively.

## 8 Conclusion

RSE-TTS reuses a pretrained speech generator for text-aware reference
encoding. Controlled experiments establish the benefits of pretrained
initialization and paired transcripts; sharing the backbone saves 48% of
encoder–generator parameters. Activation interventions trace the text
benefit through acoustic representations into eight vectors that
preserve voice identity and delivery. The full compact system improves
WER and SIM over official MOSS-TTS-Nano and, with acoustic continuation,
approaches larger models’ similarity while achieving lower WER than the
evaluated CosyVoice 3 and MOSS-TTS-Local systems.

## 9 Limitations

Compact reference conditioning still trails acoustic continuation in
speaker similarity. Diagnostics from an earlier checkpoint show that a
similarity gap is already present near the start of generation and
persists toward the end, while sensitivity to reference conditioning
decreases as acoustic history grows (Appendix C). This pattern suggests
that teacher forcing encourages reliance on acoustic history, allowing
an initial voice mismatch to persist; information loss during
compression is another possible contributor. RSE requires a reference
transcript, making transcription robustness an important consideration
for audio-only applications. Our experiments focus on English.

## 10 Ethics and Responsible Use

Voice-cloning systems can support accessibility, localization, and
personalized speech interfaces, but can also enable impersonation.
Deployment should require appropriate consent and authorization for
reference recordings and clearly communicate the synthetic nature of
generated speech where needed. Any public release should comply with the
licenses and privacy requirements of the underlying data sources.

## Appendix A Training and Inference Details

Training loss. We average token cross-entropy separately for the native
text/control channel, which includes the continuation and EOS
predictions, and for each of the 16 codec channels. The respective
weights are 1 and 2 per codec channel. The final loss is the weighted
sum of these channel means divided by the sum of their active weights
(33 when all channels are active). Only target-branch predictions are
supervised; gradients propagate through the reference pass into the
shared backbone.

Controlled ablation. All five models use the same 20k-hour subset
(approximately 8.6M pairs) and pretrained generator checkpoint, with
fresh optimizers. The random independent reference encoder is
initialized from scratch. The pretrained independent encoders copy
Global and its input embeddings from that checkpoint before training,
then update separately; the shared encoders use the generator’s
parameters throughout. The original small speaker encoder is replaced in
every variant. Training seed is 42. The learning rate starts at
$`10^{-6}`$, reaches $`2\times 10^{-5}`$ at 0.05 epoch, stays constant
through 0.5 epoch, then cosine-decays to $`10^{-6}`$ at one epoch. All
variants finish at step 27,753.

Full RSE-TTS model. We initialize from a pretrained English TTS model
with a shared-backbone reference encoder and continue training on the
larger corpus. The reference studies and full-system comparisons use the
same frozen model.

RSE-TTS sampling. Audio sampling uses temperature 0.4, top-$`k`$ 10,
top-$`p`$ 0.8, and repetition penalty 1.2. Text/EOS sampling uses
temperature 1.0, top-$`k`$ 50, and top-$`p`$ 0.99. The final benchmark
permits at most 400 codec frames and uses no text-length guard.
Controlled ablations use the same length-guard configuration across all
variants. Mechanistic studies use a fixed paired generation seed,
2026102101, and no length guard.

Reference preprocessing versus scoring. In both benchmark comparison
groups, all models receive the same reference waveform after RE-USE
enhancement (Fu et al., 2026), VAD edge trimming with 150 ms padding
while preserving interior pauses, and speech loudness normalization
toward $`-`$23 LUFS with gain bounded to ±20 dB and a $`-`$1 dBFS peak
cap. Complete remaining references are used rather than fixed
three-second crops. Primary SIM uses the processed prompt; original,
pre-enhancement reference anchors are scored separately in Appendix D.
Both scoring waveforms are normalized to $`-`$23 LUFS without additional
VAD or enhancement. ASR uses Whisper large-v3 (Radford et al., 2023)
through faster-whisper, with int8/float16 execution, English language,
beam size five, temperature zero, no VAD, and no conditioning on
preceding transcription. Text normalization for WER does not change
synthesis inputs. All benchmark ablations use this frontend. The
reference-length, expression, and mechanism studies use the
within-speaker protocols described in Sections 5.2–6, with separate real
recordings as SIM anchors.

Aggregation. System-level WER averages per-utterance normalized errors
divided by reference word count. The ablation uses the sum of normalized
errors divided by the sum of reference words. SIM averages valid
waveform scores; empty generations contribute to WER and are excluded
from SIM. Per-system scoring counts are retained with the evaluation
records. Controlled interventions use identity-cluster bootstrap
intervals, with Holm adjustment within activation-test families.
Complete-system tables report point estimates.

Reference-study setup. Six additional EARS identities are reserved for
development choices. Paired confidence intervals use identity-level
resampling. The fast and high-pitched comparisons include 30 speakers,
and the slow comparison includes 28. Additional recordings come from
Common Voice 17.0, matched to Seed-TTS references by contributor ID.
Each of 316 speakers contributes one reference–target pair of different
utterances. Training, development, and test sets contain 190, 63, and 63
disjoint speakers, respectively. Scores measure cross-utterance
embedding prediction rather than synthesized speech.

## Appendix E Additional Reference-Study Controls

Delivery transfer. The primary pitch extractor measures a mean shift of
+6.01 semitones; a second extractor measures +4.42 semitones, with a
positive direction for all 30 identities.

Embedding probes. We freeze RSE-TTS and extract its eight reference
vectors before adding the auxiliary speaker projection. Flattening these
vectors gives 6,144 features; the conventional speaker embedding
contributes 192 features, and concatenating both gives 6,336. The
conventional embedding and the 256-dimensional WavLM prediction target
are L2-normalized. Each input block is standardized per coordinate using
training-set statistics, then divided by the square root of its
dimension; the combined predictor concatenates these normalized blocks.

Each predictor uses ridge regression with an unpenalized intercept,
minimizing mean squared embedding error plus an L2 weight penalty. We
select the penalty coefficient independently for each representation
from powers of ten between $`10^{-6}`$ and $`10^{2}`$ by development-set
mean cosine similarity. All three select $`10^{-2}`$. Predictors are
fitted on the 190 training speakers and evaluated on the 63 held-out
test speakers, without refitting on the development set. Confidence
intervals use 2,000 speaker-level bootstrap resamples.

Activation interventions. With free-form reference content, correct and
reversed transcript baselines are 0.515 and 0.478. Layer-3
reference-audio restoration raises the latter to 0.509, a gain of 0.032
(95% CI: \[0.020, 0.045\]); reverse disruption lowers the correct
condition to 0.479. Norm-matched random controls fail to reproduce the
restoration. A separately selected single-head intervention has no
reliable effect.

SIM-gradient approximation and validation. Discrete autoregressive
sampling prevents direct differentiation of the complete generation
process. We construct a local surrogate by holding sampled history fixed
and using hard-forward, soft-backward codebook expectations. This
straight-through-style surrogate (Bengio et al., 2013) yields an
approximate gradient of WavLM similarity with respect to the
eight-vector prefix.

We perturb the prefix in positive, negative, or random directions with
the same norm, equal to 1% of the original prefix norm, and then perform
fresh autoregressive generation with paired sampling seeds. Mean SIM is
0.538 without perturbation, 0.564 along the positive gradient, 0.519
along its negative, and 0.540 for a random direction. Positive minus
negative is 0.045 \[0.033, 0.058\], with the positive direction better
for 29 of 30 identity means.

Fresh generation confirms that the surrogate identifies a
similarity-sensitive direction in the compressed representation. This
oracle diagnostic uses the real speaker anchor and the same WavLM metric
for optimization and evaluation. This supports using the approximation
to identify similarity-sensitive representations; the activation
interventions in Section 6 test their role in the reference-text effect.
