# Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet

James Xu Zhao    Bryan Hooi    See-Kiong Ng Affiliation: National
University of Singapore Email: [xu.zhao@u.nus.edu](mailto:)    {dcsbhk
   seekiong}@nus.edu.sg

###### Abstract

Test-time scaling increases inference-time computation through longer
reasoning chains and has shown strong performance gains across many
domains. However, frontier models still suffer from factuality
hallucinations, raising the question of whether increased computation is
effective on closed-book knowledge-intensive tasks. In this work, we
evaluate 14 reasoning models under different test-time scaling
strategies. Our results challenge its effectiveness: increasing
test-time computation does not consistently improve accuracy and often
leads to more hallucinations. We find that changes in hallucination
rates are largely driven by the model’s willingness to answer, as longer
reasoning encourages more attempts, many of which are incorrect. We also
observe patterns consistent with confirmation bias, where extended
reasoning reinforces early incorrect beliefs with fabricated details.
Finally, we provide an information-theoretic perspective showing that
compute-only test-time scaling, as a post-processing procedure of a
fixed model, cannot introduce new information about the ground-truth
answer, explaining the limited performance gains. Overall, our findings
highlight important limitations of current test-time scaling methods for
closed-book knowledge-intensive tasks.¹¹ 1 Our code and data are
available at:
[https://github.com/XuZhao0/tts-knowledge](https://github.com/XuZhao0/tts-knowledge).

## 1 Introduction

Reasoning models, such as GPT-5, Gemini 2.5, and Qwen3, have
demonstrated impressive performance on many challenging benchmarks,
ranging from competition mathematics to complex programming tasks ([Rein
et al., 2024](#bib.bib18); [Petrov et al., 2025](#bib.bib30); [Center
for AI Safety et al., 2026](#bib.bib20); [Jain et al.,
2025](#bib.bib29); [Zaremba et al., 2025](#bib.bib32)). A key technique
behind these breakthroughs is test-time scaling, where the model is
allocated more inference-time compute to perform extensive reasoning,
such as generating longer chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning
traces ([OpenAI, 2024](#bib.bib4); [Google, 2025](#bib.bib14);
[Balachandran et al., 2025](#bib.bib2)).

Despite these advances, frontier models still suffer from
hallucinations, generating statements that contradict world
knowledge ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib23); [Augenstein et al.,
2024](#bib.bib24); [Huang et al., 2025](#bib.bib35)). This remains a
fundamental challenge because many practical applications, such as
healthcare, rely on models to provide accurate information, and
hallucinations in these settings can directly undermine reliability and
create high-stakes risks ([Dahl et al., 2024](#bib.bib22); [Kim et al.,
2025](#bib.bib21)). To study this issue, knowledge-intensive tasks are
widely used to evaluate models’ ability to recall factual information
from internal knowledge. Given that test-time scaling has shown promise
across many domains, a natural question arises:

Is test-time scaling effective for closed-book knowledge-intensive
tasks?

To answer this question, we conduct a comprehensive study of test-time
scaling on knowledge-intensive tasks that require models to rely solely
on their parametric knowledge, without access to external evidence or
tools. We evaluate 14 reasoning models under different test-time scaling
strategies. Contrary to expectations, our results, summarized in
Table [1](#S1.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
show that increasing test-time computation does not consistently improve
accuracy or reduce hallucinations for most models.²² 2 Enabling thinking
can still be beneficial compared to non-thinking mode. It is the
marginal return from additional test-time reasoning that flattens. See
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Thinking vs. Non-Thinking: Is Thinking Helpful? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet").
In some cases, such as GPT-5 mini, increasing thinking time even
increases hallucinations. Our analysis suggests that these increases are
largely driven by the model attempting more questions under extended
reasoning. We further observe that longer reasoning can induce
confirmation bias, where models generate fabricated details to support
prior beliefs, resulting in overconfident hallucinations.

To explain the empirical results, we provide an information-theoretic
perspective. We model these tasks as arbitrary facts questions, where
correct answers cannot be inferred from the prompt structure. Under this
formulation, compute-only test-time scaling is a post-processing
procedure applied to a fixed model, so it cannot introduce new
information about the ground-truth answer beyond what the model already
contains. This yields an information-theoretic upper bound on
performance in information-limited regimes, explaining the limited
accuracy gains from additional test-time compute. Together with the
empirical observation that longer reasoning can increase the answer
rate, this bound also helps explain why hallucinations may rise when the
error rate among attempted answers does not decrease sufficiently.

In summary, while test-time scaling has led to impressive performance
gains across many domains, our work shows that it is not yet effective
for knowledge-intensive tasks from both empirical and theoretical
perspectives, suggesting important limitations in this setting.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Test-Time Scaling

Test-time scaling has emerged as a promising strategy for enhancing the
capabilities of large language models. It is typically categorized into
two main paradigms ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib31)): (1) the parallel
approach, which samples multiple outputs independently and aggregates
them ([Brown et al., 2024](#bib.bib26); [Snell et al.,
2025](#bib.bib34)); and (2) the sequential approach, where the model
generates long chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning traces before producing
an answer ([Wei et al., 2022](#bib.bib27); [OpenAI, 2024](#bib.bib4);
[Muennighoff et al., 2025](#bib.bib17); [Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib28)). In this work, we focus on the sequential paradigm,
which has become the dominant test-time scaling method for improving
model performance. ³³ 3 Results and analysis for the parallel approach
are provided in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Is Parallel Sampling Helpful for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet").
It is widely adopted in frontier reasoning models ([OpenAI,
2025b](#bib.bib6); [Google, 2025](#bib.bib14); [Anthropic,
2025](#bib.bib9); [xAI, 2025](#bib.bib8); [Guo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib15); [Qwen Team, 2025](#bib.bib16)), and has demonstrated
strong performance across a range of challenging tasks, such as
competition mathematics ([Veeraboina, 2023](#bib.bib19); [Petrov et al.,
2025](#bib.bib30); [Jain et al., 2025](#bib.bib29); [Rein et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18)).

However, recent studies suggest that increasing test-time computation
does not always improve performance in some tasks ([Fu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib12); [Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib10); [Liu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib39)). [Gema et al. (2025)](#bib.bib13) identify inverse
scaling, where longer reasoning may reinforce problematic patterns
rather than improve accuracy. [Cuadron et al. (2025)](#bib.bib11) reveal
that excessive internal reasoning reduces effectiveness in agentic
tasks. [Wu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib33) find that reasoning performance
follows an inverted-U relationship with CoT length, where longer
reasoning can degrade accuracy due to factors such as error
accumulation. Our study aligns with this line of work, showing
“negative” scaling of test-time computation in knowledge-intensive
tasks.

### 2.2 Factuality Hallucinations in LLMs

Factuality hallucinations, which refer to content that contradicts world
knowledge, have been a long-standing issue in large language models ([Ji
et al., 2023](#bib.bib36); [Zhang et al., 2023](#bib.bib38); [Huang et
al., 2025](#bib.bib35); [OpenAI, 2025a](#bib.bib42)). These
hallucinations reflect the models’ limitations in absorbing knowledge
and their inability to recognize knowledge boundaries ([Bang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib37)). In high-stakes domains, such as healthcare and law,
hallucinations can introduce serious risks ([Dahl et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22); [Kim et al., 2025](#bib.bib21)). Prior studies reveal
that longer responses often lead to lower factual precision ([Wei et
al., 2024b](#bib.bib41); [Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib40)), while our
work focuses on the effect of reasoning length on hallucinations. [Kalai
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib43) argue that LLMs hallucinate because current
training and evaluation paradigms favor guessing over acknowledging
uncertainty. We provide empirical evidence for this in test-time scaling
settings and further provide an information-theoretic analysis that
formalizes an information ceiling for test-time scaling. To the best of
our knowledge, this is the first study to examine how increased
test-time computation affects factuality hallucinations.

## 3 How Does Test-Time Scaling Affect Accuracy and Hallucination Ratio?

To understand the impact of test-time scaling on knowledge-intensive
tasks, we evaluate how increasing test-time computation affects accuracy
and hallucination ratio across 14 reasoning models on three benchmarks.

### 3.1 Experimental Setup

#### Benchmarks.

We evaluate three challenging knowledge-intensive benchmarks that cover
diverse topics and measure whether models can accurately access their
internal knowledge: (1) SimpleQA ([Wei et al., 2024a](#bib.bib1)),
covering topics from science to arts; (2) FACTS Parametric ([Cheng et
al., 2025](#bib.bib46)), containing questions reflecting real-world
interests; (3) FRAMES ([Krishna et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)), requiring
internal knowledge and multi-hop reasoning.

#### Models and Test-Time Scaling Settings.

We evaluate 14 large reasoning models and group them into three
categories, based on how they support test-time scaling. Models are not
allowed to access any external knowledge sources.

- •
  Reasoning effort: Models such as GPT-5 ([OpenAI, 2025b](#bib.bib6)),
  o3-mini, o4-mini ([OpenAI, 2025d](#bib.bib5)), gpt-oss-20b ([OpenAI,
  2025c](#bib.bib7)), Grok-3 mini ([xAI, 2025](#bib.bib8)) accept a
  reasoning effort parameter that adjusts the time of thinking.
- •
  Thinking budget: Models like Gemini 2.5 ([Google, 2025](#bib.bib14)),
  Claude Sonnet 4 ([Anthropic, 2025](#bib.bib9)) provide a thinking
  budget parameter that guides the model on the number of thinking
  tokens to use. A larger number encourages the model to think for a
  longer time.
- •
  Budget forcing: For DeepSeek-R1-Distill models ([Guo et al.,
  2025](#bib.bib15)) and Qwen3 models ([Qwen Team, 2025](#bib.bib16)),
  we adopt the budget forcing method ([Muennighoff et al.,
  2025](#bib.bib17)), which extends the model’s thinking process by
  appending ‘‘Wait’’ when the model attempts to terminate its reasoning.

#### Prompts.

We use a consistent prompting format for all models, except GPT-5 mini
and Claude Sonnet 4, across all benchmarks: Give me the answer to the
following question only when you are sure of it. Otherwise, say ‘I don’t
know’. Put your answer on its own line after ‘Answer:’. ⁴⁴ 4 For GPT-5
mini and Claude Sonnet 4, this prompt leads to over 80% abstention.
Therefore, we do not explicitly instruct them to abstain when uncertain.
See
Appendix [A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 Prompt Details ‣ Appendix A Additional Experiment Details ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
for details. Results with an alternative prompt that does not instruct
abstention are in
Appendix [C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Results on Alternative Prompt ‣ Appendix C Additional Experiment Results ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet").

#### Evaluation and Metrics.

Following [Wei et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib1), we use gpt-4o-mini as a
grader to evaluate responses. For each question, the grader is provided
with both the model’s predicted answer and the reference answer, and
assigns one of three labels: “correct”, “incorrect”, or “not
attempted”. ⁵⁵ 5 We randomly sampled 300 cases and compared the
automatic evaluation results with human annotations. We found only 2
disagreements. We report two metrics: (1) Accuracy, the percentage of
all questions that were answered correctly. (2) Hallucination ratio, the
percentage of all questions that were answered incorrectly. The
remaining questions are abstentions. We additionally report F-score,
which combines accuracy and hallucinations, in
Appendix [C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Test-time Scaling Results on F-Score ‣ Appendix C Additional Experiment Results ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet").
More experimental details are provided in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Additional Experiment Details ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet").

### 3.2 Effect of Test-Time Scaling on Accuracy

#### Thinking more does not consistently improve the accuracy for most models.

As shown in
Figure [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Evaluation and Metrics. ‣ 3.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 3 How Does Test-Time Scaling Affect Accuracy and Hallucination Ratio? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
increasing the reasoning length results in minimal or no accuracy gains
across most models and all benchmarks. Higher reasoning effort leads to
much longer thinking length, but does not consistently improve accuracy.
For example, o4-mini exhibits more than 8 times increase in reasoning
tokens, but the accuracy remains almost unchanged on all tasks. On
FRAMES, GPT-5 and gpt-oss-20b show a 3-5% accuracy increase when
increasing effort from ‘low’ to ‘medium’, but no further improvement at
‘high’ effort, despite the average reasoning tokens more than doubling.
Increasing the thinking budget of Claude Sonnet 4 results in minimal
accuracy gains, less than 2% improvement, even as the average thinking
length nearly triples. Among models using budget forcing, such as
R1-Distill-Qwen-7B and Qwen3-14B, accuracy fluctuates as the thinking
length increases.

#### For Gemini 2.5, low thinking budget limits accuracy due to incomplete reasoning.

We evaluate Gemini 2.5 Flash and 2.5 Pro with thinking budget of 256,
512, 1024 and 2048 tokens. On SimpleQA, accuracy improves by over 6% for
both models, as the budget increases from 256 to 1024 tokens. However,
increasing the budget further to 2048 tokens does not bring more
improvement. On FRAMES, Gemini 2.5 achieves more accuracy gains. These
improvements arise because Gemini 2.5 is often unable to complete its
reasoning under low thinking budgets, which limits its accuracy. This
issue is particularly evident on FRAMES, which requires more reasoning
steps. We provide more analysis in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Analysis of Gemini 2.5 ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet").

### 3.3 Effect of Test-Time Scaling on Hallucination Ratio

![Refer to caption](2509.06861v3/hallucination_main_results.png)

Figure 2: Hallucination ratio with increased test-time reasoning across
14 models. For most models, longer reasoning does not reduce
hallucinations. In many cases, such as GPT-5 mini, hallucination
increases with longer thinking length.

#### Thinking more does not reduce hallucinations and may even increase them.

In
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.3 Effect of Test-Time Scaling on Hallucination Ratio ‣ 3 How Does Test-Time Scaling Affect Accuracy and Hallucination Ratio? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
for most models, increasing test-time computation fails to reduce
hallucinations and can make them worse. In OpenAI models, higher
reasoning effort often leads to more hallucinations. For example, on
SimpleQA, the hallucination ratio of GPT-5-mini increases by over 15% as
reasoning length increases from 300 to 3300 tokens. Similarly, GPT-5,
o3-mini, and gpt-oss-20b show increases of 9%, 12%, and 25%,
respectively, as reasoning length scales by over 8 times. The same trend
holds on the other tasks. Models with thinking budget settings exhibit
similar behavior. For Gemini 2.5 Flash, hallucination ratio increases by
over 5% on all benchmarks. Claude Sonnet 4 shows minimal or no reduction
in hallucinations despite longer thinking. Among models using budget
forcing, none except for DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B benefits from longer
thinking in terms of reducing hallucinations.

#### For Grok-3 mini and R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, thinking more leads to fewer hallucinations.

Increasing the reasoning effort reduces the hallucination ratio of
Grok-3 mini by 4.4%, 8.0% and 1.7% on SimpleQA, FACTS Param and FRAMES,
respectively. However, considering that the reasoning length nearly
doubles on FRAMES, the reduction is relatively small.
R1-Distill-Qwen-14B shows a more noticeable improvement. On SimpleQA,
hallucinations drop by over 12% as the average reasoning length
increases from 530 to 1580 tokens. In the other two tasks, the
hallucination ratio decreases by around 8% as reasoning length
increases. Despite these reductions, R1-Distill-Qwen-14B still has a
higher hallucination ratio than other open-source models, such as
R1-Distill-Llama-8B and Qwen3-8B.

## 4 Why Does Thinking More Sometimes Lead to Fewer or More Hallucinations?

As discussed in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Effect of Test-Time Scaling on Hallucination Ratio ‣ 3 How Does Test-Time Scaling Affect Accuracy and Hallucination Ratio? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
thinking more sometimes leads to fewer hallucinations, and sometimes
more. In this section, we examine the causes of these changes.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

As illustrated in

for each question, we obtain two responses from the same model under
different reasoning levels. We focus on cases where the hallucination
outcome changes between the two settings, that is, one response is a
hallucination while the other is not. We then examine the
non-hallucinating responses and compute the ratio of “correct” versus
“not attempted”. This helps us understand whether reduced hallucinations
are due to improved knowledge retrieval or simply abstention, and
conversely, whether increased hallucinations result from risky attempts
at answering.

As discussed in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Effect of Test-Time Scaling on Hallucination Ratio ‣ 3 How Does Test-Time Scaling Affect Accuracy and Hallucination Ratio? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
Grok-3 mini and R1-Distill-Qwen-14B show reduced hallucinations with
longer thinking. ⁶⁶ 6 A special case is Gemini 2.5 Pro on FRAMES, which
exhibits a reduction in hallucinations. We provide analysis in

For these models, we analyze cases where the model hallucinates at a
lower reasoning level but not at a higher level. In contrast, GPT-5
mini, o3-mini, gpt-oss-20b, and Gemini 2.5 Flash exhibit increased

not hallucinate at a lower reasoning level but hallucinates at a higher

### 4.2 Hallucination Changes Are Driven by the Model’s Willingness to Answer

#### Fewer hallucinations are mostly due to abstention.

As shown in
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4 Why Does Thinking More Sometimes Lead to Fewer or More Hallucinations? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
for Grok-3 mini and R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, most cases of reduced
hallucinations result from the model choosing not to answer, rather than
providing a correct answer. For example, on SimpleQA for Grok-3 mini,
93.1% of the responses that are not hallucinating at higher effort are
labeled as ‘not attempted’. Similarly, for R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, the ‘not
attempted’ ratio in these cases is 88.8%. These results suggest that in
most cases where longer reasoning reduces hallucinations, the
improvement is not due to better factual recall, but rather because the
model chooses to abstain after thinking more.

#### More hallucinations mostly come from previously unattempted questions.

In
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4 Why Does Thinking More Sometimes Lead to Fewer or More Hallucinations? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
the increase in hallucinations at higher reasoning levels is largely due
to the model attempting questions it had previously abstained from. For
example, on SimpleQA, when increasing the reasoning effort of
gpt-oss-20b, 95.0% of new hallucinations come from questions that were
‘not attempted’ at the lower effort level. Similarly, on FACTS and
FRAMES, over 85% of new hallucinations arise from questions the model
had not attempted before. This pattern also appears across other models
and benchmarks. These results suggest that longer reasoning encourages
models to attempt more questions. However, for many of these newly
attempted questions, the answers are incorrect, resulting in a higher
hallucination rate.

### 4.3 Thinking More Leads to Confirmation Bias

To better understand why longer thinking leads to more attempted
answers, and thus more hallucinations, we analyze the reasoning traces
from gpt-oss-20b.

#### Longer reasoning leads to overconfident hallucinations.

In
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ More hallucinations mostly come from previously unattempted questions. ‣ 4.2 Hallucination Changes Are Driven by the Model’s Willingness to Answer ‣ 4 Why Does Thinking More Sometimes Lead to Fewer or More Hallucinations? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
gpt-oss-20b initially engages with the question at low reasoning effort.
It explores a few possibilities but finally abstains due to uncertainty.
With higher effort, the model continues searching its memory. It begins
by expressing uncertainty, but as the reasoning progresses, it gradually
becomes more confident. The thought shifts from tentative claims like
“maybe in 2005” to more confident statements such as “I am fairly sure
it’s 2005”. Despite the increased confidence, the final answer remains
incorrect. This example suggests that extended reasoning can inflate the
model’s confidence, leading to overconfident hallucinations where the
model chooses to answer despite still lacking accurate information.

#### Longer reasoning induces confirmation bias, resulting in overconfident hallucinations.

We observe signs of confirmation bias ([Nickerson, 1998](#bib.bib25)),
where the model recalls or even makes up information to reinforce its
initial belief. In
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ More hallucinations mostly come from previously unattempted questions. ‣ 4.2 Hallucination Changes Are Driven by the Model’s Willingness to Answer ‣ 4 Why Does Thinking More Sometimes Lead to Fewer or More Hallucinations? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
after tentatively proposing an answer, e.g., “He has been recognized by
AAAI maybe in 2005”, gpt-oss-20b attempts to justify this hypothesis by
generating fabricated supporting details, such as “We can check his CV…”
and “I know of the AAAI Fellows list on the AAAI website”. These
fabricated details build upon one another, reinforcing the model’s
confidence in its incorrect belief and ultimately leading to
overconfident hallucinations.

#### Hallucinated answers appear earlier and more frequently with extended reasoning.

Inspired by [Chen et al. (2025)](#bib.bib44), we locate the positions of
the incorrect final answers in the reasoning traces. We find that as
test-time computation increases, hallucinated answers tend to appear
earlier during reasoning. Specifically, on SimpleQA, the normalized
position of the first appearance drops from 45.7% at low effort to 17.2%
at high effort. Similarly, on FRAMES, the position decreases from 68.2%
to 26.3% at high effort. We visualize how these incorrect answers appear
repeatedly throughout the reasoning trace in
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ More hallucinations mostly come from previously unattempted questions. ‣ 4.2 Hallucination Changes Are Driven by the Model’s Willingness to Answer ‣ 4 Why Does Thinking More Sometimes Lead to Fewer or More Hallucinations? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
(right). It shows that hallucinated answers reoccur after their first
appearance, and the frequency of such recurrences increases with longer
reasoning. These recurrences are often accompanied by fabricated
details, further supporting the claim that extended reasoning induces
confirmation bias.

## 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks

Our empirical results show that increasing test-time computation is not
effective for knowledge-intensive tasks. We now provide a theoretical
account for this limitation. We model long-tail factual queries using an
arbitrary fact abstraction and formalize compute-only test-time scaling
as post-processing by a fixed trained model.

### 5.1 Arbitrary Facts as an Abstraction of Long-Tail Knowledge

We consider a family of “knowledge” problems where each question
requires recalling a fact that is not inferable from general patterns
(e.g., a specific person’s birthday, a rare association).
Following [Kalai et al. (2025)](#bib.bib43), we model such settings as
arbitrary facts:

###### Definition 1 (Arbitrary Facts).

Let $`c\in\mathcal{C}`$ denote a prompt. Each prompt has a finite
candidate answer set $`R_{c}`$ and a correct answer $`A_{c}\in R_{c}`$.
For each prompt $`c`$, $`A_{c}`$ is drawn uniformly from $`R_{c}`$:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \Pr[A_{c}=r\mid c]=\frac{1}{|R_{c}|}\quad\forall r\in R_{c}.
       ```                                                           |     |

The collection $`\{A_{c}\}_{c\in\mathcal{C}}`$ is independent across
prompts.

This formalizes a worst-case abstraction of long-tail knowledge, where
without prompt-specific evidence, the correct answer is statistically
indistinguishable from other candidates.
Appendix [G.1](#A7.SS1 "G.1 Non-uniform Answer Priors ‣ Appendix G Additional Modeling Assumptions and Discussions ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
discusses relaxations of this definition.

### 5.2 Compute-Only Test-Time Scaling as Post-Processing of a Fixed Model

We model compute-only test-time scaling as any algorithm that can
interact with a fixed trained model $`\hat{p}`$, without accessing any
external information sources. Here, $`\hat{p}`$ is random through
training, and “fixed” means it is not updated at test time.

###### Definition 2 (Compute-only TTS).

A compute-only test-time scaling method with budget $`k`$ is any
randomized interactive algorithm which, given a prompt $`c`$:

1.  1.  selects query strings $`q_{1},\dots,q_{k}`$ sequentially, where each
        $`q_{t}`$ may depend on $`c`$ and previous model outputs,
2.  2.  receives responses $`r_{t}\sim\hat{p}(\cdot\mid q_{t})`$,
3.  3.  outputs a final response $`\hat{R}=g(c,T_{k})`$ as a function of the
        transcript $`T_{k}:=\{(q_{t},r_{t})\}_{t=1}^{k}`$.

This subsumes common test-time scaling strategies (i) sequential
sampling approaches, such as long chain-of-thought, and (ii) parallel
sampling approaches, such as self-consistency.

### 5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS

Let $`A:=A_{c}`$ denote the latent correct answer for prompt $`c`$.
Conditioning on $`c`$, a compute-only TTS method interacts with the
fixed model $`\hat{p}`$ to produce a transcript $`T_{k}`$, and outputs
$`\hat{R}`$ as a function of $`(c,T_{k})`$. Under this interaction
model, the transcript is generated solely from $`\hat{p}`$, and
$`\hat{R}`$ is a post-processing of $`T_{k}`$. Formally, this implies
the conditional Markov structure

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
A\;\longrightarrow\;\hat{p}\;\longrightarrow\;T_{k}\;\longrightarrow\;\hat{R},
``` |  |

with a proof in
Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Proofs for Theorem and Corollary ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet").

###### Theorem 1 (Data-processing limitation of compute-only TTS).

For any compute-only TTS method,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
I(A;\hat{R}\mid c)\;\leq\;I(A;T_{k}\mid c)\;\leq\;I(A;\hat{p}\mid c).
``` |  |

This formalizes that compute-only test-time scaling cannot create
information about the latent truth beyond what is already present in the
trained model. Therefore, if a class of prompts is information-limited
in the sense that $`I(A;\hat{p}\mid c)`$ is small, then no compute-only
TTS procedure can make $`I(A;\hat{R}\mid c)`$ large.

A standard consequence converts the mutual-information ceiling into an
accuracy upper bound (equivalently, a lower bound on error probability)
when the answer space is large.

###### Corollary 1 (Accuracy bound via Fano’s inequality).

Assume $`A`$ is uniform over $`R_{c}`$ given $`c`$, and let
$`m:=|R_{c}|`$. For any compute-only TTS output $`\hat{R}`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr[\hat{R}\neq A\mid c]\;\geq\;1-\frac{I(A;\hat{p}\mid c)+\log 2}{\log m}.
``` |  |

Corollary [1](#Thmcorollary1 "Corollary 1 (Accuracy bound via Fano’s inequality). ‣ 5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
indicates that when $`I(A;\hat{p}\mid c)\approx 0`$ (e.g., prompts whose
answers are effectively rare), the model’s information about $`A`$ is
small and the error probability must be large.

###### Remark 1.

Under the Arbitrary Facts abstraction, answers are independent across
prompts, so training on other prompts cannot convey information about
$`A_{c}`$ beyond whether the specific fact for $`c`$ was observed during
training. In particular, if the training data contains no information
about the fact for $`c`$, then $`I(A_{c};\hat{p}\mid c)=0`$, and
Corollary [1](#Thmcorollary1 "Corollary 1 (Accuracy bound via Fano’s inequality). ‣ 5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
implies an unavoidable error rate close to random guessing when
$`|R_{c}|`$ is large.

#### Implications for hallucinations.

The information-theoretic analysis primarily explains why compute-only
test-time scaling has limited ability to improve factual accuracy. By
itself, however, it does not determine how the hallucination rate
changes. With an abstention option $`\bot`$, the overall hallucination
rate can be decomposed as
$`\Pr[\hat{R}\neq A\wedge\hat{R}\neq\bot\mid c]=\Pr[\hat{R}\neq\bot\mid c]\cdot\Pr[\hat{R}\neq A\mid c,\ \hat{R}\neq\bot]`$,
where the two terms are the answer rate and the error rate conditioned
on answering. Because
Corollary [1](#Thmcorollary1 "Corollary 1 (Accuracy bound via Fano’s inequality). ‣ 5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
bounds accuracy, with abstentions counted as errors, a high answer rate
imposes a lower bound on hallucinations
(Appendix [H.4](#A8.SS4 "H.4 Answer Rate and Hallucination Rate ‣ Appendix H Proofs for Theorem and Corollary ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")).
Thus, if longer reasoning increases the answer rate without a
corresponding increase in accuracy, hallucinations increase. Changes in
answering behavior are empirical observations and are not derived from
the theoretical framework.

### 5.4 Information-Limited vs. Compute-Limited Regimes

The information-theoretic bound in
Theorem [1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Data-processing limitation of compute-only TTS). ‣ 5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
does not imply that test-time scaling can never help. We distinguish two
regimes: (1) *compute-limited*, where the model has access to sufficient
information about $`A`$ but fails to fully extract or utilize it, so
additional test-time computation can improve performance; and (2)
*information-limited*, where the available information about $`A`$ is
weak, making increased computation alone insufficient.

#### Test-time scaling is more effective when additional computation can access or exploit relevant information.

We evaluate the same model on BrowseComp-Plus ([Chen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib47)), a benchmark requiring the retrieval of hard-to-find
and entangled information, under two settings: (1) *with a search tool*,
where additional computation can retrieve new evidence, and (2) *without
a search tool*, where the model must rely solely on its parametric
knowledge. As shown in
Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Implications for hallucinations. ‣ 5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
test-time scaling behaves differently across the two settings. With
search, increasing test-time compute improves accuracy from 41.2% to
53.0% and reduces hallucinations from 16.6% to 8.0%. In contrast,
without search, even with nearly 20 times more reasoning tokens,
accuracy remains unchanged while hallucinations increase by over 10%.
These results suggest that test-time scaling is more useful in
compute-limited regimes, where sufficient relevant information is
available to the model, and additional computation helps it extract and
use that information.

## 6 Conclusion

In this work, we systematically examine test-time scaling for reasoning
models on closed-book knowledge-intensive tasks. Across 14 models and
multiple scaling strategies, increasing test-time computation does not
consistently improve factual accuracy or reduce hallucinations. Our
analysis shows that changes in hallucinations are largely driven by the
model’s willingness to answer: some models attempt more questions and
hallucinate more, while others abstain more and hallucinate less without
improving accuracy. We also observe confirmation bias, where extended
reasoning repeatedly supports an initially incorrect answer with
fabricated details. From an information-theoretic perspective,
compute-only test-time scaling is constrained by the information already
available in a fixed model, limiting its benefits in information-limited
regimes. Together, these findings reveal important limitations of
current compute-only scaling approaches for factual knowledge tasks.

#### Implications for evaluation.

Our findings also highlight the importance of reporting the full
distribution of model outcomes. Because many benchmarks emphasize
accuracy and give no credit for abstention, models may be encouraged to
guess when uncertain ([Kalai et al., 2025](#bib.bib43)). Accuracy alone
can therefore obscure whether gains reflect genuinely improved
capability or a higher answer rate accompanied by more errors. We
recommend reporting accuracy, hallucination rate, and abstention rate
together to distinguish capability gains from changes in answering
behavior.

## Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, changes in answering behavior
under increased test-time computation are empirical observations, and we
leave their underlying causes for future work. Second, our evaluation
focuses on benchmarks requiring short-form answers. We do not cover
other response formats, such as open-ended generation. Third, although
we observe reasoning patterns consistent with confirmation bias, we do
not propose interventions to mitigate them. Understanding these
behavioral patterns and developing effective control mechanisms are
important directions for future research.

## Appendix A Additional Experiment Details

### A.1 Benchmarks

We conduct experiments on three challenging, annotator-verified, and
relatively new knowledge-intensive benchmarks that cover a broad range
of topics (e.g., science, history, arts) and answer types (e.g., person,
date, number).

- •
  SimpleQA ([Wei et al., 2024a](#bib.bib1)): A benchmark curated by
  human annotators, covering a wide range of topics, from science to TV
  shows. A question example is: “Who received the IEEE Frank Rosenblatt
  Award in 2010?”. We randomly sample 800 questions for evaluation.
- •
  FACTS parametric ([Cheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib46)): A benchmark that
  measures models’ ability to access their internal knowledge. Questions
  are verified by annotators to reflect widespread user interest. A
  question example is: “highest hockey stadium in india”. We randomly
  sample 600 questions for evaluation.
- •
  FRAMES ([Krishna et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)): Questions in FRAMES tend
  to be more complex and often require multi-hop reasoning. An example
  is: “What Pink Floyd album came out the year Pablo Picasso died?” We
  use all 824 questions for evaluation.

In
Section [5.4](#S5.SS4 "5.4 Information-Limited vs. Compute-Limited Regimes ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
we conduct experiments on BrowseComp-Plus ([Chen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib47)), a benchmark of hard-to-find browsing questions with
multiple constraints. One example is: “An article written in 2023
analyzes the allegorical theme in an animated movie originally released
in the 1980s. A person who shares the article author’s name once
received the AIA Henry Adams Medal. What is the title of the movie being
discussed in the article?”. We evaluate 200 verified questions from the
benchmark, following [Zhao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib49).

### A.2 Test-Time Scaling Settings

We evaluate 14 large reasoning models under different test-time scaling
strategies. The settings used to obtain the results in
Figure [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Evaluation and Metrics. ‣ 3.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 3 How Does Test-Time Scaling Affect Accuracy and Hallucination Ratio? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
and
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.3 Effect of Test-Time Scaling on Hallucination Ratio ‣ 3 How Does Test-Time Scaling Affect Accuracy and Hallucination Ratio? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
are as follows:

- •
  Reasoning effort: For models that support three levels of reasoning
  effort, i.e., GPT-5, GPT-5 mini, o3-mini, o4-mini, and gpt-oss-20b, we
  evaluate: low, medium, and high. For Grok-3 mini, only two levels are
  available: low and high.
- •
  Thinking budget: For Gemini 2.5 Flash and 2.5 Pro, we set thinking
  budget to 256, 512, 1024, and 2048 tokens. For Claude Sonnet 4, we set
  thinking budget to 1024, 2048, and 4096 tokens.
- •
  Budget forcing: For DeepSeek-R1-Distill models and Qwen3 models, we
  use budget forcing by appending ‘‘Wait’’ multiple times. Specifically,
  we evaluate with 0 (default), 2, 4, 8, and 12 extension times.

### A.4 Prompt Details

For all models except GPT-5 mini and Claude Sonnet 4, we use the
following prompt on all benchmarks:

> Give me the answer to the following question only when you are sure of
> it. Otherwise, say ‘I don’t know’. Put your answer on its own line
> after ‘Answer:’.

However, this prompt causes over-refusal for GPT-5 mini and Claude
Sonnet 4, where the model refuses to answer in over 80% of questions.
This behavior is also reported by  [OpenAI (2025a)](#bib.bib42).

Therefore, for GPT-5 mini, we use the following prompt:

> Give me the answer to the following question. Put your answer on its
> own line after ‘Answer:’.

For Claude Sonnet 4, we adopt the prompting approach from [Gema et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib13):

> Give me the answer to the following question. You have a thinking
> token budget of about \<thinking_budget\> tokens. YOU MUST USE ALL OF
> YOUR THINKING TOKENS. Put your answer on its own line after ‘Answer:’.

For the non-thinking mode of Claude Sonnet 4
(Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Thinking vs. Non-Thinking: Is Thinking Helpful? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")),
we use the following prompt:

> Give me the answer to the following question. Put your answer on its
> own line after ‘Answer:’.

### A.5 Details of Experimental Results

We compare model behavior across different thinking levels
(Section [4](#S4 "4 Why Does Thinking More Sometimes Lead to Fewer or More Hallucinations? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"))

## Appendix B Is Parallel Sampling Helpful for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks?

![Refer to caption](2509.06861v3/sc_results.png)

We focused on the sequential test-time scaling approach in our main
experiments and concluded that it is not yet effective for
knowledge-intensive tasks. In this section, we explore whether test-time
scaling with a parallel approach is helpful.

#### Experimental setup.

We adopt self-consistency  ([Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib45)), which
samples multiple paths and selects the final answer via majority vote.
For each sample, we use the following settings: GPT-5-mini and o3-mini
are set to ‘low’ reasoning effort. Gemini 2.5 Flash uses a thinking
budget of 1024 tokens. Qwen3-8B and R1-Distill-Qwen-14B generate natural
outputs without extension. Prompt settings follow
Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 3 How Does Test-Time Scaling Affect Accuracy and Hallucination Ratio? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet").

#### Parallel test-time scaling does not consistently improve accuracy for most models.

As shown in
Figure [6](#A2.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Appendix B Is Parallel Sampling Helpful for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
increasing the number of samples does not lead to consistent accuracy
gains for most models. For example, the reasoning tokens of o3-mini
increase by over 15 times, but accuracy remains unchanged. Similarly,
for GPT-5-mini, Qwen3-8B, and R1-Distill-Qwen-14B on FACTS Parametric,
accuracy remains unchanged despite a significant increase in reasoning
tokens. On SimpleQA, GPT-5-mini and R1-Distill-Qwen-14B improve by only
around 1% from SC@1 to SC@5, with no further gains beyond that. One

SimpleQA. On FACTS Parametric, increasing samples from SC@10 to SC@15

hallucination ratio than other models.

## Appendix C Additional Experiment Results

### C.1 Test-time Scaling Results on F-Score

![Refer to caption](2509.06861v3/fscore_main_results.png)

Figure 7: F-score with increased test-time computation across 14
reasoning models. For most models, extended reasoning does not
consistently improve performance.

Following [Wei et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib1), we report the F-score,
defined as the harmonic mean of overall accuracy and accuracy
conditioned on attempted questions. This metric captures both precision
and recall by accounting for accuracy, hallucination ratio, and
abstentions, providing a more comprehensive measure of performance.
Formally, F-score is defined as:

|     |                                    |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \text{F-score}=\frac{2c}{2c+2i+n},
       ```                                 |     | (1) |

where $`c`$ is the number of correct answers, $`i`$ is the number of
incorrect answers, and $`n`$ is the number of unanswered (abstained)
questions.

#### Increasing test-time computation does not consistently improve F-score.

As shown in
Figure [7](#A3.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ C.1 Test-time Scaling Results on F-Score ‣ Appendix C Additional Experiment Results ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
increasing test-time computation leads to little or no improvement in
F-score for most models, except Gemini 2.5. For OpenAI models and
Grok-3-mini, F-score remains unchanged as reasoning tokens increase. For
example, for gpt-oss-20b on FRAMES, the F-score improves by 5.7% when
increasing reasoning effort from “low” to “medium”, but shows no further
gains despite a threefold increase in reasoning tokens. On SimpleQA, the
F-score of GPT-5 remains around 52%, even when reasoning tokens increase
by 10 times. A similar pattern is observed for Claude Sonnet 4. For
models using budget forcing, F-score fluctuates without a consistent
upward trend.

#### For Gemini 2.5, F-score improves with thinking more.

For Gemini 2.5 Flash and Pro, F-score increases by over 5% on SimpleQA
and FRAMES as the thinking budget increases. On FACTS Parametric, the
F-score of Gemini 2.5 Flash remains unchanged, while Gemini 2.5 Pro
first improves and then plateaus. The improvement on SimpleQA and FRAMES
is mainly due to increased accuracy: under low thinking budgets, Gemini
2.5 often fails to complete its reasoning, limiting performance, as
analyzed in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Analysis of Gemini 2.5 ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet").
Although hallucinations also increase with more reasoning, the gains in
accuracy outweigh these increases, resulting in higher F-scores.

### D.1 Experimental Setup

We focus on models that natively support both thinking and non-thinking
modes for a controlled comparison. Specifically, GPT-5 supports minimal
reasoning effort, which produces very few or no reasoning tokens. For

### D.2 Thinking Can Be Helpful for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks

#### Enabling thinking increases accuracy for most models on both tasks, especially on FRAMES.

As shown in
Table [3](#A4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Appendix D Thinking vs. Non-Thinking: Is Thinking Helpful? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
enabling thinking improves accuracy for most models. GPT-5 exhibits a
21.3% accuracy improvement on SimpleQA and a substantial 50.3% gain on
FRAMES. GPT-5 mini also achieves gains across both tasks. Gemini 2.5
Flash benefits similarly, with accuracy increasing by 9.8% on SimpleQA
and 15.9% on FRAMES. Claude Sonnet 4 also exhibits improvements on both
tasks. For Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B, enabling thinking leads to higher
accuracy on FRAMES. This suggests that thinking is particularly helpful
for complex tasks that require multi-hop reasoning, such as FRAMES.

#### For most models, enabling thinking leads to reduced hallucinations.

In
Table [3](#A4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Appendix D Thinking vs. Non-Thinking: Is Thinking Helpful? ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
enabling thinking often reduces hallucinations. GPT-5 shows a 9.6%
decrease on SimpleQA. Hallucination ratio of GPT-5 mini drops by over
30% on both tasks. Qwen3-8B reduces hallucinations by 47.1% on SimpleQA
and 26.9% on FRAMES. Qwen3-14B shows similar improvements. For Claude
Sonnet 4, thinking reduces hallucinations on FRAMES but not on SimpleQA.
In contrast, Gemini 2.5 Flash produces more hallucinations with thinking
enabled. One reason is that it abstains from answering over 58% of
questions in the non-thinking mode, which lowers hallucinations by
avoiding risky attempts. A similar pattern is observed with GPT-5 on
FRAMES, where it abstains from 75% of questions under minimal thinking,
leading to fewer hallucinations.

### D.3 Explanation from an Information-Theoretic Perspective

#### Thinking helps when useful information is already encoded.

For most models except Qwen3 on SimpleQA, enabling thinking improves
accuracy, especially on FRAMES, which requires multi-hop reasoning. This
suggests that, for some questions, the model already contains useful
information about the correct answer but fails to fully utilize it under
minimal reasoning. In such cases, thinking helps extract this
information, leading to better performance. This behavior is similar to
the compute-limited regime described in
Section [5.4](#S5.SS4 "5.4 Information-Limited vs. Compute-Limited Regimes ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
where additional computation improves performance by better leveraging
existing knowledge. In contrast, for Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B on SimpleQA,
enabling thinking provides no improvement, which may indicate that the
available information is insufficient.

#### Further test-time scaling is limited by information availability.

For most models, enabling thinking improves performance over
non-thinking. However, our main experiments show diminishing returns as
test-time computation increases further. On knowledge-intensive tasks,
when the relevant facts are already encoded in the model, a modest
amount of reasoning may be sufficient to retrieve and use them. When the
required information is absent or weakly represented, additional compute
alone cannot recover it, limiting further accuracy gains.

## Appendix G Additional Modeling Assumptions and Discussions

In this section, we discuss a relaxation of arbitrary facts in
Definition [1](#Thmdefinition1 "Definition 1 (Arbitrary Facts). ‣ 5.1 Arbitrary Facts as an Abstraction of Long-Tail Knowledge ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
impact of external information sources, and benchmarks mixing factual
retrieval and structured reasoning.

### G.1 Non-uniform Answer Priors

Definition [1](#Thmdefinition1 "Definition 1 (Arbitrary Facts). ‣ 5.1 Arbitrary Facts as an Abstraction of Long-Tail Knowledge ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
assumes a uniform answer distribution. More generally, let the correct
answer follow a prompt-dependent distribution $`\pi_{c}`$ over
$`R_{c}`$, i.e., $`A\sim\pi_{c}`$. The relevant uncertainty is then the
conditional entropy
$`H(A\mid c)=-\sum_{r\in R_{c}}\pi_{c}(r)\log\pi_{c}(r)`$.

The data-processing limitation in
Theorem [1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Data-processing limitation of compute-only TTS). ‣ 5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
is unchanged. The error probability lower bound is stated in terms of
the entropy $`H(A\mid c)`$. In particular, a standard form of Fano’s
inequality ([Fano, 1961](#bib.bib51)) yields

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr[\hat{R}\neq A\mid c]\;\geq\;\frac{H(A\mid c)-I(A;\hat{p}\mid c)-\log 2}{\log\left(|R_{c}|\right)}.
``` |  |

When $`\pi_{c}`$ is highly skewed, $`H(A\mid c)`$ is small, and one can
achieve high accuracy even by guessing the most probable answer; such
instances are therefore not strongly information-limited. Our
“information-limited” discussion is most relevant when $`H(A\mid c)`$ is
large, such as near-uniform answer priors.

### G.2 Impact of External Information Sources

Theorem [1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Data-processing limitation of compute-only TTS). ‣ 5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
assumes compute-only access to $`\hat{p}`$. If a test-time scaling
method additionally queries an external oracle $`O`$ (e.g., retrieval
over a corpus, tool execution) whose responses may be correlated with
the ground-truth answer $`A`$, then the transcript is generated from
both $`(\hat{p},O)`$ and the relevant Markov chain becomes

|     |                                     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       A\to(\hat{p},O)\to T_{k}\to\hat{R}.
       ```                                  |     |

By the data processing inequality,

|     |                                                             |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       I(A;\hat{R}\mid c)\leq I\!\left(A;(\hat{p},O)\mid c\right).
       ```                                                          |     |

Since $`O`$ can introduce information about $`A`$ beyond what is
contained in $`\hat{p}`$, the ceiling $`I(A;(\hat{p},O)\mid c)`$ can be
strictly larger than $`I(A;\hat{p}\mid c)`$. Increasing test-time
compute can then improve performance by extracting and utilizing more of
the evidence provided by $`O`$, i.e., increasing $`I(A;T_{k}\mid c)`$,
up to this tool-augmented ceiling.

### G.3 Benchmarks Mix Factual Retrieval and Structured Reasoning

Knowledge-intensive benchmarks do not consist only of arbitrary-fact
questions. A single question may require both retrieving relevant
knowledge and reasoning over that knowledge. For example, a science
question may require recalling a scientific principle and then applying
it to the given problem.

We can represent this as

|     |           |     |
|-----|-----------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       A=f(c,K),
       ```        |     |

where $`c`$ is the prompt, $`K`$ denotes the relevant background
knowledge, and $`f`$ represents the reasoning needed to produce the
answer.

When the necessary information in $`K`$ is already encoded in the model,
additional test-time computation may help retrieve, combine, or apply
it. This is the compute-limited component of the question. In contrast,
when required knowledge is absent or only weakly represented in the
model, additional computation alone cannot supply that missing
information. This is the information-limited component.

Our arbitrary-facts analysis characterizes the information-limited
component. It does not rule out gains from additional computation on the
structured reasoning component. Thus, the benefit of test-time scaling
depends on which component is the main bottleneck.

### H.1 Formal Model of Compute-only Interaction

Fix a test prompt $`c`$ and a latent correct answer $`A:=A_{c}`$. Let
$`\hat{p}`$ denote the trained model, viewed as a conditional
distribution over responses given queries.

A compute-only test-time scaling algorithm interacts with $`\hat{p}`$
for $`k`$ rounds. Let $`\mathcal{H}_{t-1}:=(c,q_{<t},r_{<t})`$ denote
the history before round $`t`$. The algorithm samples a query

|     |                                              |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       q_{t}\sim Q_{t}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}),
       ```                                           |     |

for some policy $`Q_{t}`$, and receives

|     |                                    |     |
|-----|------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       r_{t}\sim\hat{p}(\cdot\mid q_{t}).
       ```                                 |     |

After $`k`$ rounds, it outputs $`\hat{R}=g(c,q_{1:k},r_{1:k},U)`$ for
some measurable function $`g`$ and internal randomness $`U`$. The
transcript is $`T_{k}:=(q_{1:k},r_{1:k})`$.

#### Allowed observations.

The test-time scaling algorithm may use sampled outputs and likelihood
information, such as token logprobs or scores of a finite set of
candidate answers, provided by the fixed model $`\hat{p}`$, which are
included in the transcript $`T_{k}`$. The key restriction is that all
observables are generated from $`\hat{p}`$ alone without accessing any
external information source.

### H.2 Proof of Theorem [1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Data-processing limitation of compute-only TTS). ‣ 5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")

###### Proof.

We condition on the fixed prompt $`c`$ throughout.

Step 1: Proof of $`I(A;\hat{R}\mid c)\leq I(A;T_{k}\mid c)`$. By
construction, $`\hat{R}`$ is a possibly randomized function of
$`(c,T_{k})`$, hence, conditioning on $`c`$, we have the Markov chain

|     |                       |     |
|-----|-----------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       A\to T_{k}\to\hat{R}.
       ```                    |     |

By the data processing inequality (DPI) ([Beaudry and Renner,
2012](#bib.bib50)),

|     |                                          |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       I(A;\hat{R}\mid c)\leq I(A;T_{k}\mid c).
       ```                                       |     |

Step 2: Proof of $`I(A;T_{k}\mid c)\leq I(A;\hat{p}\mid c)`$. It
suffices to establish the Markov chain

|     |                      |     |
|-----|----------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       A\to\hat{p}\to T_{k}
       ```                   |     |

equivalently the conditional independence
$`A\perp T_{k}\mid(c,\hat{p})`$, i.e.,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr(T_{k}=t\mid A,c,\hat{p})=\Pr(T_{k}=t\mid c,\hat{p})\quad\text{for all transcripts }t.
``` |  | (2) |

Recall that the transcript is
$`T_{k}=(q_{1},r_{1},\ldots,q_{k},r_{k})`$, and define the history
before round $`t`$ as

|     |                                       |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{H}_{t-1}:=(c,q_{<t},r_{<t}).
       ```                                    |     |

By
Definition [2](#Thmdefinition2 "Definition 2 (Compute-only TTS). ‣ 5.2 Compute-Only Test-Time Scaling as Post-Processing of a Fixed Model ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"),
at each round $`t`$ the algorithm selects a query according to some
policy

|     |                                              |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       q_{t}\sim Q_{t}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}),
       ```                                           |     |

and then receives a model response sampled from the fixed model

|     |                                    |     |
|-----|------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       r_{t}\sim\hat{p}(\cdot\mid q_{t}).
       ```                                 |     |

Fix an arbitrary realization $`t=(q_{1:k},r_{1:k})`$. By the chain rule,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Pr(T_{k}=t\mid A,c,\hat{p})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Pr\big(q_{i},r_{i}\mid A,c,\hat{p},q_{<i},r_{<i}\big)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Pr\big(q_{i},r_{i}\mid A,\hat{p},\mathcal{H}_{i-1}\big).`$ |  | (3) |

We now show that each factor
in ([3](#A8.E3 "In Proof. ‣ H.2 Proof of Theorem ‣ Appendix H Proofs for Theorem and Corollary ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"))
is independent of $`A`$ once we condition on
$`(\hat{p},\mathcal{H}_{i-1})`$.

For a fixed $`i`$, factorize:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Pr\big(q_{i},r_{i}\mid A,\hat{p},\mathcal{H}_{i-1}\big)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\Pr\big(q_{i}\mid A,\hat{p},\mathcal{H}_{i-1}\big)\;\Pr\big(r_{i}\mid A,\hat{p},\mathcal{H}_{i-1},q_{i}\big).`$ |  | (4) |

Consider the two terms:

*(i) Query selection.* Given the history $`\mathcal{H}_{i-1}`$, the
distribution of $`q_{i}`$ is specified by the algorithm’s policy
$`Q_{i}`$ and does not use $`A`$ or $`\hat{p}`$ as additional inputs.
Hence

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr\big(q_{i}\mid A,\hat{p},\mathcal{H}_{i-1}\big)=\Pr\big(q_{i}\mid\mathcal{H}_{i-1}\big)=Q_{i}\big(q_{i}\mid\mathcal{H}_{i-1}\big).
``` |  | (5) |

*(ii) Model response.* Given $`\hat{p}`$ and the chosen query $`q_{i}`$,
the response $`r_{i}`$ is drawn from $`\hat{p}(\cdot\mid q_{i})`$, and
thus is conditionally independent of $`(A,\mathcal{H}_{i-1})`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr\big(r_{i}\mid A,\hat{p},\mathcal{H}_{i-1},q_{i}\big)=\Pr\big(r_{i}\mid\hat{p},q_{i}\big)=\hat{p}\big(r_{i}\mid q_{i}\big).
``` |  | (6) |

Substituting ([5](#A8.E5 "In Proof. ‣ H.2 Proof of Theorem ‣ Appendix H Proofs for Theorem and Corollary ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"))
and ([6](#A8.E6 "In Proof. ‣ H.2 Proof of Theorem ‣ Appendix H Proofs for Theorem and Corollary ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"))
into ([4](#A8.E4 "In Proof. ‣ H.2 Proof of Theorem ‣ Appendix H Proofs for Theorem and Corollary ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"))
yields

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr\big(q_{i},r_{i}\mid A,\hat{p},\mathcal{H}_{i-1}\big)=Q_{i}\big(q_{i}\mid\mathcal{H}_{i-1}\big)\,\hat{p}\big(r_{i}\mid q_{i}\big),
``` |  | (7) |

whose right-hand side does not depend on $`A`$. Therefore,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr\big(q_{i},r_{i}\mid A,\hat{p},\mathcal{H}_{i-1}\big)=\Pr\big(q_{i},r_{i}\mid\hat{p},\mathcal{H}_{i-1}\big)\quad\text{for all }i.
``` |  |

Plugging this equality back into the product
expansion ([3](#A8.E3 "In Proof. ‣ H.2 Proof of Theorem ‣ Appendix H Proofs for Theorem and Corollary ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"))
implies

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr(T_{k}=t\mid A,c,\hat{p})=\Pr(T_{k}=t\mid c,\hat{p})\quad\text{for all }t,
``` |  |

which
proves ([2](#A8.E2 "In Proof. ‣ H.2 Proof of Theorem ‣ Appendix H Proofs for Theorem and Corollary ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"))
and hence $`A\perp T_{k}\mid(c,\hat{p})`$, i.e., the Markov chain
$`A\to\hat{p}\to T_{k}`$ (conditioning on $`c`$).

Finally, by the data processing inequality applied to
$`A\to\hat{p}\to T_{k}`$ (conditioning on $`c`$),

|     |                                          |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       I(A;T_{k}\mid c)\leq I(A;\hat{p}\mid c).
       ```                                       |     |

Consequence. Combining Steps 1 and 2 yields both inequalities and also
implies the conditional Markov chain $`A\to\hat{p}\to T_{k}\to\hat{R}`$
(conditioning on $`c`$).

∎

### H.3 Proof of Corollary [1](#Thmcorollary1 "Corollary 1 (Accuracy bound via Fano’s inequality). ‣ 5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")

###### Proof.

Condition on $`c`$ and let $`m:=|R_{c}|`$. Assume $`A`$ is uniform over
$`R_{c}`$, so $`H(A\mid c)=\log m`$. Let

|     |                                  |     |
|-----|----------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       P_{e}:=\Pr[\hat{R}\neq A\mid c],
       ```                               |     |

and define the error indicator

|     |                                 |     |
|-----|---------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       E:=\mathbf{1}\{\hat{R}\neq A\}.
       ```                              |     |

Because $`E`$ is determined by $`(A,\hat{R})`$,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle H(A\mid\hat{R},c)`$ | $`\displaystyle=H(A,E\mid\hat{R},c)`$ |  | (8) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=H(E\mid\hat{R},c)+H(A\mid E,\hat{R},c).`$ |  | (9) |

The first term satisfies

|     |                                            |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       H(E\mid\hat{R},c)\leq H(E\mid c)=h(P_{e}),
       ```                                         |     |

where $`h(\cdot)`$ is the binary entropy. When $`E=0`$, we have
$`A=\hat{R}`$, so

|     |                           |     |
|-----|---------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       H(A\mid E=0,\hat{R},c)=0.
       ```                        |     |

When $`E=1`$, the answer $`A`$ can take at most $`m`$ possible values,
including when $`\hat{R}`$ is an abstention or another output outside
$`R_{c}`$. Therefore,

|     |                                   |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       H(A\mid E=1,\hat{R},c)\leq\log m.
       ```                                |     |

It follows that

|     |                                                                   |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       H(A\mid\hat{R},c)\leq h(P_{e})+P_{e}\log m\leq\log 2+P_{e}\log m.
       ```                                                                |     |

Rearranging gives

|     |                                                   |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       P_{e}\geq\frac{H(A\mid\hat{R},c)-\log 2}{\log m}.
       ```                                                |     |

Since $`A`$ is uniform over $`R_{c}`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
I(A;\hat{R}\mid c)=H(A\mid c)-H(A\mid\hat{R},c)=\log m-H(A\mid\hat{R},c).
``` |  |

Hence,

|     |                                                       |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       P_{e}\geq 1-\frac{I(A;\hat{R}\mid c)+\log 2}{\log m}.
       ```                                                    |     |

Finally,
Theorem [1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Data-processing limitation of compute-only TTS). ‣ 5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
gives

|     |                                            |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       I(A;\hat{R}\mid c)\leq I(A;\hat{p}\mid c),
       ```                                         |     |

and therefore

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr[\hat{R}\neq A\mid c]\geq 1-\frac{I(A;\hat{p}\mid c)+\log 2}{\log m}.
``` |  |

∎

### H.4 Answer Rate and Hallucination Rate

Corollary [1](#Thmcorollary1 "Corollary 1 (Accuracy bound via Fano’s inequality). ‣ 5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
bounds $`\Pr[\hat{R}\neq A\mid c]`$, which counts abstention as an
error. The following corollary restates this result in terms of the
answer rate and the overall hallucination rate, as used in
Section [5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet").

###### Corollary 2 (Answer rate and hallucination rate).

Assume $`A`$ is uniform over $`R_{c}`$ given $`c`$, and let
$`m:=|R_{c}|`$. For any compute-only TTS output $`\hat{R}`$ with
abstention option $`\bot\notin R_{c}`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr[\hat{R}\neq A\wedge\hat{R}\neq\bot\mid c]\;\geq\;\Pr[\hat{R}\neq\bot\mid c]-\frac{I(A;\hat{p}\mid c)+\log 2}{\log m}.
``` |  |

###### Proof.

Condition on $`c`$ throughout. Since $`\bot\notin R_{c}`$, every
attempted answer is either correct or incorrect, so

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Pr[\hat{R}\neq A\wedge\hat{R}\neq\bot\mid c]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\Pr[\hat{R}\neq\bot\mid c]-\Pr[\hat{R}=A\mid c].`$ |  | (10) |

Corollary [1](#Thmcorollary1 "Corollary 1 (Accuracy bound via Fano’s inequality). ‣ 5.3 An Information-Theoretic Limitation for Compute-Only TTS ‣ 5 Information-Theoretic Limits of Test-Time Scaling on Knowledge-Intensive Tasks ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
states
$`\Pr[\hat{R}\neq A\mid c]\geq 1-\frac{I(A;\hat{p}\mid c)+\log 2}{\log m}`$,
equivalently

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr[\hat{R}=A\mid c]\;\leq\;\frac{I(A;\hat{p}\mid c)+\log 2}{\log m}.
``` |  | (11) |

Substituting ([11](#A8.E11 "In Proof. ‣ H.4 Answer Rate and Hallucination Rate ‣ Appendix H Proofs for Theorem and Corollary ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"))
into ([10](#A8.E10 "In Proof. ‣ H.4 Answer Rate and Hallucination Rate ‣ Appendix H Proofs for Theorem and Corollary ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet"))
yields the claim. ∎

Corollary [2](#Thmcorollary2 "Corollary 2 (Answer rate and hallucination rate). ‣ H.4 Answer Rate and Hallucination Rate ‣ Appendix H Proofs for Theorem and Corollary ‣ Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning Models Is Not Effective for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks Yet")
makes the tradeoff explicit. The subtracted term represents an
